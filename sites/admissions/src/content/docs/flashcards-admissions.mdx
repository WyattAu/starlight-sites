## How to Use Flashcards

**First pass:** Go through all cards without rating — just flip and read. This familiarises you with the content.

**Second pass onward:** Rate honestly. 1 = could not recall at all, 2 = recalled with difficulty, 3 = recalled correctly, 4 = instant recall. The spaced repetition algorithm shows you cards rated 1-2 more frequently.

**Active recall:** Before flipping, try to verbalise the answer from memory. Passive reading of the front does not create strong memory traces.

**Session length:** 10-15 minutes per session is optimal. Multiple short sessions beat one long session for retention.

## Intuition

**Admissions tests measure aptitude, not just knowledge:** These tests assess logical reasoning, problem-solving ability, and critical thinking under time pressure. They are designed to predict academic potential, not test memorised facts.

**Why it matters:** Performing well on admissions tests opens doors to competitive university programmes and professional schools.

**The key insight:** Time management is as important as knowledge — strategic question selection and pacing often matter more than knowing every answer.

## Common Mistakes

**Rating every flashcard as "easy" without honest self-assessment:** The spaced repetition system only works if you accurately rate your recall. Marking cards as known when you only partially remember them prevents the system from showing you cards you need to review. Be honest — the goal is learning, not inflating your confidence.

**Skipping the explanation after flipping a flashcard:** Even if you got the answer right, reading the explanation reinforces the reasoning and may reveal nuances you missed. The explanation often contains the "why" behind the answer, which is more valuable than the answer itself for exam preparation.

**Using flashcards as a substitute for active problem-solving:** Flashcards are excellent for definitions and key facts, but admissions tests require applying knowledge to unfamiliar problems. Use flashcards alongside practice problems, not instead of them.

**Reviewing too many cards in one session:** Going through 50+ cards in a single session creates fatigue and reduces retention. Research shows that 15-20 minutes of focused review with 20-30 cards is more effective than marathon sessions. Shorter, more frequent sessions leverage the spacing effect.

**Not connecting flashcard facts to broader understanding:** Memorising that "the MAT tests mathematical reasoning" is less useful than understanding why it tests reasoning — because university mathematics requires problem-solving, not formula recall. Try to connect each flashcard fact to the reasoning behind it.

**Skipping difficult cards:** If a card is genuinely hard, that is exactly the card you need to review most. The spaced repetition algorithm is designed to show you difficult cards more often. Skipping them defeats the purpose of the system.

**Over-relying on recognition instead of recall:** Flipping a card and thinking "I knew that" is recognition, not recall. True recall means you can state the answer before seeing it. If you only recognise answers after flipping, you need more practice with active recall.

**Not adapting to your learning gaps:** The spaced repetition system tracks your performance, but it cannot force you to address weak areas. If you consistently rate cards low in a particular topic, spend additional time studying that topic outside of flashcard sessions. Use flashcards as a diagnostic, not a replacement for learning.
