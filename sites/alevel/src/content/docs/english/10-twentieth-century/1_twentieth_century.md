# Twentieth-Century Literature

## Introduction

Twentieth-century literature is assessed across all major A-Level exam boards as a post-1900 or post-1945 component. The period encompasses modernism, post-war fiction, and contemporary writing, each responding to distinct historical pressures: world wars, decolonisation, technological change, and shifting social hierarchies. This section addresses the major movements, techniques, and thematic concerns required for examination.

## Modernism (c. 1900-1940)

### Context

Modernism emerged from the convergence of several forces: the trauma of the First World War, Freud's theories of the unconscious, Einstein's relativity, and the rapid urbanisation of European society. Modernist writers rejected Victorian realism in favour of formal experimentation, fragmentation, and subjective perception. The movement was significantly influenced by Imagism, the Vorticist movement, and the work of European writers such as Joyce and Kafka.

### Virginia Woolf (1882-1941)

Woolf's fiction is defined by **stream of consciousness**, a narrative technique that attempts to reproduce the continuous flow of a character's thoughts, perceptions, and memories. In _Mrs Dalloway_ (1925), the narrative moves freely between the minds of Clarissa Dalloway, Septimus Warren Smith, and other characters, creating a polyphonic texture that resists singular interpretation.

Woolf's use of stream of consciousness serves thematic purposes: it reveals the gap between social performance and inner experience. Clarissa's outward composure conceals anxiety and regret; Septimus's apparent madness contains a perception of reality that the "sane" characters cannot access. The novel's structure, moving through a single day in June, enacts Woolf's interest in how time is experienced subjectively rather than chronologically.

In _To the Lighthouse_ (1927), Woolf explores memory, loss, and the act of artistic creation. The novel's three-part structure—"The Window," "Time Passes," and "The Lighthouse"—compresses a decade into the middle section, where the Ramsay house decays in the absence of its inhabitants. Mrs Ramsay's death is reported parenthetically, a formal choice that enacts the arbitrary violence of time.

For AO2, students should analyse how Woolf's sentence structure—long, undulating sentences that modulate between perception and reflection—enacts consciousness itself.

### James Joyce (1882-1941)

Joyce's _A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man_ (1916) traces Stephen Dedalus's intellectual and artistic development through a prose style that evolves with the protagonist's consciousness. The infantile language of the opening pages ("Once upon a time and a very good time it was there was a moocow") matures into the dense, Latinate syntax of the final chapters. This technique, in which form mirrors content, is a key modernist innovation.

_Ulysses_ (1922) employs radical formal experimentation, including the "stream of consciousness" chapter ("Lestrygonians"), the catechistic chapter ("Ithaca"), and the unpunctuated monologue of Molly Bloom. Joyce's use of Homeric parallelism structures the novel's 18 episodes, though the allegory is not consistently sustained. For A-Level study, focus on "The Dead" from _Dubliners_ (1914), where Gabriel Conroy's final epiphany—"His soul swooned slowly"—demonstrates Joyce's controlled use of free indirect discourse.

### T.S. Eliot (1888-1965)

Eliot's "The Waste Land" (1922) is the definitive modernist poem. Its fragmentation, multilingual allusions, and shifting voices enact the cultural desolation it describes. The poem's method of **collage**—juxtaposing high and low culture, literary quotation and colloquial speech—creates meaning through juxtaposition rather than linear argument.

The opening lines establish the poem's central paradox: "April is the cruellest month, breeding / Lilacs out of the dead land." Spring, traditionally a symbol of renewal, becomes cruel because it forces consciousness upon those who would prefer dormancy. The poem's five-part structure moves from sterility through fragments of cultural memory to a provisional, uncertain conclusion.

Students should note that Eliot's notes, included in the original publication, are themselves part of the text's strategy: they both invite and frustrate scholarly interpretation.

## Post-War Fiction (1945-1980)

### Context

Post-war fiction responds to the Holocaust, the Cold War, nuclear anxiety, and the decline of the British Empire. The period is marked by a loss of confidence in progress and reason, expressed through dystopian fiction, psychological realism, and linguistic experimentation.

### George Orwell (1903-1950)

_1984_ (1949) is a dystopian novel that examines the relationship between language, thought, and political control. The concept of **Newspeak**—a language designed to reduce the range of expressible thought—extends to the principle of **doublethink**: the capacity to hold two contradictory beliefs simultaneously. Winston Smith's rebellion is fundamentally linguistic: his diary, his memory of the past, and his relationship with Julia all represent acts of linguistic resistance against a regime that seeks to control meaning.

The novel's ending, in which Winston "loved Big Brother," is not defeat but the annihilation of individual consciousness. For AO2, students should analyse how Orwell's plain, declarative prose style enacts the very reduction of language the novel warns against.

### William Golding (1911-1993)

_Lord of the Flies_ (1954) inverts the conventions of the adventure narrative. The stranded schoolboys initially establish democratic order, but the novel traces the rapid disintegration of civilisation into tribalism and violence. The **conch** symbolises democratic authority; its destruction marks the collapse of rational discourse.

Golding's use of **allegory** connects the novel to the biblical Fall and to the political context of the Cold War. The figure of the Lord of the Flies—the pig's head on a stick—is simultaneously a literal object and a symbol of the innate human capacity for evil. Simon's hallucinatory encounter with the head ("Fancy thinking the Beast was something you could hunt and kill!") articulates the novel's central argument: evil is not external but internal.

### Margaret Atwood (1939-)

_The Handmaid's Tale_ (1985) extends the dystopian tradition into feminist territory. The novel's regime, Gilead, enforces patriarchal control through the appropriation of women's reproductive capacity. Offred's narrative voice—intimate, digressive, unreliable—resists the regime's erasure of individual identity.

Atwood's use of **palimpsest**—the layering of biblical language over contemporary oppression—creates an ironic disjunction between the regime's rhetoric and its practice. The "Ceremony," a ritualised act of rape framed as biblical duty, demonstrates how language can be weaponised to normalise atrocity. For AO3, students should connect the novel to the religious right's political ascendancy in 1980s America.

## Contemporary Voices (1980-Present)

Contemporary literature is characterised by diversity of voice and form. Postcolonial writers—Salman Rushdie, Zadie Smith, Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie—explore identity, migration, and the legacy of empire. Experimental writers—Ali Smith, George Saunders, Jennifer Egan—push narrative form into new configurations.

For A-Level study, the key skill is connecting contemporary texts to their literary predecessors. AQA and Edexcel particularly reward students who can trace thematic or formal connections across periods.

## Key Literary Techniques

### Stream of Consciousness

A narrative method that reproduces the continuous, unedited flow of a character's mental processes. It rejects the conventional narrator's mediation in favour of direct access to consciousness. Key practitioners: Woolf, Joyce, Faulkner.

**How to analyse**: Attend to punctuation (or its absence), association rather than logic, sensory intrusion, and the gap between what the character thinks and what they say.

### Fragmentation

The deliberate disruption of narrative continuity, chronological order, or coherent form. Fragmentation reflects modernist and postmodernist scepticism about stable meaning. It appears in Eliot's "The Waste Land," Woolf's shifting perspectives, and contemporary experimental fiction.

**How to analyse**: Identify what is missing, interrupted, or juxtaposed. Consider whether fragmentation enacts thematic content (e.g., the fragmentation of identity or society).

### Unreliable Narration

A narrative voice whose account of events is biased, incomplete, or deliberately misleading. The reader must distinguish between what the narrator says and what the text implies. Examples include Stevens in Ishiguro's _The Remains of the Day_ and Offred in Atwood's _The Handmaid's Tale_.

**How to analyse**: Identify discrepancies between the narrator's account and the evidence of the text. Consider why the narrator is unreliable—psychological defence, self-deception, deliberate concealment.

### Postcolonial Themes

Postcolonial literature examines the cultural, psychological, and political legacies of colonialism. Key concepts include **hybridity** (the mixing of cultures), **mimicry** (the colonised subject's imitation of the coloniser), and **othering** (the construction of colonised peoples as fundamentally different).

**How to analyse**: Attend to how language, setting, and characterisation reflect or resist colonial power structures. Consider the politics of whose story is told and in whose language.

## Assessment Notes

- **AO1**: Demonstrate knowledge of form, structure, and language across prose, poetry, and drama.
- **AO2**: Focus on how techniques such as stream of consciousness, fragmentation, and unreliable narration shape meaning.
- **AO3**: Contextualise within the specific historical moment: modernism's response to WWI, post-war fiction's engagement with totalitarianism and nuclear anxiety, contemporary writing's postcolonial concerns.
- **AO4**: Draw connections between texts across the twentieth century and earlier periods.

## Common Mistakes

**Confusing the narrator's perspective with the author's views:** The narrator is a construct within the text, not the author. An unreliable narrator (e.g., Stevens in _The Remains of the Day_) may express views that Fitzgerald, Ishiguro, or Atwood deliberately contradict through irony, structure, or other characters. Always distinguish between narrator and author.

**Using context as a list of facts rather than integrated analysis:** Dropping in historical dates or biographical details without connecting them to the text's language and form earns minimal marks. Context should illuminate your analysis of how meaning is created, not replace it. For example, mentioning post-war disillusionment is only useful if you show how it shapes specific word choices or structural decisions.

**Writing about what the text says instead of how it says it:** Describing plot events ("Gatsby is revealed to be a criminal") is retelling, not analysis. Focus on how Fitzgerald's narrative choices — point of view, symbolism, sentence structure — create that meaning. Ask "how does the text produce this effect?" not "what happens?"
