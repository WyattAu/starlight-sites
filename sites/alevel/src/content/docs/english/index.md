## Common Pitfalls

- Memorising quotes without understanding their analytical significance
- Ignoring context when the question specifically asks for it
- Writing plot summaries instead of analytical responses
- Using generic phrases like "this creates an effect" without specifying what effect
- Failing to structure essays with clear argument development

## Essay Writing Framework

Follow this structure for effective literary essays:

1. **Introduction**: State your argument clearly, outline the texts and contexts
2. **Body Paragraph 1**: First analytical point with embedded quotations
3. **Body Paragraph 2**: Second analytical point, building on or complicating the first
4. **Body Paragraph 3**: Consider alternative interpretations or a different aspect
5. **Conclusion**: Synthesise your argument, avoid introducing new points

## Literary Terminology Quick Reference

Essential terms for A-Level English:

| Term | Definition | Example |
|------|------------|---------|
| Metaphor | Direct comparison without using 'like' or 'as' | "All the world's a stage" |
| Simile | Comparison using 'like' or 'as' | "My love is like a red, red rose" |
| Personification | Giving human qualities to non-human things | "The wind whispered" |
| Juxtaposition | Placing two contrasting elements together | Light vs dark imagery |
| Pathetic Fallacy | Using weather or landscape to reflect mood | Storm during conflict |
| Narrative Voice | The perspective from which the story is told | First person unreliable |

## Set Text Guidance

Check your exam board's specification for the exact set texts. Common set texts include:

- **Shakespeare**: Hamlet, Macbeth, Othello, The Tempest
- **19th Century Novel**: Jane Eyre, Great Expectations, The Great Gatsby
- **Post-1900**: An Inspector Calls, A Streetcar Named Desire, Never Let Me Go
- **Poetry Anthology**: Specific poems from the exam board's anthology
- **Unseen Poetry**: Unknown poems requiring timed analysis
