## A-Level History -- Tudor and Stuart England Flashcards

20 flashcards with spaced repetition. Press Space to flip, then rate your recall (1--4).

### What These Flashcards Cover

These flashcards test your knowledge of the key political, religious, and social developments in England from the Tudor dynasty through the Stuart period. You should be able to explain causes and consequences, evaluate the significance of key events, and construct historically valid arguments.

**Key areas:**

- **The Tudor Dynasty:** Henry VII's seizure of the throne at Bosworth (1485), his use of purges and patronage to secure the dynasty. Henry VIII's break with Rome (1534) driven by the desire for a male heir and the search for a new wife. Edward VI's Protestant reforms. Mary I's Catholic restoration and persecution ("Bloody Mary"). Elizabeth I's religious settlement (1559), the Spanish Armada (1588), and the Essex Rebellion (1601).
- **The English Reformation:** The Act of Supremacy (1534) made the monarch Head of the Church of England. The dissolution of the monasteries (1536-1541) transferred wealth and land. The Book of Common Prayer (1549, revised 1552) standardised worship. The religious see-saw between Protestant and Catholic monarchs created lasting divisions.
- **The Stuart Period and Civil War:** James I's divine right beliefs clashed with Parliament. Charles I's personal rule (1629-1640), Ship Money, and the Petition of Right (1628). The Long Parliament, the Grand Remonstrance, and the descent into civil war (1642). The New Model Army, the execution of Charles I (1649), and the Commonwealth under Cromwell.
- **The Interregnum and Restoration:** The Protectorate (1653-1658) under Cromwell. The dissolution of the Rump Parliament. The Restoration of Charles II (1660) and the Declaration of Breda. The political and religious compromises of the Restoration settlement.
- **The Glorious Revolution:** The exclusion crisis, James II's Catholic policies, the invitation to William of Orange (1688). The Bill of Rights (1689) and the establishment of constitutional monarchy. The Jacobite risings and their failure.

### Intuition

Think of this period as a tug-of-war between the Crown and Parliament over who holds ultimate authority. The Tudors managed this balance through personal charisma and political skill. The Stuarts tried to assert divine right and lost. The Civil War was the violent resolution of this tension, and the Glorious Revolution established that Parliament is sovereign.

Religion was never just about faith -- it was bound up with politics, identity, and international relations. A Catholic monarch was seen as a threat to English independence (aligned with Spain or France), while a Protestant monarch could align with Protestant allies.

### Key Dates to Remember

- **1485:** Henry VII wins the Battle of Bosworth, founding the Tudor dynasty.
- **1534:** Act of Supremacy makes Henry VIII Head of the Church of England.
- **1558:** Elizabeth I ascends the throne.
- **1588:** The Spanish Armada is defeated.
- **1642:** The English Civil War begins.
- **1649:** Charles I is executed; the Commonwealth begins.
- **1660:** The Restoration of Charles II.
- **1688:** The Glorious Revolution.
- **1689:** The Bill of Rights establishes constitutional monarchy.

### Common Pitfalls

1. **Oversimplifying Henry VIII's break with Rome.** It was not just about wanting a divorce. It was about securing the dynasty (male heir), seizing monastic wealth, and asserting national sovereignty. Reducing it to "he wanted to divorce Catherine" is too simplistic.
2. **Treating the Civil War as a simple good vs evil story.** Both sides had legitimate grievances and committed atrocities. The Parliamentarians were not universally "good" -- many were motivated by religious extremism and self-interest.
3. **Confusing the Interregnum with the Protectorate.** The Interregnum (1649-1660) is the entire period without a monarch. The Protectorate (1653-1658) is specifically Cromwell's rule as Lord Protector.
4. **Forgetting the role of religion in the Glorious Revolution.** It was not just a political coup. James II's Catholicism and his promotion of Catholicism in public life was the primary trigger for the revolution.
