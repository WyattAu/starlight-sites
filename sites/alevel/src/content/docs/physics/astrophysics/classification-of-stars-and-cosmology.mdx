## Classification of Stars and Cosmology

> **Info:** Board Coverage AQA Paper 2 (Astrophysics option) | Edexcel Paper 1 (P10) | OCR (A) Paper 2 | CIE P4
>

## 1. Stellar Classification

Stars are classified by their surface temperature into spectral classes **O B A F G K M** (hot to
cool, remembered by *"Oh Be A Fine Girl, Kiss Me"*).

| Class | Temperature (K) | Colour | Example |
| ----- | --------------- | ------ | ------- |
| O | 30,000–60,000 | Blue | Orion Nebula stars |
| B | 10,000–30,000 | Blue-white | Rigel |
| A | 7,500–10,000 | White | Sirius, Vega |
| F | 6,000–7,500 | Yellow-white | Canopus |
| G | 5,000–6,000 | Yellow | **The Sun** |
| K | 3,500–5,000 | Orange | Arcturus |
| M | 2,500–3,500 | Red | Betelgeuse, Proxima Centauri |

The classification is based on the **absorption lines** in the stellar spectrum: O-type stars show
ionised helium lines (hot enough to ionise helium); M-type stars show molecular bands and neutral
metals (cool enough for molecules to survive).

### The Hydrogen Balmer Series

The Balmer absorption lines (visible) are strongest in **A-class** stars. In O-type stars the
hydrogen is ionised (no bound electron to absorb); in M-type stars the electron is in the $n = 2$
level only rarely (too cold for thermal excitation). A-type stars have the right temperature for
significant $n = 2$ population — the sweet spot for Balmer absorption.

## 2. The Hertzsprung–Russell Diagram

The H–R diagram plots **absolute magnitude** (or luminosity) against **temperature** (or spectral
class), temperature decreasing left to right.

Key regions:

- **Main sequence**: stars fusing hydrogen in their cores, running from hot bright O-stars (top
  left) to cool dim M-dwarfs (bottom right). About 90% of stars.
- **Red giants and supergiants**: evolved stars that have exhausted core hydrogen; large radius,
  cool surface, high luminosity (giants) or extreme luminosity (supergiants). Top right.
- **White dwarfs**: the exposed core of a dead low-mass star; hot but small, so low luminosity
  despite high temperature. Bottom left.

The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star at absolute magnitude $M = 4.83$.

### Stefan–Boltzmann and Stellar Radii

$$
L = 4 \pi r^2 \sigma T^4
$$

comparing a star to the Sun (subscript $\odot$):

$$
\frac{L}{L_\odot} = \left(\frac{r}{r_\odot}\right)^2 \left(\frac{T}{T_\odot}\right)^4
$$

**Worked example.** Betelgeuse: $T = 3500\ \mathrm{K}$ (about $T_\odot/2$), $L \approx 10^5
L_\odot$. Find its radius.

$$
\frac{r}{r_\odot} = \sqrt{\frac{L}{L_\odot}} \cdot \left(\frac{T_\odot}{T}\right)^2 \approx \sqrt{10^5} \cdot 4 \approx 1260
$$

Betelgeuse is over 1000 solar radii — a supergiant that would engulf the orbit of Jupiter.

## 3. Supernovae, Neutron Stars and Black Holes

### Type II Supernova

A star with mass $> 8 M_\odot$ fuses progressively heavier elements (H → He → C → O → Si → Fe).
Iron-56 has the highest binding energy per nucleon, so fusion stops: the core collapses in
milliseconds, the outer layers rebound off the rigid core in a shock wave, and the star explodes
as a Type II supernova, briefly outshining its entire galaxy.

### Neutron Stars

For core masses between roughly 1.4 and 3 $M_\odot$, the collapse halts when neutron degeneracy
pressure balances gravity. The result is a **neutron star**: diameter ~20 km, mass ~1.5
$M_\odot$, density comparable to an atomic nucleus (~$10^{17}$ kg/m³). Rotation rates up to
hundreds of Hz (pulsars).

### Black Holes

For core masses $> 3 M_\odot$, not even neutron degeneracy pressure can resist gravity. The core
collapses to a **black hole**: a region where escape velocity exceeds $c$. The boundary is the
**event horizon** at the Schwarzschild radius:

$$
R_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}
$$

For $M = 10 M_\odot$: $R_s \approx 30$ km.

## 4. Standard Candles and Distance

### Type Ia Supernovae

Type Ia supernovae occur when a white dwarf in a binary system accretes matter from its companion
until it reaches the Chandrasekhar limit (~$1.4 M_\odot$) and carbon fusion ignites
explosively. Because the mass at detonation is always the same, the peak luminosity is always
approximately the same — Type Ia supernovae are **standard candles**.

The distance is found by comparing the known absolute magnitude ($M \approx -19.3$) to the
apparent magnitude $m$ via the distance modulus:

$$
m - M = 5 \log_{10}\left(\frac{d}{10\ \mathrm{pc}}\right)
$$

### Cepheid Variables

Cepheid variables pulsate with a period that correlates directly with their absolute magnitude
(the **period–luminosity relationship**). Henrietta Leavitt discovered this in 1908; Hubble used
it to measure the distance to Andromeda and prove galaxies exist beyond the Milky Way.

## 5. Hubble's Law and the Expanding Universe

Hubble's 1929 observations showed that the redshift of distant galaxies is proportional to their
distance:

$$
v = H_0 d, \qquad H_0 \approx 70\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}Mpc^{-1}}
$$

The redshift $z$ is defined as $z = \frac{\lambda_{\text{obs}} - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0}$, and for
$z \ll 1$: $v \approx cz$.

**Worked example.** A galaxy shows a spectral line at 500.2 nm where the rest wavelength is 500.0
nm. Find the recession velocity and distance.

$$
z = \frac{0.2}{500} = 4\times10^{-4}, \quad v = cz = 1.2\times10^5\ \mathrm{km/s}, \quad d = \frac{v}{H_0} \approx 1.7\ \mathrm{Mpc}
$$

## 6. The Big Bang and the CMB

If galaxies are receding, they were closer in the past. Extrapolating backwards leads to the
**Big Bang** model: the universe began from an extremely hot, dense state approximately 13.8
billion years ago.

Evidence:

- **Hubble's law** — the universal recession of distant galaxies
- **Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)** — predicted by Gamow (1948), discovered by Penzias and
  Wilson (1965), a uniform 2.7 K black-body radiation filling all space
- **Abundance of light elements** — Big Bang nucleosynthesis correctly predicts the ~75% H, ~25%
  He ratio

## 7. Dark Matter and Dark Energy

Rotation curves of galaxies remain flat at large radii instead of declining as
$v \propto r^{-1/2}$ — visible mass alone cannot explain this. The missing mass is called
**dark matter** (does not emit, absorb or reflect light; detected only gravitationally).

Type Ia supernova data from 1998 onward shows that distant supernovae are *dimmer* than expected:
the expansion of the universe is **accelerating**. The cause is called **dark energy**
($\Lambda \approx 0.7$ of the total energy density).

The composition of the universe: approximately 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter, 68% dark
energy.

## 8. Common Pitfalls

- **Magnitude sign convention**: brighter stars have *more negative* magnitudes. The distance
  modulus $m - M = 5\log_{10}(d/10)$ gives positive $m - M$ for distant (dim-looking) objects.
- **Temperature axis on H–R diagram** increases to the LEFT (cool right, hot left) — the
  counterintuitive convention causes diagram-reading errors.
- **Type Ia vs Type II**: Type Ia = white dwarf + accretion = standard candle. Type II = massive
  star core collapse = NOT a standard candle (varies too much).
- **Redshift formula only valid for $z \ll 1$**: at large $z$, relativistic cosmology replaces
  the simple $v = cz$ relation.
- The CMB temperature (2.7 K) is the **current** temperature: it has cooled from ~3000 K at
  recombination due to expansion.

| Object/Phenomenon | Key Evidence | Significance |
| ----------------- | ------------ | ------------ |
| Main sequence | H–R diagram | Hydrogen fusion, mass–luminosity relation |
| Type Ia supernova | Standard candle | Distance measurement to cosmological scales |
| Cepheid variables | Period–luminosity | Distance within and beyond the Local Group |
| CMB | Uniform 2.7 K radiation | Evidence for the Big Bang |
| Hubble's law | Redshift–distance relation | Universe is expanding |
| Dark matter | Flat rotation curves | 27% of universe, unknown nature |
