## Nuclear Power and Safety

> **Info:** Board Coverage AQA Paper 3 (Section B) | Edexcel CP6 | OCR (A) Paper 2 | CIE P4
>
The induced fission of uranium-235 releases about 200 MeV per event. A reactor is an engineering
arrangement for releasing that energy at a **controlled, steady rate** and turning it into
electricity. Every component in the core exists to solve one of three problems: keep the chain
reaction going, slow the neutrons down, or absorb the excess.

## 1. The Fission Chain Reaction

A thermal neutron absorbed by ${}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}$ produces the unstable
${}^{236}_{92}\mathrm{U}$, which splits into two unequal fragments plus two or three fresh
neutrons:

$$
{}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U} + {}^{1}_{0}n \rightarrow {}^{236}_{92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow {}^{144}_{56}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{89}_{36}\mathrm{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}n + 200\ \mathrm{MeV}
$$

Each released neutron can induce further fissions. If, on average, exactly one neutron from each
fission goes on to cause another fission, the reaction is **critical**: steady power. Fewer than
one: the reaction dies out (subcritical). More than one: it grows exponentially (supercritical) --
fine inside a bomb, catastrophic in a power station.

## 2. The Moderator

Fission neutrons emerge at about 2 MeV ("fast" neutrons). Uranium-235 fissions much more readily
when hit by **slow, thermal** neutrons (around 1-2 eV), so the neutrons must be slowed between
fissions. The moderator does this by **elastic collisions**.

- In **thermal reactors** (e.g. the AGR and PWR) the moderator is water or graphite.
- The best moderator has a low mass number: a neutron loses the largest fraction of its kinetic
  energy in a head-on collision with a particle of comparable mass. This is why
  ${}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}$ (in water) and ${}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}$ (graphite) are used, and why
  deuterium (${}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}$) is slightly worse but still used in heavy-water reactors.
- A moderator must **not** absorb neutrons appreciably.

In about 25 collisions with water a 2 MeV neutron reaches thermal energies. A fast-breeder reactor,
by contrast, has **no moderator**: it relies on fast neutrons converting
${}^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U}$ into plutonium-239 faster than the plutonium is consumed.

## 3. Control Rods and Criticality

**Control rods** (boron or cadmium) are raised and lowered into the core to absorb neutrons. Their
job is to make the reactor **critical rather than supercritical**: rod depth is adjusted so the
effective neutron multiplication factor $k$ sits at $k = 1$ at steady power.

Two facts examiners reward:

- Control rods are **moveable** and **rapidly adjustable**; the moderator is fixed; the coolant
  circulates. Confusing these three roles is the single most common marking-point loss.
- Dropping the rods fully (SCRAM) shuts the fission chain reaction down in seconds. The reactor
  still produces **decay heat** afterwards, which is why cooling must continue.

The fuel is enriched to roughly 3-4 % ${}^{235}\mathrm{U}$ -- far below weapons-grade -- precisely
so that it cannot explode like a bomb; a bomb requires near-pure ${}^{235}\mathrm{U}$ assembled
supercritical almost instantly.

## 4. The Coolant

The coolant carries the fission energy (mostly the kinetic energy of the fragments) from the core
to the boilers. Requirements: high specific heat capacity, chemically stable at high temperature,
and low neutron absorption.

- PWR: ordinary water acts as **both moderator and coolant** in one loop, kept at about
  150 atmospheres so it stays liquid at 320 °C.
- Gas-cooled reactors: carbon dioxide gas through the core.
- Liquid sodium coolant in fast reactors.

A steam generator transfers energy from the primary (radioactive) loop to a secondary loop; the
steam drives turbines, and the reactor is essentially a way of boiling water.

## 5. Safety Systems and Shielding

- **Shielding**: several metres of concrete surround the core, absorbing neutrons and gamma
  radiation. The fuel stays inside the shielding while in use; the coolant loop may be inside or
  outside depending on design.
- **Emergency cooling**: independent backup systems inject water if the primary coolant loop fails.
  Loss-of-coolant accidents (LOCA) are the design-basis concern, because decay heat continues after
  shutdown.
- **Reactor containment**: the building is designed to contain steam and any released
  radioactivity.

## 6. Radioactive Waste

Spent fuel rods contain fission products with a huge range of half-lives, plus unreprocessed
${}^{235}\mathrm{U}$ and bred ${}^{239}\mathrm{Pu}$.

- **High-level waste** (spent fuel, fission products): thermally hot and intensely radioactive.
  Stored in cooling ponds for months to years while short-lived isotopes decay, then vitrified
  (locked in glass) for long-term storage deep underground.
- **Intermediate-level waste** (reactor components, chemical sludges): sealed in concrete or resin
  in steel drums.
- **Low-level waste** (gloves, clothing, filters): compacted and buried in shallow lined
  repositories.

The activity of stored waste falls with the half-lives of its isotopes; show you can use
$A = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ with $\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}}$ to justify how long a category of
waste must be stored before handling becomes safe.

## 7. Common Pitfalls

- The moderator **slows neutrons**; control rods **absorb them**. Swapping these two roles loses
  both marks.
- "The water prevents the reactor melting" is not the moderator's job -- the coolant removes
  *thermal* energy; the moderator *slows neutrons*.
- Neutrons are slowed by **elastic collisions with nuclei**, not by "friction" with the water.
- Enrichment makes the fuel economical to use, not safe to explode: a civil reactor cannot detonate
  like a fission weapon.
- Activity after shutdown: the chain reaction stops within seconds, but **decay heat** continues
  for years.

## 8. Summary

| Component | Material | Function |
| --------- | -------- | -------- |
| Fuel | Enriched ${}^{235}\mathrm{U}$ (${O_2}$) | Sustains the chain reaction |
| Moderator | Water or graphite | Slows fast neutrons to thermal speeds by elastic collisions |
| Control rods | Boron or cadmium | Absorb neutrons; keep $k = 1$; SCRAM shuts down |
| Coolant | Water, CO₂ or liquid sodium | Transfers fission energy to the boilers |
| Shielding | Thick concrete | Absorbs neutrons and gamma radiation |
