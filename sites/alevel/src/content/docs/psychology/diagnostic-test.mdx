## How the Diagnostic Scoring Works

After completing the test, you receive a results breakdown:

- **Topic Breakdown** -- each topic shows your score, colour-coded green (strong, 80%+), amber
  (moderate, 50-79%), or red (weak, below 50%).
- **Strengths** -- topics where you scored 80% or above.
- **Needs Improvement** -- topics where you scored below 50%.
- **Recommended Study Topics** -- all topics where you did not score in the strong range.

The adaptive engine targets weak topics first, so your weakest areas are tested more thoroughly. Use
the results to prioritise which topic pages to revise.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing correlation with causation.** In psychological research, a correlation between two variables (e.g., anxiety and depression) does not prove that one causes the other. A third variable (e.g., sleep deprivation) might cause both. Always check whether the study used an experimental design (with manipulation and control) before claiming causation.

2. **Overgeneralising from key studies.** Many classic studies (Milgram, Asch, Bandura) used specific samples (e.g., male American university students) and artificial settings. Findings may not generalise to other cultures, genders, or real-world contexts. Always note the sample and setting when evaluating generalisability.

3. **Confusing reliability with validity.** A study can be reliable (consistent results) but not valid (measuring the wrong thing). For example, a questionnaire that consistently measures anxiety when you want to measure depression is reliable but not valid. Both are needed for good research.

4. **Assuming biological explanations are always "more scientific."** Biological approaches use brain scans and genetic studies, but these methods have limitations (e.g., correlations in brain imaging, heritability ≠ determinism). Humanistic and psychodynamic approaches offer insights that biological methods cannot capture.

5. **Forgetting to evaluate research.** The exam rewards evaluation, not just description. For every study, note at least one strength and one limitation. Common evaluation points: ecological validity, demand characteristics, ethical issues, and generalisability.
