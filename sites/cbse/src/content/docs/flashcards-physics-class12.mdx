## Intuition

Electromagnetism is the unification of electric and magnetic forces — moving charges create magnetic fields, and changing magnetic fields create electric fields. Think of Faraday's law as nature's reluctance to let magnetic flux change: a coil will generate whatever EMF is needed to oppose a change in flux passing through it. Optics at this level explores how light behaves as both a wave (interference, diffraction) and a particle (photoelectric effect), bridging classical and quantum physics.

## How to Use These Flashcards

**First pass:** Go through all 20 cards without rating — just flip and read. This familiarises you with the content and identifies weak areas.

**Rating system:** 1 = could not recall, 2 = recalled with difficulty, 3 = recalled correctly, 4 = instant recall. The spaced repetition algorithm shows cards rated 1-2 more frequently.

**Diagram practice:** For optics and electromagnetism cards, sketch the diagram from memory before flipping. Visual recall is essential for physics exams.

**Common Pitfalls**

- **Electrostatics**: Forgetting that the electric field inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is zero — charges redistribute on the surface to cancel any internal field.
- **Optics**: Using the thin lens formula sign convention incorrectly — always define the direction of incident light as positive and apply the Cartesian sign convention consistently.
- **Semiconductors**: Confusing n-type and p-type semiconductors — n-type has excess electrons (donor impurities), while p-type has excess holes (acceptor impurities).
- **Magnetism**: Mixing up the right-hand rule for force on a current-carrying conductor with the right-hand rule for induced EMF — they apply to different physical situations.
- **AC circuits**: Forgetting that capacitive reactance decreases with frequency (Xc = 1/ωC) while inductive reactance increases (Xl = ωL) — this affects which components dominate at different frequencies.

## Study Tips

- **Draw free body diagrams for every mechanics problem:** Identify all forces, draw them at the correct points, and label them evidently. Missing even one force (often friction or normal reaction) leads to wrong equations.
- **Practise ray diagrams from memory:** Optics questions often award marks for correct ray diagrams. Practice drawing principal rays through concave and convex lenses and mirrors until the diagrams are automatic.
- **Derive formulas, do not memorise them:** Understanding how a formula is derived (e.g., the lens formula from similar triangles) means you can reconstruct it during the exam if you forget it.
