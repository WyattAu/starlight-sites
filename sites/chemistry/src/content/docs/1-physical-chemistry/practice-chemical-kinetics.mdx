## Rate Laws and Integrated Rate Laws

<PracticeProblem client:only="solid-js" question={"For a first-order reaction, the integrated rate law is $\\ln[A] = -kt + \\ln[A]_0$. What is the half-life in terms of the rate constant $k$?&quot; options={[ '$t_{1/2} = \\ln 2 / k = 0.693/k$', '$t_{1/2} = [A]_0 / 2k$', '$t_{1/2} = 1/k[A]_0$', '$t_{1/2} = 1/k$', ]} correctAnswer={0} explanation=&quot;At half-life: $[A] = [A]_0/2$, so $\\ln([A]_0/2) = -kt_{1/2} + \\ln[A]_0$, giving $-\\ln 2 = -kt_{1/2}$, thus $t_{1/2} = \\ln 2/k$. For a first-order reaction, the half-life is independent of the initial concentration. For zero-order: $t_{1/2} = [A]_0/2k$ (depends on $[A]_0$). For second-order: $t_{1/2} = 1/k[A]_0$."} difficulty="easy"  />

<PracticeProblem client:only="solid-js" question={"A plot of $\\ln[A]$ versus time yields a straight line with slope $-0.0231\\,\\text{s}^{-1}$. What is the order of the reaction and the rate constant?&quot; options={[ 'First order; $k = 0.0231\\,\\text{s}^{-1}$', 'Zero order; $k = -0.0231\\,\\text{s}^{-1}$', 'Second order; $k = 0.0231\\,\\text{s}^{-1}$', 'First order; $k = 2.31\\,\\text{s}^{-1}$', ]} correctAnswer={0} explanation=&quot;A straight line of $\\ln[A]$ vs. time indicates a first-order reaction, since $\\ln[A] = -kt + \\ln[A]_0$ is linear with slope $-k$. Here $k = 0.0231$ s$^{-1}$ and $t_{1/2} = 0.693/0.0231 = 30.0$ s. For second-order, a plot of $1/[A]$ vs. time would be linear. For zero-order, $[A]$ vs. time is linear."} difficulty="easy"  />

<PracticeProblem client:only="solid-js" question={"For the reaction $2\\text{NO}_2 \\to \\text{NO} + \\text{O}_2$, the experimental rate law is $v = k[\\text{NO}_2]^2$. This suggests the reaction is:&quot; options={[ 'Second order overall and likely bimolecular (but the stoichiometric coefficient does not necessarily equal the order)', 'First order because the stoichiometry is 2:1:1', 'Termolecular because three molecules are involved', 'Zero order with respect to $\\text{NO}_2$', ]} correctAnswer={0} explanation=&quot;The overall order is 2 (second order in $\\text{NO}_2$). The rate law is experimentally determined and does not necessarily match the stoichiometric coefficients. A second-order rate law suggests a bimolecular rate-determining step, but the actual mechanism might involve multiple steps with intermediates."} difficulty="medium"  />

<PracticeProblem client:only="solid-js" question={"For a reaction with the experimental rate law $v = k[A]^2[B]$, if the concentration of $A$ is doubled and $B$ is tripled, by what factor does the rate increase?&quot; options={['$2^2 \\times 3 = 12$', '$2 \\times 3 = 6$', '$2 + 3 = 5$', '$2^2 + 3 = 7$']} correctAnswer={0} explanation=&quot;The rate is proportional to $[A]^2[B]$. ## Reaction Mechanisms and Catalysis

<PracticeProblem client:only="solid-js" question={"For a reaction mechanism with a slow rate-determining step followed by fast steps, what is the relationship between the rate law and the rate-determining step?&quot; options={[ 'The rate law reflects the molecularity of the rate-determining step (for an elementary step)', 'The rate law always matches the overall stoichiometry', 'The rate law has no relationship to any individual step', 'The rate law is determined by the fastest step', ]} correctAnswer={0} explanation=&quot;For an elementary (single molecular event) rate-determining step, the rate law directly reflects its molecularity. For example, if the RDS is $\\text{A} + \\text{B} \\to \\text{products}$ (bimolecular), the rate law is $v = k[A][B]$. If the RDS involves intermediates, the steady-state approximation or pre-equilibrium approximation may be needed to express the rate law in terms of reactants."} difficulty="medium"  />

<PracticeProblem client:only="solid-js" question={"The steady-state approximation assumes that the concentration of reactive intermediates remains approximately constant during the reaction. Mathematically, this means:&quot; options={[ '$d[\\text{intermediate}]/dt \\approx 0$', '$[\\text{intermediate}] = 0$ at all times', '$d[\\text{intermediate}]/dt = k[\\text{reactants}]$', '$[\\text{intermediate}] = [\\text{products}]$', ]} correctAnswer={0} explanation=&quot;The steady-state approximation sets $d[\\text{I}]/dt = 0$, meaning the rate of formation equals the rate of consumption of the intermediate. This allows expressing $[\\text{I}]$ in terms of reactant concentrations without solving the full differential equations. It is valid when the intermediate is highly reactive (consumed rapidly after formation), so its concentration remains low and nearly constant."} difficulty="medium"  />

<PracticeProblem client:only="solid-js" question={"The Arrhenius equation $k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$ relates the rate constant to temperature. <PracticeProblem client:only="solid-js" question={"In enzyme kinetics, the Michaelis-Menten equation $v = \\frac{V_{\\max}[S]}{K_M + [S]}$ gives the reaction velocity. When $k_2 \\ll k_{-1}$, $K_M \\approx K_d$ (dissociation constant). A Lineweaver-Burk plot ($1/v$ vs $1/[S]$) yields $K_M$ from the $x$-intercept."} difficulty="medium"  />

<PracticeProblem client:only="solid-js" question={"A catalyst increases the rate of a chemical reaction. Which of the following is true about the catalyzed vs. uncatalyzed reaction?&quot; options={[ 'The catalyst lowers the activation energy but does not change $\\Delta H$ or $\\Delta G^\\circ$; it provides an alternative pathway', 'The catalyst changes the equilibrium constant', 'The catalyst increases the activation energy to slow the reverse reaction', 'The catalyst is consumed in the reaction', ]} correctAnswer={0} explanation=&quot;A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy $E_a$. ## Common Mistakes

**Confusing reaction order with stoichiometric coefficients:** The rate law $\text{rate} = k[A]^m[B]^n$ has orders $m$ and $n$ determined experimentally, not from the balanced equation. A reaction $2A \to B$ is not necessarily second order in $A$ — it could be first order, zero order, or any order. Always determine orders from experimental data.

**Using the wrong integrated rate law:** Each reaction order has a different integrated rate law: zero order ($[A] = [A]_0 - kt$), first order ($\ln[A] = \ln[A]_0 - kt$), second order ($1/[A] = 1/[A]_0 + kt$). Using the wrong one gives incorrect rate constants and half-lives. Check which plot gives a straight line to identify the order.

**Confusing the Arrhenius equation activation energy with the overall reaction energy:** The activation energy $E_a$ is the energy barrier for the rate-determining step, not the overall $\Delta H$ or $\Delta G$ of the reaction. A reaction can be highly exothermic ($\Delta H \ll 0$) but slow (high $E_a$). Do not confuse thermodynamic favourability with kinetic speed.
