## Physical Chemistry -- Practice Problems

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: First-Order Kinetics — Drug Degradation

**Problem:** A drug degrades via first-order kinetics with $k = 2.5 \times 10^{-4}$ min$^{-1}$ at 25°C. If the initial concentration is 100 mg/L, how long does it take for the concentration to drop to 90 mg/L?

**Solution:**

For first-order kinetics: $[A] = [A]_0 e^{-kt}$

Rearranging for time:

$$t = -\frac{1}{k} \ln\frac{[A]}{[A]_0} = -\frac{1}{2.5 \times 10^{-4}} \ln\frac{90}{100}$$

$$t = -4000 \times \ln(0.90) = -4000 \times (-0.1054) = 421 \text{ min} \approx 7.0 \text{ hours}$$

**Half-life check:** $t_{1/2} = \ln 2 / k = 0.693 / 2.5 \times 10^{-4} = 2772$ min $\approx 46$ hours. Since we're only losing 10% (not 50%), the time should be much less than the half-life. ✓

**Key insight:** First-order half-lives are independent of concentration. This is why drug shelf-life is expressed as $t_{90}$ (time to 90% remaining), not as an absolute amount.

### Example 2: Particle in a Box — Conjugated Systems

**Problem:** For $\beta$-carotene (11 conjugated double bonds), model the $\pi$ electrons as particles in a box. The conjugation length is approximately 1.80 nm. Calculate the wavelength of the lowest-energy electronic transition.

**Solution:**

With 11 double bonds, there are 22 $\pi$ electrons. Each energy level holds 2 electrons (Pauli exclusion), so the HOMO has $n = 11$ and the LUMO has $n = 12$.

Energy levels: $E_n = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8mL^2}$

$$\Delta E = E_{12} - E_{11} = \frac{h^2}{8mL^2}(12^2 - 11^2) = \frac{h^2}{8mL^2}(23)$$

$$\Delta E = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34})^2 \times 23}{8 \times (9.109 \times 10^{-31}) \times (1.80 \times 10^{-9})^2}$$

$$\Delta E = \frac{4.39 \times 10^{-66} \times 23}{8 \times 9.109 \times 10^{-31} \times 3.24 \times 10^{-18}}$$

$$\Delta E = \frac{1.01 \times 10^{-64}}{2.36 \times 10^{-47}} = 4.28 \times 10^{-18} \text{ J}$$

Converting to wavelength:

$$\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E} = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^8)}{4.28 \times 10^{-18}} = 4.64 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m} = 464 \text{ nm}$$

**Comparison:** Experimental $\lambda_{\max} = 452$ nm. The particle-in-a-box model gives a reasonable estimate (within 3%) for this highly conjugated system.

### Example 3: Boltzmann Distribution at Different Temperatures

**Problem:** Calculate the ratio of molecules in the first excited rotational state ($J = 1$) to the ground state ($J = 0$) for HCl at 300 K and 1000 K. The rotational temperature is $\Theta_{\text{rot}} = 15.2$ K.

**Solution:**

For a linear rotor, the energy of level $J$ is $E_J = BJ(J+1)$ where $B = k_B \Theta_{\text{rot}}$.

The degeneracy of level $J$ is $g_J = 2J + 1$.

$$\frac{n_1}{n_0} = \frac{g_1 e^{-E_1/k_BT}}{g_0 e^{-E_0/k_BT}} = \frac{3 \times e^{-2\Theta_{\text{rot}}/T}}{1 \times e^{0}} = 3e^{-2\Theta_{\text{rot}}/T}$$

**At 300 K:**

$$\frac{n_1}{n_0} = 3e^{-2(15.2)/300} = 3e^{-0.1013} = 3 \times 0.9037 = 2.71$$

**At 1000 K:**

$$\frac{n_1}{n_0} = 3e^{-2(15.2)/1000} = 3e^{-0.0304} = 3 \times 0.9701 = 2.91$$

**Key insight:** At room temperature, the $J = 1$ state is already more populated than $J = 0$ (ratio 2.71) due to the 3-fold degeneracy advantage. At higher temperatures, the ratio approaches the degeneracy ratio (3:1) as the exponential factor approaches 1.

## Intuition

**Physical chemistry is the mathematical backbone of all chemistry:** It connects abstract thermodynamic quantities (entropy, free energy) to measurable quantities (temperature, pressure, voltage). Think of it as the "physics of chemical systems" — applying energy conservation and statistical reasoning to molecules.

**Why it matters:** Physical chemistry principles underpin battery design, refrigeration, atmospheric chemistry, and pharmaceutical stability. Without understanding Gibbs free energy, you cannot predict whether a drug will degrade on the shelf.

**The key insight:** $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$ is the single most important equation in chemistry — it determines spontaneity at constant temperature and pressure, unifying enthalpy and entropy into one decision rule.
