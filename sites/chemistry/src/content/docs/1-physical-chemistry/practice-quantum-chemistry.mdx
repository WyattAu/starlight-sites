## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Particle in a Box — Conjugated Polyene

**Problem:** For 1,3-butadiene (4 $\pi$ electrons), model the conjugated system as a particle in a box. The C-C bond length is 1.34 Å, and the box extends 0.5 Å beyond each end carbon. Calculate the wavelength of the HOMO→LUMO transition.

**Solution:**

**Step 1:** Determine box length

$L = 3 \times 1.34 + 2 \times 0.5 = 5.02$ Å = $5.02 \times 10^{-10}$ m

**Step 2:** Identify HOMO and LUMO

4 $\pi$ electrons fill $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ (2 electrons per level). HOMO: $n = 2$. LUMO: $n = 3$.

**Step 3:** Calculate energy gap

$$\Delta E = \frac{h^2}{8mL^2}(3^2 - 2^2) = \frac{h^2}{8mL^2}(5)$$

$$\Delta E = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34})^2 \times 5}{8 \times (9.109 \times 10^{-31}) \times (5.02 \times 10^{-10})^2}$$

$$\Delta E = \frac{2.195 \times 10^{-66}}{1.838 \times 10^{-48}} = 1.194 \times 10^{-18} \text{ J}$$

**Step 4:** Convert to wavelength

$$\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E} = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^8)}{1.194 \times 10^{-18}} = 1.664 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m} = 166 \text{ nm}$$

**Comparison:** Experimental $\lambda_{\max} = 217$ nm. The particle-in-a-box model underestimates the wavelength because it assumes constant potential (no electron-nuclear attraction).

### Example 2: Hydrogen Atom — Transition Wavelengths

**Problem:** Calculate the wavelength of the photon emitted when an electron in hydrogen transitions from $n = 4$ to $n = 2$ (Balmer series).

**Solution:**

$$\Delta E = E_4 - E_2 = -13.6\left(\frac{1}{16} - \frac{1}{4}\right) = -13.6 \times (-0.1875) = +2.55 \text{ eV}$$

Converting to wavelength:

$$\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E} = \frac{1240 \text{ eV·nm}}{2.55 \text{ eV}} = 486 \text{ nm}$$

This is the **H$\beta$ line** in the visible Balmer series (blue-green light).

**Balmer series formula:** $\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H\left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{n^2}\right)$ for $n = 3, 4, 5, \ldots$

| Transition | $n_i \to n_f$ | $\lambda$ (nm) | Colour |
|------------|---------------|----------------|--------|
| H$\alpha$ | $3 \to 2$ | 656 | Red |
| H$\beta$ | $4 \to 2$ | 486 | Blue-green |
| H$\gamma$ | $5 \to 2$ | 434 | Violet |
| H$\delta$ | $6 \to 2$ | 410 | Violet |

### Example 3: Harmonic Oscillator — Vibrational Spectroscopy

**Problem:** HCl has a fundamental vibrational frequency of $\tilde{\nu} = 2990$ cm⁻¹. Calculate the force constant and the zero-point energy.

**Solution:**

**Step 1:** Calculate the force constant

$$k = 4\pi^2 c^2 \tilde{\nu}^2 \mu$$

Reduced mass of HCl:

$$\mu = \frac{m_H \cdot m_{Cl}}{m_H + m_{Cl}} = \frac{1.008 \times 35.45}{1.008 + 35.45} \times \frac{1}{N_A} = 1.628 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}$$

$$k = 4\pi^2 (3.00 \times 10^{10} \text{ cm/s})^2 (2990 \text{ cm}^{-1})^2 (1.628 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg})$$

$$k = 516 \text{ N/m}$$

**Step 2:** Calculate zero-point energy

$$E_0 = \frac{1}{2}h c \tilde{\nu} = \frac{1}{2}(6.626 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^{10})(2990)$$

$$E_0 = 2.97 \times 10^{-20} \text{ J} = 0.186 \text{ eV}$$

**Key insight:** The zero-point energy means HCl continues to vibrate even at absolute zero. This is a direct consequence of the uncertainty principle — confining the oscillator to its equilibrium position would violate $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \hbar/2$.

## Intuition

**Quantum chemistry replaces classical orbits with probability clouds:** Electrons do not orbit the nucleus like planets around the sun. Instead, they exist as wavefunctions — mathematical descriptions of where an electron is *likely* to be found. The Schrödinger equation is the master equation that determines these probability distributions.

**Why it matters:** Quantum chemistry explains why atoms bond, why metals conduct electricity, and why semiconductors have band gaps. Without it, modern electronics, solar cells, and LED technology would be impossible to design.

**The key insight:** The Pauli exclusion principle — no two electrons can occupy the same quantum state — is why the periodic table has its structure and why matter takes up space.

## Common Mistakes

**Confusing the rigid rotor with the harmonic oscillator:** The rigid rotor model ($E_J = J(J+1)\hbar^2/2I$) describes rotation with equally spaced transitions ($\Delta E = 2B$). The harmonic oscillator ($E_v = (v+1/2)\hbar\omega$) describes vibration with equally spaced transitions ($\Delta E = \hbar\omega$). Mixing up these models gives incorrect spectral predictions.

**Assuming all transitions are allowed:** The selection rule $\Delta J = \pm 1$ for rotational spectroscopy comes from conservation of angular momentum during photon absorption. Transitions with $\Delta J \neq \pm 1$ are forbidden in the electric dipole approximation. Ignoring selection rules leads to predicting spectral lines that do not exist.

**Forgetting that the reduced mass $\mu$ differs from the atomic mass:** For a diatomic molecule AB, the reduced mass is $\mu = m_A m_B / (m_A + m_B)$, not directly $m_A$ or $m_B$. Using the wrong mass in rotational or vibrational calculations gives incorrect energy levels and spectral frequencies.
