**Q1. In the Boltzmann distribution, the probability of finding a particle in state $i$ with energy $\varepsilon_i$ at temperature $T$ is $p_i = e^{-\varepsilon_i/k_BT}/q$. What is the molecular partition function $q$?**

A. $q = \sum_i e^{-\varepsilon_i/k_BT}

**Answer: A**
— The partition function $q = \sum_i e^{-\varepsilon_i/k_BT}$ is the normalization constant for the Boltzmann distribution, ensuring $\sum_i p_i = 1$. It encodes all thermodynamic information: $U = Nk_B T^2 (\partial \ln q / \partial T)_V$, $S = Nk_B(\ln q + T(\partial \ln q / \partial T)_V)$, $A = -Nk_BT\ln q$. Higher temperatures give larger $q$ (more states accessible).

**Q2. For a system with energy levels $\varepsilon_0 = 0$, $\varepsilon_1 = \epsilon$, $\varepsilon_2 = 2\epsilon$ (each non-degenerate), what is the partition function at temperature $T$?**

A. $q = 1 + e^{-\epsilon/k_BT}

**Answer: A**
— $q = \sum_i g_i e^{-\varepsilon_i/k_BT}$ where $g_i$ is the degeneracy. Since all states are non-degenerate ($g_i = 1$): $q = e^{-0/k_BT} + e^{-\epsilon/k_BT} + e^{-2\epsilon/k_BT} = 1 + e^{-\epsilon/k_BT} + e^{-2\epsilon/k_BT}$. At high $T$ ($k_BT \gg \epsilon$), all three terms approach 1 and $q \to 3$. At low $T$, $q \to 1$.

**Q3. The translational partition function for a single ideal gas molecule in a container of volume $V$ at temperature $T$ is:**

A. $q_{\text{trans}

**Answer: A**
— From quantum mechanics, the translational energy levels in a 3D box give $q_{\text{trans}} = \sum_{n_x,n_y,n_z} \exp\left(-\frac{h^2}{8mk_B T V^{2/3}}(n_x^2+n_y^2+n_z^2)\right)$. In the classical limit (high $T$, large $V$), this sum becomes the integral $q_{\text{trans}} = V(2\pi m k_B T/h^2)^{3/2}$. The $V$ dependence leads directly to the ideal gas equation of state.
*Difficulty: hard*


**Q4. The rotational partition function for a heteronuclear diatomic molecule (treated as a rigid rotor) at sufficiently high temperature is:**

A. $q_{\text{rot}

**Answer: A**
— At high $T$ ($T \gg \Theta_r = \hbar^2/2Ik_B$), $q_{\text{rot}} = T/(\sigma \Theta_r)$. The symmetry number $\sigma = 1$ for heteronuclear diatomics (e.g., HCl) and $\sigma = 2$ for homonuclear (e.g., N$_2$, accounting for indistinguishable orientations). This factor prevents overcounting rotational states by the indistinguishable rotations.

**Q5. The vibrational partition function for a harmonic oscillator with frequency $\ u$ is $q_{\text{vib}} = \frac{e^{-h\ u/2k_BT}}{1 - e^{-h\ u/k_BT}}$. At room temperature ($T \ll \Theta_{\text{vib}} = h\ u/k_B$), the vibrational contribution to the internal energy is approximately:**

A. $U_{\text{vib}

**Answer: A**
— At $T \ll \Theta_{\text{vib}}$, most molecules are in the ground vibrational state ($v = 0$), so $U_{\text{vib}} \approx N\varepsilon_0 = N(h\ u/2)$. The classical equipartition prediction ($k_BT$ per vibrational mode) is not reached because the energy spacing $h\ u$ is much larger than $k_BT$. Only at $T \gg \Theta_{\text{vib}}$ does $U_{\text{vib}} \to Nk_BT$.

**Q6. The Sackur-Tetrode equation gives the molar entropy of a monatomic ideal gas: $S_m = R\ln\left[\frac{e^{5/2} V}{N_A \Lambda^3}\right]$. What is the thermal de Broglie wavelength $\Lambda$?**

A. $\Lambda = \frac{h}

**Answer: A**
— The thermal de Broglie wavelength $\Lambda = h / \sqrt{2\pi m k_B T}$ represents the quantum wavelength associated with a particle at temperature $T$. When $V/N\Lambda^3 \gg 1$, the gas behaves classically (Boltzmann statistics). When $V/N\Lambda^3 \lesssim 1$, quantum effects (Bose-Einstein or Fermi-Dirac statistics) become important.

**Q7. The canonical ensemble describes a system at fixed $N$, $V$, and $T$. The canonical partition function is $Q = q^N/N!$ (for distinguishable translational states). The Helmholtz free energy is:**

A. &quot;$A = -k_BT \ln Q = -Nk_BT(\ln q - \ln N + 1)$ using Stirling's approximation&quot;, '$A = k_BT \ln Q$
B. $A = -Nk_BT \ln q$
C. $A = E - TS + PV$',

**Answer: A**
— $A = -k_BT \ln Q$. For an ideal gas: $Q = q^N/N!$, so $\ln Q = N\ln q - \ln(N!) \approx N\ln q - N\ln N + N$ (Stirling). Thus $A = -Nk_BT(\ln(q/N) + 1)$. From $A$, all other thermodynamic properties follow: $S = -(\partial A/\partial T)_V$, $P = -(\partial A/\partial V)_T$, $U = A + TS$.
*Difficulty: medium*


**Q8. The Maxwell-Boltzmann speed distribution gives the probability density for molecular speeds as $f(v) \propto v^2 e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}$. The most probable speed is:**

A. $v_p = \sqrt{2k_BT/m}

**Answer: A**
— The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution gives three characteristic speeds: most probable $v_p = \sqrt{2k_BT/m}$, mean $\langle v \rangle = \sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m}$, and root-mean-square $v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3k_BT/m}$. The relationship is $v_p < \langle v \rangle < v_{\text{rms}}$. All scale as $\sqrt{T/m}$.

**Q9. The Boltzmann entropy formula $S = k_B \ln W$ relates entropy to the number of microstates $W$. For a system of $N$ distinguishable particles with two energy levels ($\varepsilon_0$ and $\varepsilon_1$), if $n$ particles are in the excited state, $W = \binom{N}{n}$. What is the maximum entropy configuration?**

A. Maximum $S$ when $n = N/2$ (equal populations of both levels)
B. Maximum $S$ when $n = N$ (all particles excited)
C. Maximum $S$ when $n = 0$ (all particles in ground state)
D. Maximum $S$ when $n = 1$ (single excitation)',

**Answer: A**
— $W = \binom{N}{n}$ is maximized when $n = N/2$, giving the most microstates. $S = k_B \ln \binom{N}{n}$ is therefore maximized at equal populations. This is the Second Law: spontaneous processes tend toward configurations with more microstates (higher $W$, higher $S$). At finite $T$, the actual equilibrium population depends on the energy gap via the Boltzmann distribution.
*Difficulty: hard*


**Q10. The equipartition theorem states that each quadratic degree of freedom contributes $\frac{1}{2}k_BT$ to the internal energy. For a diatomic gas at moderate temperatures (translational and rotational modes fully excited, vibrational modes frozen), what is $C_V$?**

A. $C_V = \frac{5}

**Answer: A**
— A diatomic molecule has 3 translational degrees of freedom (contributes $3/2 R$ to $C_V$), 2 rotational (linear molecule rotates about 2 axes, contributes $2 \times 1/2 R = R$), and 1 vibrational mode. At moderate temperatures ($T_{\text{rot}} \ll T \ll T_{\text{vib}}$), vibration is not excited. Total $C_V = 3/2 R + R = 5/2 R$. At very high $T$, vibration adds $R$ more (kinetic + potential), giving $7/2 R$.
