## Intuition

**Thermodynamics is nature's bookkeeping for energy:** The laws of thermodynamics are not abstract rules — they describe what is physically possible. Energy cannot be created (first law), and processes always increase total disorder (second law). These constraints shape everything from engine design to biological processes.

**Why it matters:** Thermodynamics determines whether fuels will burn, whether drugs are stable, and whether power plants can generate electricity efficiently.

**The key insight:** A negative $\Delta G$ means a reaction is spontaneous, but spontaneous does not mean fast — thermodynamics tells you the destination, kinetics tells you the speed.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Calculating Work for Isothermal Expansion

**Problem:** 2.00 mol of an ideal gas expands reversibly from 5.00 L to 15.0 L at 300 K. Calculate the work done.

**Solution:**

For a reversible isothermal expansion of an ideal gas:

$$w = -nRT \ln\frac{V_f}{V_i}$$

Substituting values:

$$w = -(2.00 \text{ mol})(8.314 \text{ J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1})(300 \text{ K}) \ln\frac{15.0}{5.00}$$

$$w = -(2.00)(8.314)(300) \ln(3.00)$$

$$w = -(4988.4)(1.0986)$$

$$w = -5481 \text{ J} = -5.48 \text{ kJ}$$

**Physical interpretation:** The negative sign indicates work is done *by* the gas on the surroundings. The gas pushes against the external pressure as it expands. Since the process is isothermal and the gas is ideal, $\Delta U = 0$, so $q = -w = +5.48$ kJ (heat absorbed from surroundings).

### Example 2: Entropy Change for Irreversible Process

**Problem:** 1.00 mol of an ideal gas at 300 K is expanded irreversibly against a constant external pressure of 1.00 bar from 10.0 L to 20.0 L. Calculate $\Delta S_{\text{system}}$, $\Delta S_{\text{surroundings}}$, and $\Delta S_{\text{total}}$.

**Solution:**

**Step 1: Calculate $\Delta S_{\text{system}}$**

Entropy is a state function. We design a reversible path between the same states:

$$\Delta S_{\text{system}} = nR \ln\frac{V_f}{V_i} = (1.00)(8.314) \ln\frac{20.0}{10.0} = 8.314 \times 0.6931 = +5.76 \text{ J K}^{-1}$$

**Step 2: Calculate $q_{\text{irrev}}$**

For expansion against constant external pressure:

$$w = -P_{\text{ext}} \Delta V = -(1.00 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa})(20.0 - 10.0) \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}^3 = -1000 \text{ J}$$

Since $\Delta U = 0$ (isothermal, ideal gas): $q = -w = +1000$ J

**Step 3: Calculate $\Delta S_{\text{surroundings}}$**

$$\Delta S_{\text{surr}} = \frac{-q_{\text{irrev}}}{T_{\text{surr}}} = \frac{-1000}{300} = -3.33 \text{ J K}^{-1}$$

**Step 4: Calculate $\Delta S_{\text{total}}$**

$$\Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S_{\text{surr}} = 5.76 - 3.33 = +2.43 \text{ J K}^{-1}$$

**Key insight:** $\Delta S_{\text{total}} > 0$ confirms the process is irreversible (spontaneous). The entropy generated ($+2.43$ J K$^{-1}$) represents the "cost" of irreversibility.

### Example 3: Gibbs Free Energy and Spontaneity

**Problem:** At 298 K, the standard Gibbs free energy of formation are: $\Delta_f G^\circ(\text{H}_2\text{O}, l) = -237.1$ kJ/mol and $\Delta_f G^\circ(\text{H}_2\text{O}_2, l) = -120.4$ kJ/mol. For the reaction $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2(l) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2(g)$, calculate $\Delta_r G^\circ$ and determine spontaneity.

**Solution:**

$$\Delta_r G^\circ = \sum \Delta_f G^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta_f G^\circ(\text{reactants})$$

$$\Delta_r G^\circ = \left[(-237.1) + \frac{1}{2}(0)\right] - \left[(-120.4)\right]$$

$$\Delta_r G^\circ = -237.1 + 120.4 = -116.7 \text{ kJ/mol}$$

Since $\Delta_r G^\circ < 0$, the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide is **spontaneous** under standard conditions.

**Physical interpretation:** The large negative $\Delta_r G^\circ$ explains why $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ is unstable and decomposes readily. The driving force is the formation of very stable water molecules.

### Example 4: Van't Hoff Equation Application

**Problem:** For the reaction $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NO}_2(g)$, $K_p = 0.148$ at 298 K and $\Delta H^\circ = +57.2$ kJ/mol. Calculate $K_p$ at 350 K.

**Solution:**

Using the integrated van't Hoff equation:

$$\ln\frac{K_2}{K_1} = -\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)$$

$$\ln\frac{K_2}{0.148} = -\frac{57200}{8.314}\left(\frac{1}{350} - \frac{1}{298}\right)$$

$$\ln\frac{K_2}{0.148} = -(6880.5)(0.002857 - 0.003356)$$

$$\ln\frac{K_2}{0.148} = -(6880.5)(-0.000499) = +3.433$$

$$\frac{K_2}{0.148} = e^{3.433} = 30.95$$

$$K_2 = 0.148 \times 30.95 = 4.58$$

**Key insight:** For this endothermic reaction ($\Delta H^\circ > 0$), increasing temperature increases $K_p$ (from 0.148 to 4.58). This is consistent with Le Chatelier's principle: adding heat favors the endothermic direction.
