## What Guide Signs Do

Guide signs are the network's wayfinding layer: green for directions
and distances, blue for motorist services, brown for recreation and
cultural sites. Warning and regulatory signs tell you what to *do*;
guide signs tell you *where you are* and *how to get where you're
going*. Written tests check both the color meanings and route-marker
logic.

## Green: Directions and Distances

- **Distance signs**: list destinations with mileages; the top
  destination is the nearest major city on this route.
- **Exit signs**: exit number = mile marker on most interstates
  (post-2000s renumbering), so "Exit 212" means mile 212 — a built-in
  navigation check.
- **Action-point signs**: "Exit 25 — 1 mile", then ½ mile, then the
  exit gore with an arrow. Position your lane change at the first one.
- **Destination/junction signs**: announce upcoming route numbers;
  a shield with "TO" routes you via a connector.
- **Advance guide sign test trap**: the correct lane for an exit is
  shown by the arrow position on the sign, not the sign's order.

## Blue: Motorist Services

Blue squares mark services you may need mid-trip:

- Gas, food, lodging (the classic tri-service sign)
- Hospitals (blue with a white H)
- Rest areas (blue with a picnic-table or restroom symbol)
- Evacuation route markers (blue with a wave — coastal states)
- Phone/emergency services along highways

Blue signs never regulate traffic; a blue "hospital ahead" requires
no action unless you need one.

## Brown: Recreation and Culture

Brown signs point to parks, forests, historic sites, beaches, ski
areas, and attractions. Logos on brown panels (state tourist
attractions) indicate the attraction's own branding. Tests ask one
canonical question: brown = **recreational and cultural interest**.

## Route Markers and Numbering Logic

| Marker | Network | Numbering logic |
|--------|---------|-----------------|
| Red-white-blue shield | Interstate | Even = east-west, odd = north-south; 3-digit spurs serve cities |
| White shield on black | US Highway | Same parity logic, older network |
| State outline/pentagon | State routes | Formats vary by state |
| Green/white county | County roads | Varies |

The interstate parity rule is a recurring test question: **even
numbers run east-west, odd numbers run north-south**, and a leading
odd digit (I-405) is a spur/loop off a parent route (I-405 branches
from I-5's corridor).

## Mile Markers and Exit Numbers

Mile markers count from the state's southern or western border. If
your GPS says exit 40 and signs show exit 38, the next exit is yours —
mile-based exits make walkie-talkie-style coordination possible
("pickup at mile 112"). States that never converted (a handful in the
northeast) use sequential exit numbers instead; the testable default
is the mile-based system.

## How Guide Signs Are Tested

1. **Color meaning**: green = direction/distance, blue = services,
   brown = recreation. One-point recall.
2. **Route logic**: even/odd interstate directions; 3-digit = spur.
3. **Sign recognition**: hospital H (blue), rest area (blue), picnic
   area (brown), evacuation route (blue wave).
4. **Action questions**: "first advance sign for your exit appears —
   what now?" Answer: move into the proper lane early, don't brake
   yet.

| Number | Direction | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| Even | East-west | I-10, I-80, I-90 |
| Odd | North-south | I-5, I-95 |
| 3-digit odd prefix | Spur into city | I-405 off I-5 corridor logic |
| 3-digit even prefix | Belt/loop bypass | Beltways around metros |
