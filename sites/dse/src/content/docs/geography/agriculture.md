# Agricultural Systems and Food Security

## Key Terms

- **Subsistence agriculture:** Farming practiced primarily to provide food for the farmer and their
  family, with minimal surplus for sale or trade. Characterised by low input, low output, and
  labour-intensive methods.
- **Commercial agriculture:** Farming conducted primarily for sale and profit, using higher inputs
  (machinery, fertilisers, irrigation) and producing larger quantities for domestic and export
  markets.
- **Intensive agriculture:** Farming that maximises output per unit of land through high inputs of
  labour, capital, or technology (e.g., high-yielding crop varieties, irrigation, chemical inputs).
- **Extensive agriculture:** Farming that uses relatively large areas of land per unit of output,
  with low labour inputs and low yields per hectare.
- **Rice paddy (wet rice cultivation):** A system of rice growing in flooded fields (paddies),
  requiring flat terrain, controlled water levels, and warm temperatures throughout the growing
  season.
- **Food security:** A condition in which all people have physical, social, and economic access to
  sufficient, safe, and nutritious food at all times to meet their dietary needs and food
  preferences for an active and healthy life.
- **Urban agriculture:** The cultivation of food within and around urban areas, including
  rooftop gardens, community farms, and aquaponic systems.

## Agricultural Systems

### Subsistence vs Commercial Agriculture

Subsistence agriculture is farming practiced primarily to provide food for the farmer and family,
with minimal surplus for sale, characterised by low inputs, low outputs, and labour-intensive methods.
Commercial agriculture is conducted primarily for sale and profit, using higher inputs of machinery,
fertilisers, and irrigation, producing larger quantities for domestic and export markets.

| Feature | Subsistence | Commercial |
|---------|------------|------------|
| Purpose | Feed the farmer and family | Sale and profit |
| Farm size | Small (often less than 2 hectares) | Large (hundreds or thousands of hectares) |
| Mechanisation | Minimal; manual labour dominant | High; tractors, harvesters, irrigation |
| Crop type | Staple crops (rice, millet, cassava) | Cash crops (wheat, soybeans, vegetables) |
| Surplus | Little or none | Significant; sold domestically or exported |
| Labour | Family labour | Hired wage labour |

Most farmers in Southeast Asia practice a mixture of subsistence and commercial farming. The Green
Revolution (from the 1960s) promoted the adoption of high-yielding varieties (HYVs), chemical
fertilisers, and irrigation, transforming much subsistence agriculture into semi-commercial or fully
commercial systems.

### Von Thunen's Model of Agricultural Location

Johann Heinrich von Thunen (1826) proposed a model of agricultural land use based on distance from a
central market, assuming a flat, featureless plain with uniform soil and climate. The model predicts
concentric rings of agricultural activity: dairying and intensive horticulture nearest the market
(perishable, high-value products), then forest (bulky firewood and building materials), then grain
farming (lower transport costs per unit value), and finally livestock ranching at the periphery
(extensive land use requiring large areas).

Land rent at any point is determined by:

$$\text{Land Rent} = \text{Market Price} - \text{Production Cost} - \text{Transport Cost}$$

Crops that are bulky, heavy, or perishable have steeper transport cost gradients and are located
closer to the market.

## Rice Cultivation in Southeast Asia

Rice cultivation requires specific environmental conditions: minimum temperatures of
$20\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ during the growing season (optimal at $25$ to $30\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$),
minimum $1{,}000\ \mathrm{mm}$ of rainfall (most paddies require $1{,}500$ to $2{,}000\ \mathrm{mm}$
annually), standing water (5-10 cm depth) throughout most of the growing period, flat terrain for
field levelling, alluvial soils with high clay content, and labour-intensive management at all stages.

### The Green Revolution and Rice

The Green Revolution transformed rice cultivation in Southeast Asia through high-yielding varieties
(HYVs) developed at the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI) in the Philippines (IR8, released
in 1966, doubled yields), irrigation infrastructure enabling double and triple cropping, chemical
fertilisers and pesticides, and mechanisation including tractors and combine harvesters.

### Consequences of the Green Revolution

The Green Revolution produced dramatic increases in rice production and reduced food insecurity in
South and Southeast Asia. However, it also generated increased inequality (larger farmers could
afford HYV inputs while smaller farmers could not), environmental degradation (chemical runoff,
soil degradation, biodiversity loss), displacement of traditional rice varieties, and
groundwater depletion from water-intensive systems.

## Agricultural Change in the Pearl River Delta

### Historical Context

The Pearl River Delta in Guangdong Province was historically one of China's most productive
agricultural regions, known as the "Land of Fish and Rice." Wet rice cultivation, fish ponds, and
sugar cane production dominated the landscape.

### Urbanisation and Industrialisation

Since the economic reforms of 1978, the Pearl River Delta has undergone one of the most rapid
agricultural-to-industrial transitions in history. Vast areas of rice paddies and vegetable gardens
have been converted to factories, roads, and housing. Agriculture's share of GDP in Guangdong
Province has declined from approximately 30% in 1978 to below 5% recently, while rural workers have
moved to urban factories or service industries, making the Pearl River Delta home to tens of millions
of migrant workers. Some agricultural land is held by farmers in anticipation of future development,
leading to underutilisation.

### Remaining Agriculture

Despite urbanisation, some agriculture persists in the Pearl River Delta:

- **High-value horticulture:** Flowers, vegetables, and fruits for urban markets.
- **Aquaculture:** Fish, shrimp, and shellfish farming in coastal and inland ponds. The Pearl River
  Delta is one of China's largest aquaculture regions.
- **Organic and niche farming:** Some farms have pivoted to organic produce, agritourism, and
  specialty products for the growing urban middle class.

### Food Security in Hong Kong

#### The Food Import Dependency

Hong Kong imports approximately 90% of its food supply, making it one of the most
food-import-dependent territories in the world. This creates significant vulnerability to supply
chain disruption (e.g., border closures during the COVID-19 pandemic), price volatility, and
quality control challenges across complex international supply chains.

### Food Sources

Mainland China provides approximately 90% of fresh vegetables, live poultry, and live fish, plus
significant quantities of meat and rice. Southeast Asia supplies rice (Thailand, Vietnam) and
seafood. Australia, New Zealand, and other developed countries provide meat, dairy, and winter
vegetables. Global markets supply fruits, processed foods, and specialty items.

### Strategies to Enhance Food Security

Strategies include the Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation Department supporting local farmers
through subsidies and technical advice, the New Agricultural Policy (2016) promoting modernised
farming in the New Territories, limited strategic rice stockpiling, diversification of supply
sources, and cross-boundary food safety cooperation with mainland China.

## Urban Agriculture

Urban agriculture has gained attention for enhancing food security, reducing food miles, and
improving urban green spaces. Types in Hong Kong include rooftop farms on public housing estates,
community farms in the New Territories, indoor and vertical farming using hydroponic or aeroponic
systems (producing year-round with minimal water, though energy costs are high), and aquaponics
integrating fish farming with plant cultivation. Challenges include extremely high land costs,
regulatory barriers, the fact that urban agriculture can only meet a tiny fraction of food demand,
and limited public knowledge despite growing interest.

## Fisheries in the South China Sea

The South China Sea supports over 3,000 fish species and is one of the world's most productive
fisheries. Coral reefs provide critical nursery habitat. Methods range from traditional artisanal
fishing to large-scale commercial trawling.

### Threats to Fisheries

Fisheries face overfishing (many stocks depleted, bottom trawling destroying seabed habitats),
territorial disputes (China, Vietnam, the Philippines, Taiwan, Malaysia, and Brunei claim
overlapping portions), environmental degradation (coral reef destruction and pollution), and climate
change (ocean warming and acidification affecting reef health and fish distribution).

### Hong Kong's Fishing Industry

Hong Kong's fishing industry has declined significantly since the 1970s-1980s due to overfishing,
pollution, and urban encroachment. Mariculture in sheltered bays produces fish in floating cages,
limited by water quality, disease, and typhoon damage. Fishing and mariculture now contribute less
than 0.1% of GDP and support approximately 10,000 workers.

## Intuition

Agriculture is the original human technology — the first time we stopped chasing food and started making it. The Green Revolution was like upgrading from a hand tool to a power drill: same job, dramatically more output. But intensive farming is a bargain with the soil — you get more crops now but deplete the nutrients that will grow crops later. Urban agriculture is the surprise return of farming to the city, like rooftop gardens and vertical farms that bring the countryside to the skyline. The South China Sea fisheries show what happens when a shared resource has no shared management — everyone takes as much as they can, and the fish disappear.

## Exam Tips

1. **Distinguish agricultural systems:** When comparing subsistence and commercial farming,
   use specific examples (e.g., shifting cultivation in Borneo versus rice paddy farming in the
   Mekong Delta) and reference specific inputs, outputs, and environmental conditions.

2. **Use the von Thunen model critically:** The model assumes a simplified world without transport
   infrastructure, climate variation, or government policy. Apply it to real-world examples while
   noting its limitations.

3. **Reference specific case studies:** For the Pearl River Delta, mention specific cities
   (Shenzhen, Guangzhou) and the scale of land conversion. For rice cultivation, cite specific
   yield data or mention IR8 and the IRRI.

4. **Address food security holistically:** Food security encompasses availability (is food
   produced?), access (can people afford it?), utilisation (is it nutritious?), and stability (is
   supply reliable?). Discuss all four dimensions.

5. **Evaluate urban agriculture realistically:** While urban agriculture has symbolic and
   educational value, acknowledge that it cannot significantly contribute to Hong Kong's food
   supply at current scales. Its value lies in awareness, community building, and marginal
   supply diversification.

6. **Link to other topics:** Connect agriculture to climate (changing rainfall and temperature
   affecting yields), population (food demand linked to population size and income), and resources
   (water use for irrigation, land use competition with urbanisation).

## Common Mistakes

**Confusing subsistence with commercial agriculture based on crop type alone:** Both can grow rice. The difference is purpose (feeding family vs selling) and scale (small vs large), not just what's grown.

**Assuming the Green Revolution was purely beneficial:** It increased yields but also caused inequality (small farmers couldn't afford inputs), environmental damage (chemical runoff), and loss of traditional varieties.

**Mixing up food security with food self-sufficiency:** Hong Kong imports 90% of its food but is food secure because it can afford to buy what it needs. Food self-sufficiency means producing your own food, which is different from having access to food.
