### Additional Flashcard Topics

- **Demographic Transition Model**: describes population change through five stages: high birth/death (pre-industrial), falling death rate (transitional), falling birth rate (industrial), low birth/death (post-industrial), and declining population. Each stage has different implications for development. Stage 2 countries (e.g., many African nations) have rapid population growth; Stage 4 countries (e.g., Japan) face aging populations.

- **Plate Tectonics**: divergent boundaries (plates move apart, new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges), convergent boundaries (plates collide, causing subduction zones or mountain building), transform boundaries (plates slide past each other, causing earthquakes). These explain volcanic and seismic activity patterns worldwide.

- **Urbanisation Patterns**: primate cities dominate national urban systems (e.g., Bangkok in Thailand), while gamma cities are smaller regional centres. Urbanisation rates vary by development level. LDCs urbanise faster as rural populations migrate to cities seeking employment.

- **Climate Classification**: the Köppen system uses temperature and precipitation patterns to classify climates into five major groups (A-E). Tropical (A), arid (B), temperate (C), continental (D), polar (E). Each group is further divided based on seasonal patterns.

- **Development Indicators**: HDI (Human Development Index) combines life expectancy, education, and GDP per capita. It provides a broader measure of development than GDP alone. Countries with high HDI (e.g., Norway) in standard practice have strong healthcare, education, and economic systems.

- **Natural Hazards**: earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanic eruptions, hurricanes, and floods are classified by magnitude and frequency. Vulnerability depends on exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity. A wealthy country may experience fewer deaths from the same hazard than a poor country due to better infrastructure and warning systems.

## Intuition

Geography connects people to place. Think of it as understanding why things are where they are: why cities form at river mouths, why earthquakes follow fault lines, why some countries are rich and others poor. Each flashcard reveals a pattern that connects physical processes to human outcomes. The DSE Geography exam rewards the ability to link physical geography (tectonics, climate) with human geography (urbanisation, development) in a single answer.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Confusing weather and climate**: weather is short-term (days), climate is long-term average (30+ years). "It's cold today" is weather; "London has mild winters" is climate. Never use the terms interchangeably in exam answers.
- **Ignoring scale**: a local flood is different from a regional monsoon pattern. Always consider whether the question asks about local, regional, or global scale. The same process operates differently at different scales.
- **Overlooking human factors**: physical geography explains potential, but human decisions (policy, technology, culture) determine outcomes. Don't ignore the human dimension—a fault line causes earthquakes, but building codes determine whether people die.
- **Forgetting to use case studies**: DSE Geography requires specific examples. When discussing urbanisation, mention specific cities (e.g., Mumbai, Lagos) with data. Examiners award marks for concrete evidence.
- **Mixing up push and pull factors**: push factors drive people away from an area (poverty, conflict), pull factors attract them (jobs, services). Migration is caused by both working together, not just one or the other.
