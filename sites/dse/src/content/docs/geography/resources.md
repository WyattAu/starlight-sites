## Resources and Sustainability

## Key Terms

- **Water resource management:** The planning, development, and control of water supply and use to
  ensure adequate, reliable, and sustainable water availability for human needs and ecosystems.
- **Dongjiang water:** Fresh water supplied from the Dongjiang (East River) in Guangdong Province,
  mainland China, to Hong Kong under a bilateral agreement dating from 1960.
- **Country Park:** A protected area designated under the Country Parks Ordinance (Cap. 208) in Hong
  Kong, covering mountainous terrain and providing recreation, conservation, and water catchment
  functions.
- **Land premium:** Revenue collected by the Hong Kong government from the sale or lease of land,
  representing a major source of government income.
- **Land reclamation:** The process of creating new land from the sea, riverbed, or other water
  bodies by filling or draining.
- **Sustainability:** Meeting the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability
  of future generations to meet their own needs (Brundtland definition, 1987).

## Water Resource Management

### Water Supply Challenges

Hong Kong faces significant water supply constraints:

- **Limited natural freshwater:** Hong Kong has few rivers and lakes. Rainfall is seasonal, with
  approximately 80% falling between May and September, while the dry season (October to March)
  receives minimal precipitation.
- **High demand:** A population of over 7.4 million people, combined with industrial and commercial
  activity, produces daily water demand exceeding 2.7 million m$^3$.
- **Storage limitations:** Mountainous terrain restricts the area available for reservoir
  construction.

### Reservoir System

Hong Kong operates 17 reservoirs with a combined capacity of approximately 586 million m$^3$. The
major reservoirs include:

- **Plover Cove Reservoir:** Completed in 1968, created by damming a coastal inlet and pumping out
  salt water. At the time, it was the largest reservoir in Hong Kong.
- **High Island Reservoir:** Completed in 1978, also created from a former coastal inlet. Located in
  the Sai Kung area, it is the largest reservoir by capacity.
- **Tai Lam Chung Reservoir:** One of the earliest major reservoirs, completed in 1957.

The Water Supplies Department operates catchwater systems that channel rainwater from hillsides into
reservoirs. Despite these infrastructure investments, Hong Kong can only meet approximately 20-30%
of its annual water demand from local sources.

### Dongjiang Water Supply

The Dongjiang-Shenzhen water supply scheme has been Hong Kong's primary water source since 1960.
Fresh water is pumped from the Dongjiang River in Guangdong Province through a network of treatment
plants and pipelines to Hong Kong.

Key features:

- **Agreement basis:** A 1960 agreement between the Hong Kong and Guangdong governments, renewed
  and revised multiple times. The most recent agreement (2024) extends supply through 2034.
- **Volume:** Annual supply of approximately 820 million m$^3$ (with adjustments based on demand and
  availability). This represents approximately 70-80% of Hong Kong's total water consumption.
- **Treatment:** Water is treated at the Dongjiang-Shenzhen Water Supply Project Treatment Works
  before delivery, with additional treatment at Hong Kong's reception works.
- **Price:** The price of Dongjiang water is periodically renegotiated and has historically been
  influenced by political as well as economic factors.

### Water Conservation

Hong Kong has implemented several water conservation measures:

The tiered water tariff ranges from approximately HKD 4.16 per m$^3$ for the first 12 m$^3$ per
four months to HKD 9.05 per m$^3$ for consumption exceeding 31 m$^3$. Leak reduction efforts
address distribution losses historically exceeding 20% of total supply. A reverse osmosis
desalination plant at Tseung Kwan O (135,000 m$^3$ per day capacity) was commissioned in 2023.
Recycled water use for non-potable purposes remains less extensively developed than in comparison
cities such as Singapore.

## Energy Resources

### Energy Mix

Hong Kong's primary energy sources for electricity generation include:

- **Natural gas:** Accounts for approximately 40-45% of electricity generation. Supplied as LNG
  (liquefied natural gas), imported and regasified at the Black Point and Castle Peak power
  stations.
- **Coal:** Historically dominant but declining, now providing approximately 15-20% of electricity.
  Being phased out under the government's climate action plan.
- **Nuclear:** Imported electricity from the Daya Bay Nuclear Power Plant in Guangdong Province
  supplies approximately 25-30% of Hong Kong's electricity.
- **Renewables:** A small but growing share, including solar, wind, and waste-to-energy facilities.

### Daya Bay Nuclear Power Plant

The Daya Bay Nuclear Power Plant, located approximately 50 km northeast of Hong Kong in Guangdong
Province, began commercial operation in 1994. It was China's first commercial nuclear power station,
using French-designed pressurised water reactors (PWRs).

Key features:

- **Capacity:** Two units with a combined capacity of approximately 1,800 MW.
- **Ownership:** China General Nuclear Power Group (51%), CLP Power Hong Kong (25%), and formerly
  Électricité de France.
- **Supply to Hong Kong:** CLP Power purchases electricity for distribution in Kowloon and the New
  Territories.
- **Safety concerns:** Proximity to Hong Kong and Shenzhen raises public concern about nuclear
  safety.

### Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG)

Hong Kong imports LNG to fuel its power stations, with an LNG receiving terminal at Black Point
providing flexibility in sourcing beyond pipeline-delivered natural gas from mainland China.

### Energy Transition

The Hong Kong government has committed to achieving carbon neutrality before 2050, phasing out
coal-fired generation, increasing renewable and clean energy, and improving energy efficiency.
These targets align with the Paris Agreement.

## Land Resources

### Country Parks

Hong Kong's Country Parks system covers approximately 40% of the territory's total land area,
encompassing 24 country parks and 22 special areas. The system is administered under the Country
Parks Ordinance (Cap. 208).

Functions of Country Parks:

1. **Conservation:** Protecting natural habitats, biodiversity, and landscapes. Hong Kong supports
   over 3,300 species of vascular plants, 240 butterfly species, and 550 bird species, many within
   country park boundaries.
2. **Recreation:** Providing hiking trails (the 100 km MacLehose Trail, Hong Kong Trail), camping
   sites, and nature interpretation facilities.
3. **Water catchment:** Many reservoirs are located within country parks, with the surrounding
   vegetation protecting water quality.
4. **Education:** Nature centres and visitor centres provide environmental education programmes.

The Country Parks face pressures from development encroachment, illegal dumping, overuse of popular
trails, and invasive species affecting native ecosystems.

### Land Premium System

Hong Kong's government revenue model has historically been heavily dependent on land premium income.
Under the colonial leasehold system, all land belongs to the government, which grants leases to
private users. Revenue is generated through:

- **Land sales:** Government land is sold by public auction or tender to the highest bidder.
- **Lease modifications:** Premium is charged when existing leases are modified to permit higher
  density or changed land use.
- **Short-term waivers:** Fees for temporary use of government land.

Land premium can account for 10-30% of government revenue in strong property markets, creating
fiscal vulnerability during market downturns and conflicts of interest regarding housing
affordability.

### Reclamation Debates

Land reclamation has been a major strategy for expanding Hong Kong's developable land area, with
approximately 70 km$^2$ reclaimed since the mid-nineteenth century (roughly 6% of total land area).
Notable projects include Chek Lap Kok (Hong Kong International Airport, approximately 12 km$^2$),
the West Kowloon Cultural District (approximately 40 hectares), and the Kwai Tsing Container
Terminals.

Reclamation controversies centre on environmental impact (destruction of marine habitats including the
Mai Po Marshes Ramsar site), escalating costs as easy sites are exhausted, and Section 120 of the
Hong Kong Basic Law, which requires the government to protect Victoria Harbour's natural environment,
effectively limiting further reclamation.

The government's 2018 Lantau Tomorrow Vision proposed creating artificial islands near Kau Yi Chau
for housing development, but the project has faced significant public opposition on environmental
and cost grounds.

## Sustainability Framework

Hong Kong's sustainable development challenges include transitioning from fossil fuels to cleaner
energy while maintaining grid reliability, diversifying water supply sources and reducing per capita
consumption, balancing housing needs with environmental protection, and managing waste (approximately
11,000 tonnes of municipal solid waste daily with a recycling rate below 30%) within limited
landfill capacity. The government's Sustainable Development Council has adopted indicators across
economic, social, and environmental dimensions, though critics argue progress has been limited by
the dominance of economic growth objectives.

## Intuition

Resources are not just things in the ground — they are relationships between nature, technology, and human need. A mineral is not a resource until someone figures out how to extract it and someone else wants to buy it. South China's development was fuelled by resources that were extracted, processed, and consumed in a global chain. Sustainability is the art of using resources without consuming the future — like eating the interest without touching the principal. The tension is real: developing nations need resources to grow, but over-extraction degrades the environment that supports everyone.

## Exam Tips

1. **Quantify everything:** Use specific figures for water supply volumes, reservoir capacities, energy
   percentages, and country park areas. Examiners reward precision.

2. **Present balanced arguments:** For reclamation and land premium topics, discuss both sides.
   Reclamation provides developable land but destroys marine ecosystems. Land premium funds public
   services but contributes to housing unaffordability.

3. **Link resource management to population:** Connect water demand to population size and growth.
   Connect land supply to housing demand. Connect energy demand to economic structure and living
   standards.

4. **Evaluate policy effectiveness:** When discussing water conservation or energy transition,
   assess whether targets are being met, what barriers exist, and what additional measures could be
   taken.

5. **Use case studies:** Reference specific projects (Tseung Kwan O desalination plant, Daya Bay
   Nuclear Power Plant, Plover Cove Reservoir) with dates, capacities, and outcomes.

6. **Consider trade-offs:** Resource management inherently involves trade-offs. Acknowledge that
   meeting one objective (e.g., housing supply) may conflict with another (e.g., environmental
   conservation). The examiner expects nuanced analysis.

## Common Mistakes

**Confusing water supply with water self-sufficiency:** Hong Kong can only meet 20-30% of water demand locally, but it's water secure because of Dongjiang supply. Don't assume dependence on imports equals insecurity.

**Assuming Country Parks are only for conservation:** They also provide recreation, water catchment, and education functions. Don't describe them as purely environmental protection areas.

**Mixing up land premium with rates:** Land premium is from selling/leasing land. Rates are annual property taxes. They're different revenue sources with different economic implications.
