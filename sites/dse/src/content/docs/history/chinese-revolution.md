## Fall of the Qing Dynasty

The Qing Dynasty collapsed in 1911 after two and a half centuries of rule, ending over two thousand years of imperial governance in China. The dynasty's decline resulted from a combination of internal decay and external pressures.

**Key Terms:**

- **Taiping Rebellion (1850-1864):** A massive civil war that claimed an estimated 20-30 million lives and exposed the weakness of Qing authority.
- **Self-Strengthening Movement (1861-1895):** An attempt to modernise China's military and industry while preserving Confucian values.
- **Hundred Days' Reform (1898):** A brief programme of modernisation that was suppressed by conservative forces led by Empress Dowager Cixi.

The Qing Dynasty faced a series of crises in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Military defeats in the Opium Wars, the Sino-Japanese War (1894-1895), and the Boxer Rebellion (1900) exposed China's weakness and led to territorial concessions and economic exploitation by foreign powers.

Sun Yat-sen's revolutionary movement, supported by overseas Chinese communities and elements of the modernised New Army, launched the Wuchang Uprising on 10 October 1911. The uprising triggered a chain of provincial declarations of independence from the Qing government. Sun Yat-sen was provisional president of the newly established Republic of China, though he soon yielded power to Yuan Shikai, a military strongman who had brokered the abdication of the last emperor, Puyi.

## The Warlord Era (1916-1928)

Yuan Shikai's death in 1916 left a power vacuum that was filled by regional military commanders who controlled their own territories. The warlord era was characterised by shifting alliances, endemic warfare, and the fragmentation of central authority.

**Source Analysis:** Warlord-era propaganda from both Nationalist and Communist sources should be evaluated critically. These documents were produced within partisan frameworks and may exaggerate the achievements of one faction while vilifying opponents. Cross-reference propaganda claims with independent reporting and demographic data.

The May Fourth Movement of 1919, triggered by the Treaty of Versailles's transfer of German concessions in Shandong to Japan, represented a broader cultural and political awakening. Intellectuals called for science, democracy, and the rejection of traditional Confucian values. The movement also generated radical political currents that would eventually benefit both the Nationalist Party (KMT) and the Chinese Communist Party (CCP).

## Rise of the CCP and KMT

The Kuomintang (KMT) and the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) emerged as the two dominant political forces in twentieth-century China, though their fortunes fluctuated dramatically over the following decades.

Sun Yat-sen founded the KMT on the principles of nationalism, democracy, and people's livelihood (the Three Principles of the People). After repeated failures to establish a unified government, Sun accepted Soviet assistance in 1923 and agreed to cooperate with the nascent CCP. The First United Front (1924-1927) saw KMT and CCP members working together under Soviet guidance.

Chiang Kai-shek succeeded Sun Yat-sen as KMT leader in 1925 and launched the Northern Expedition (1926-1928) to reunify China. The expedition achieved nominal unification but was accompanied by the Shanghai Massacre of April 1927, in which Chiang turned on his Communist allies, killing thousands. This event ended the First United Front and initiated a decade of civil conflict between the KMT and CCP.

## The Long March (1934-1935)

The Long March was a strategic retreat undertaken by the CCP to escape KMT encirclement campaigns. Approximately 80,000 CCP troops and supporters began the march in October 1934, though only around 8,000 completed the full journey to Yan'an in Shaanxi Province.

**Key Terms:**

- **Encirclement Campaigns:** KMT military operations designed to isolate and destroy CCP base areas.
- **Zunyi Conference (January 1935):** A pivotal meeting during the Long March that established Mao Zedong's leadership of the CCP.
- **Yan'an Period (1936-1947):** The period during which the CCP was based in Yan'an, developing its organisational structure and political programmes.

The Long March established Mao Zedong as the undisputed leader of the CCP. The march also served as a powerful propaganda narrative, demonstrating the resilience and commitment of the Communist movement. The experience of the march created a cadre of experienced revolutionary leaders who would govern the People's Republic of China after 1949.

## The Nanjing Decade (1927-1937)

The Nanjing Decade represented the period of KMT rule under Chiang Kai-shek, characterised by efforts at economic modernisation, infrastructure development, and the suppression of Communist opposition.

**Source Analysis:** Statistics from the Nanjing Decade regarding economic growth, literacy rates, and infrastructure development should be treated with caution. The KMT government had limited control over significant portions of China, and data collection was inconsistent. Use multiple sources to evaluate the scale and distribution of modernisation achievements.

The government pursued fiscal reforms, including the unification of the currency and the establishment of a central banking system. Infrastructure development included the construction of railways, roads, and telegraph networks. However, modernisation was unevenly distributed, favouring urban centres and coastal regions while rural areas remained impoverished.

The Nanjing Decade ended abruptly with the Xi'an Incident of December 1936, in which Chiang Kai-shek was kidnapped by a warlord and pressured into agreeing to a Second United Front with the CCP to resist Japanese aggression.

## The Second United Front and Chinese Civil War

The KMT bore the brunt of conventional warfare against Japan, while the CCP expanded its influence through guerrilla operations and political organisation in rural areas.

The Chinese Civil War resumed in earnest after Japan's surrender in August 1945. Despite initial KMT advantages in numbers and American support, the CCP achieved a series of decisive victories. The Liaoshen Campaign (September-November 1948), the Huaihai Campaign (November 1948-January 1949), and the Pingjin Campaign (November 1948-January 1949) destroyed the KMT's best armies.

**Key Terms:**

- **Land reform:** CCP redistribution of land from landlords to peasants, which secured rural support.
- **People's Liberation Army (PLA):** The military force of the CCP.
- **Chiang Kai-shek's retreat to Taiwan:** The KMT government relocated to Taiwan after 1949, maintaining the Republic of China government.

## Establishment of the PRC and Early Policies

Mao Zedong proclaimed the establishment of the People's Republic of China on 1 October 1949. The new government implemented radical transformations of Chinese society and economy.

**Land Reform (1950-1953):** The CCP redistributed land from landlords to peasants, accompanied by public trials and executions of landlords. Estimates of deaths during land reform range from one to four million.

**Great Leap Forward (1958-1962):** Mao's campaign to rapidly industrialise China through collectivisation and backyard industry. The programme resulted in the Great Chinese Famine, with estimates of deaths ranging from 15 to 55 million, making it one of the deadliest man-made disasters in history.

**Cultural Revolution (1966-1976):** A decade of political upheaval in which Mao sought to reassert his authority and purge perceived enemies. The Red Guards persecuted intellectuals, destroyed cultural artefacts, and disrupted education and economic production. Estimates of deaths during the Cultural Revolution range from 500,000 to two million.

## Exam Tips

- When analysing the causes of the Qing Dynasty's fall, distinguish between long-term structural weaknesses (military defeats, economic exploitation, social unrest) and immediate triggers (Wuchang Uprising, provincial declarations of independence).
- For source-based questions on revolutionary movements, evaluate the purpose and audience of the source. Propaganda, official histories, and personal accounts serve different functions and reflect different perspectives.
- In essay questions, avoid presenting the Chinese Revolution as a simple narrative of Communist victory. Analyse the role of contingency, strategy, and external factors (Japanese invasion, American support) in shaping outcomes.
- Use specific evidence including dates, casualty figures, policy details, and historiographical interpretations to support arguments.
- When evaluating Mao's legacy, present a balanced assessment that acknowledges both achievements (national unification, literacy improvements) and catastrophic failures (Great Leap Forward, Cultural Revolution).

## Intuition

The Chinese Revolution was a decades-long upheaval that transformed one of the world's oldest civilizations. Think of it as a river that changed course multiple times: initially flowing toward Western-style reform, then diverting toward radical Maoism, and finally settling into a pragmatic socialist-market hybrid. The revolution succeeded not because of a single brilliant strategy but because the Communist Party adapted more effectively than its rivals to the conditions of rural China, the way a plant survives by growing toward whatever light is available.

## Common Mistakes

**Assuming the CCP won because of ideology alone:** The CCP succeeded because of practical advantages: land reform won peasant support, guerrilla tactics exploited rural areas, and KMT corruption alienated the population. Ideology mattered less thanorganisation and strategy.

**Confusing the Long March with a military victory:** It was a strategic retreat that nearly destroyed the CCP. Its significance is mythological (demonstrating resilience) rather than military. Only 8,000 of 80,000 survived.

**Assuming the Chinese Civil War ended in 1949:** Fighting continued in remote areas into the early 1950s. The PRC was proclaimed in October 1949, but consolidation of control took years.
