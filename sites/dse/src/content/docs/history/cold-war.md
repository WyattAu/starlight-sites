## Origins of the Cold War

The Cold War emerged from the ideological incompatibility between the United States and the Soviet Union and their competing visions for the post-war international order. The alliance between the two powers during World War II was always one of convenience against a common enemy, and fundamental disagreements over the future of Europe became apparent before the war's end.

**Key Terms:**

- **Iron Curtain:** Winston Churchill's 1946 metaphor describing the boundary between Soviet-controlled Eastern Europe and the democratic West.
- **Containment:** The American policy of preventing the spread of Soviet influence, articulated by George Kennan in 1946-1947.
- **Truman Doctrine (1947):** American commitment to support free peoples resisting subjugation by armed minorities or outside pressures.

The immediate post-war period saw rapid escalation of tensions. The Soviet Union established satellite states across Eastern Europe, violating wartime agreements regarding free elections. The Berlin Blockade (1948-1949), the establishment of NATO (1949), and the formation of the Warsaw Pact (1955) formalised the division of Europe into rival military blocs.

## Containment and Proxy Wars

The doctrine of containment shaped American foreign policy for four decades. Rather than direct military confrontation, the superpowers engaged in proxy conflicts across the developing world.

**Key Terms:**

- **Proxy war:** A conflict in which the major powers support opposing sides without direct military engagement.
- **Domino Theory:** The belief that the fall of one country to communism would trigger the fall of neighbouring states.
- **Brinkmanship:** The practice of pushing dangerous situations to the edge of conflict to achieve political objectives.

The Korean War (1950-1953) represented the first major proxy conflict. North Korean forces, backed by the Soviet Union and China, invaded South Korea on 25 June 1950. The United Nations, led by the United States, intervened to defend South Korea. The war ended in an armistice that restored the pre-war boundary at the 38th parallel, but no peace treaty was signed. The conflict resulted in approximately three million casualties, predominantly civilians.

The Vietnam War (1955-1975) was the longest and most divisive of the Cold War proxy conflicts. The United States supported South Vietnam against the Communist North Vietnamese government and the Viet Cong insurgency. American military involvement escalated from advisory roles in the early 1960s to over 500,000 combat troops by 1968. The Tet Offensive of 1968, while a military defeat for the North Vietnamese, turned American public opinion against the war. The Paris Peace Accords of 1973 led to American withdrawal, and Saigon fell to North Vietnamese forces on 30 April 1975.

## The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)

The Cuban Missile Crisis brought the world closest to nuclear war. The Soviet Union deployed nuclear missiles in Cuba, approximately 90 miles from the United States. President John F. Kennedy imposed a naval blockade on Cuba and demanded the removal of the missiles. After thirteen days of intense negotiation, the crisis was resolved when the Soviets agreed to withdraw the missiles in exchange for a American pledge not to invade Cuba and a secret agreement to remove American missiles from Turkey.

**Source Analysis:** When examining documents from the Cuban Missile Crisis, consider the context of nuclear deterrence. Official communications between Kennedy and Khrushchev were carefully crafted for diplomatic purposes and may conceal the true deliberations of each government. Intelligence assessments from the period may contain errors or reflect institutional biases.

## The Sino-Soviet Split

The Sino-Soviet split represented a fundamental realignment of the Communist bloc during the 1960s. Initially close allies, China and the Soviet Union diverged over ideological questions, national interests, and leadership of the international Communist movement.

**Key Terms:**

- **De-Stalinisation:** Nikita Khrushchev's 1956 denunciation of Stalin's cult of personality, which the Chinese leadership regarded as revisionist.
- **Great Leap Forward (1958-1962):** Mao Zedong's economic programme, which the Soviet Union criticised and refused to support with technical expertise.
- **Border conflict (1969):** Military clashes along the Ussuri River that brought the two powers to the brink of war.

The split had profound consequences for international relations. It ended the appearance of a monolithic Communist bloc and created opportunities for American diplomacy. Henry Kissinger's secret visit to Beijing in 1971 exploited the division, leading to the normalisation of Sino-American relations and the isolation of the Soviet Union.

## Impact on Hong Kong

Hong Kong's position during the Cold War was shaped by its status as a British colony on the border of Communist China. The territory served as a critical intelligence hub for Western agencies monitoring developments in mainland China.

**Refugee Waves:** The establishment of the People's Republic of China in 1949 triggered a massive influx of refugees. An estimated 750,000 to one million people crossed into Hong Kong between 1945 and 1956, dramatically increasing the population and providing the labour force for Hong Kong's industrialisation.

## Thaw and End of the Cold War

The Cold War underwent significant transformation during the 1970s and 1980s. The period of detente in the 1970s saw improved relations between the superpowers, including arms limitation agreements (SALT I and SALT II) and increased diplomatic engagement.

The election of Ronald Reagan in 1980 initially intensified Cold War tensions, with increased military spending and aggressive rhetoric. However, the ascension of Mikhail Gorbachev as Soviet leader in 1985 initiated fundamental reforms. Gorbachev's policies of glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring) undermined the foundations of Soviet power.

The Cold War ended abruptly with the fall of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989 and the subsequent dissolution of the Soviet Union on 25 December 1991. The causes of the Soviet collapse remain debated among historians. Some emphasise economic stagnation and the unsustainability of the Soviet system; others focus on the role of Gorbachev's reforms in inadvertently triggering centrifugal forces.

## Intuition

The Cold War was a staring contest between two superpowers, each pointing nuclear weapons at the other while trying not to blink. Neither side wanted direct confrontation because mutual assured destruction meant both would lose, so they fought through proxies — like chess players using other people's pieces. Hong Kong sat on the fault line between these two worlds, a capitalist outpost on the doorstep of communist China. The city became a listening post, a trading post, and an escape route — its prosperity was built partly on the tensions of the era.

## Exam Tips

- When analysing Cold War events, distinguish between the perspectives of the superpowers, regional actors, and the developing world. Different historiographical schools (orthodox, revisionist, post-revisionist) assign varying responsibility for the conflict.
- For source-based questions, evaluate the provenance of documents carefully. Cold War intelligence assessments may contain deliberate misinformation, while diplomatic communications were crafted for specific audiences.
- In essay questions, avoid presenting the Cold War as a simple binary conflict. The involvement of middle powers, the role of ideology versus national interest, and the experiences of non-aligned states all complicate the narrative.
- Use specific evidence including dates, treaty terms, casualty figures, and policy details to support arguments about the causes and consequences of Cold War conflicts.
- When discussing proxy wars, analyse the local conditions and motivations alongside the superpower involvement. Reducing conflicts like Korea or Vietnam solely to Cold War dynamics oversimplifies their complexity.

## Common Mistakes

**Treating the Cold War as purely ideological:** While ideology mattered, national interest and power politics drove most decisions. The Sino-Soviet split shows that Communist solidarity was weaker than often assumed.

**Assuming the Domino Theory was accurate:** The fall of one country to communism did not automatically cause neighbours to fall. Each country's outcome depended on local conditions, not just ideological contagion.

**Confusing containment with rollback:** Containment meant preventing further spread of communism. Rollback meant actively reversing it. The US pursued containment (except briefly under Reagan), not rollback.
