## Decolonisation After World War II

Decolonisation accelerated rapidly after 1945, fundamentally transforming the international order. The process was driven by multiple factors, including the weakening of European colonial powers during the war, the rise of nationalist movements, and the ideological pressures of the Cold War.

**Key Terms:**

- **Decolonisation:** The process by which colonial powers granted independence to their overseas territories.
- **Nationalism:** An ideology emphasising the interests and self-determination of a particular nation.
- **Non-Aligned Movement:** An organisation of states that did not align with either superpower bloc during the Cold War.

The end of World War II exposed the vulnerability of European colonial empires. Japan's conquests in Southeast Asia demonstrated that European powers could not defend their colonies, undermining the myth of European invincibility. The post-war period saw a rapid succession of independence movements across Asia and Africa.

## Indian Independence (1947)

India's independence in 1947 represented the most significant decolonisation event of the twentieth century. The Indian independence movement, led by the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League, employed a combination of non-violent resistance and political negotiation.

**Key Terms:**

- **Non-cooperation movement:** Mahatma Gandhi's strategy of civil disobedience against British rule, including boycotts of British institutions and goods.
- **Partition:** The division of British India into the independent states of India and Pakistan along religious lines.
- **Mountbatten Plan (1947):** The plan for the transfer of power from Britain to India, including the partition of the subcontinent.

**Source Analysis:** When examining sources on Indian independence, evaluate the perspective and purpose of each document. British official communications may emphasise administrative concerns and the desire for orderly transition, while Indian nationalist sources articulate political aspirations and the moral case for self-determination. Oral histories from partition survivors provide essential evidence of the human cost of partition but may be shaped by subsequent memories and narratives.

The partition of India resulted in one of the largest mass migrations in history, with an estimated 10-20 million people displaced and between 200,000 and two million killed in communal violence. The legacy of partition continues to shape relations between India and Pakistan.

## Indonesian Independence

Indonesian independence was achieved through a prolonged struggle against Dutch colonial rule. The Japanese occupation during World War II weakened Dutch authority and provided nationalist leaders with organisational experience.

**Key Events:**

- **Proclamation of independence (17 August 1945):** Sukarno and Hatta declared Indonesian independence two days after Japan's surrender.
- **Dutch military actions (1947-1949):** The Netherlands attempted to reassert colonial control through military force, triggering international condemnation.
- **Dutch recognition of Indonesian sovereignty (27 December 1949):** Under American and international pressure, the Netherlands transferred sovereignty to the United States of Indonesia.

The Indonesian struggle for independence demonstrated the importance of international opinion in the decolonisation process. The Cold War context meant that both the United States and the Soviet Union had interests in preventing the reimposition of colonial rule, providing diplomatic support for Indonesian independence.

## Vietnamese Decolonisation

Vietnam's path to independence involved prolonged conflict against French colonial rule and subsequent American intervention. Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh movement declared Vietnamese independence on 2 September 1945, drawing on both nationalist and Communist ideologies.

**Key Terms:**

- **First Indochina War (1946-1954):** The conflict between the Viet Minh and French colonial forces.
- **Battle of Dien Bien Phu (1954):** The decisive French defeat that ended French colonial rule in Indochina.
- **Geneva Accords (1954):** The agreement that divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel, pending reunification elections.

The Geneva Accords created a divided Vietnam, with Ho Chi Minh's Communist government in the North and an American-backed regime in the South. The promised reunification elections were never held, leading to the Vietnam War (1955-1975) and eventual Communist victory.

## African Decolonisation

African decolonisation proceeded rapidly in the 1960s, with seventeen nations gaining independence in 1960 alone. The process varied significantly across the continent, from relatively peaceful transfers of power to prolonged armed struggles.

**Key Events:**

- **Ghana's independence (1957):** Under Kwame Nkrumah, Ghana became the first sub-Saharan African colony to gain independence, inspiring liberation movements across the continent.
- **Algerian War of Independence (1954-1962):** A brutal conflict that resulted in over one million Algerian deaths and shaped French decolonisation policy.
- **Mau Mau Uprising (1952-1960):** A rebellion in Kenya against British colonial rule, resulting in significant casualties and the acceleration of Kenyan independence.

The legacy of colonialism in Africa included arbitrary borders that divided ethnic groups and combined rival communities, economic structures designed for resource extraction rather than domestic development, and educational and legal systems based on European models.

## Impact on Hong Kong

Hong Kong's position within the decolonisation process was unique. Unlike other British colonies, Hong Kong's future was determined not by local independence movements but by negotiations between Britain and the People's Republic of China.

**Source Analysis:** When examining documents related to Hong Kong's decolonisation, consider the position of the three key parties: the British government, the Chinese government, and the Hong Kong population. Each had different interests and priorities regarding the territory's future.

The waves of decolonisation across Asia and Africa created pressures that affected Hong Kong directly. The independence of India (1947), Malaya (1957), and Singapore (1965) altered Hong Kong's regional position and commercial relationships. The territory increasingly served as a financial and commercial hub connecting China with the global economy.

## The Sino-British Joint Declaration

The Sino-British Joint Declaration, signed on 19 December 1984, established the framework for the transfer of sovereignty over Hong Kong from Britain to China on 1 July 1997.

**Key Terms:**

- **One Country, Two Systems:** Deng Xiaoping's concept allowing Hong Kong to maintain its capitalist economic system and legal framework for fifty years after the handover.
- **Basic Law:** The constitutional document governing Hong Kong's governance after 1997.
- **Joint Liaison Group:** The body established to oversee the transition of sovereignty.

**Key Provisions:**

| Provision | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Sovereignty transfer | China to resume sovereignty over Hong Kong on 1 July 1997 |
| One Country, Two Systems | Hong Kong to maintain its capitalist system and way of life for fifty years |
| Legal system | Hong Kong's common law legal system to be preserved |
| Economic system | Hong Kong to retain its status as a free port and international financial centre |
| Immigration | China not to impose immigration controls between the mainland and Hong Kong |
| Defence and foreign affairs | China to assume responsibility for defence and foreign affairs |

The Joint Declaration represented a pragmatic solution to a complex diplomatic problem. Britain accepted Chinese sovereignty in exchange for guarantees regarding Hong Kong's autonomy and way of life. The agreement was registered with the United Nations and established international legal obligations.

## Path to 1997 Handover

The period between the signing of the Joint Declaration and the 1997 Handover was marked by significant political and social developments.

**Key Events:**

- **Basic Law drafting (1985-1990):** The process of creating Hong Kong's post-handover constitutional document, marked by debates over democratic reform.
- **Tiananmen Square protests (1989):** The crackdown in Beijing undermined confidence in the Chinese government's commitment to Hong Kong's autonomy and triggered a wave of emigration.
- **Last Governor Chris Patten's democratic reforms (1992-1997):** Controversial expansion of democratic representation in the Legislative Council, opposed by the Chinese government.

The 1997 Handover ceremony on 1 July 1997 marked the end of 156 years of British colonial rule. The transition was characterised by both ceremony and anxiety, as residents and observers assessed the implications of Chinese sovereignty.

## Exam Tips

- When analysing decolonisation, distinguish between the motivations of the colonial powers (economic costs, strategic considerations, moral pressure) and the aspirations of independence movements (self-determination, economic justice, cultural identity).
- For source-based questions on decolonisation, evaluate the perspective of different actors: colonial administrators, nationalist leaders, ordinary citizens, and international observers. Each provides different insights into the process and its consequences.
- In essay questions, avoid treating decolonisation as a single, uniform process. Compare the experiences of different colonies, noting the factors that led to peaceful transitions versus prolonged conflicts.
- Use specific evidence including dates, casualty figures, treaty terms, and policy details to support arguments about the causes and consequences of decolonisation.
- When discussing Hong Kong's path to 1997, analyse the interests and perspectives of the British government, the Chinese government, and the Hong Kong population. Each party had distinct priorities that shaped the outcome.

## Intuition

Decolonisation was the process of empires dismantling themselves, sometimes voluntarily and sometimes under pressure. Imagine a landlord who built a house, rented it to tenants, and then had to decide whether to sell it, return it, or try to keep collecting rent while the tenants changed the locks. The timing, method, and consequences of decolonisation varied enormously across regions, shaped by the strength of independence movements, the economic interests of the coloniser, and the Cold War competition between superpowers.
