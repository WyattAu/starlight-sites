## Why This Guide Exists

The DSE (Diploma of Secondary Education) is the standardised examination taken by all Hong Kong students at the end of secondary school. It determines university admissions and shapes career pathways. This hub page links every resource on this site — organised by subject, with study plans and exam strategies to help you prepare efficiently.

Every topic below links to detailed notes, practice questions, flashcards, and diagnostic quizzes. Use this page as your starting point, then drill into the subjects and topics where you need the most work.

## Recommended Study Plan

Preparing for the DSE is a marathon, not a sprint. Here is a structured approach that balances all subjects while building deep understanding.

### Phase 1: Foundation (Months 1–4)

- Start with the subject you find hardest — your brain learns best when it is fresh
- Read through all topic notes for each subject, marking areas of uncertainty
- Complete flashcards daily for active recall
- Aim for one diagnostic quiz per subject per week

### Phase 2: Consolidation (Months 5–8)

- Revisit weak areas identified by diagnostic quizzes
- Begin practice questions in exam conditions (timed, no notes)
- Cross-reference between subjects — for example, link DSE Physics mechanics to DSE Maths coordinate geometry
- Start working through past papers

### Phase 3: Exam Readiness (Months 9–12)

- Focus almost entirely on practice questions and past papers
- Analyse examiner reports to understand common marking criteria
- Do full-length timed papers under real conditions
- Use flashcards to maintain recall on topics you already know well
- Reserve the last two weeks for light revision and rest

### Daily Routine

| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| Morning | Active recall — flashcards for 20 minutes |
| Afternoon | Topic study — read notes on one new topic |
| Evening | Practice — complete 5–10 practice questions |
| Weekend | Diagnostic quiz + review weak areas |

## Tips for Maximising Your Score

1. **Use diagnostic quizzes first** — they tell you exactly where to focus your time
2. **Spaced repetition beats cramming** — review flashcards daily, not the night before
3. **Explain concepts out loud** — if you cannot explain it simply, you do not understand it
4. **Practise under exam conditions** — timed, quiet, no notes
5. **Learn from wrong answers** — the diagnostic quizzes and practice questions are most useful when you study the explanations for incorrect responses
6. **Connect topics across subjects** — DSE rewards students who can draw links between different areas of knowledge
7. **Read examiner reports** — they reveal what markers actually look for in top-scoring answers

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How should I start using this site?

Take a diagnostic quiz for each subject. This takes about 10–15 minutes per subject and gives you a clear picture of your strengths and weaknesses. Then prioritise studying the topics where you scored lowest.

### How many subjects should I study at once?

Most students manage 4–5 DSE subjects. Study no more than 3 subjects per day to maintain focus. Alternate between a science subject, a humanity, and mathematics each day.

### Are the practice questions similar to real DSE exams?

The practice questions are modelled on DSE-style problems. They test the same concepts and use similar formats, but they are not past papers. Use them alongside official HKEAA past papers for the best preparation.

### How often should I use the flashcards?

Daily. Flashcards use spaced repetition — each time you get a card right, it appears less frequently. This is the most efficient way to retain factual knowledge. Spend 15–20 minutes each morning.

### What is the difference between topic notes and practice questions?

Topic notes explain concepts, definitions, and worked examples. Practice questions test whether you can apply that knowledge independently. Study the notes first, then test yourself with the questions.

### Can I use this site alongside my school revision?

Absolutely. These notes are designed to complement classroom teaching, not replace it. Use them to fill gaps in your understanding, review topics before exams, and practise applying knowledge to new problems.
