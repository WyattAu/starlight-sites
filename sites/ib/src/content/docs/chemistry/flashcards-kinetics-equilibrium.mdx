### Key Concepts

**Rate Equation**: Rate = k[A]ⁿ[B]ᵐ, where k is the rate constant, and n and m are the orders with respect to each reactant. Orders are determined experimentally, not from stoichiometric coefficients. The overall order is n + m.

**Arrhenius Equation**: k = Ae^(−Ea/RT), where A is the pre-exponential factor, Ea is the activation energy, R is the gas constant, and T is temperature in Kelvin. A plot of ln k vs 1/T gives a straight line with gradient −Ea/R.

**Equilibrium Constant (Kc)**: For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ. Kc > 1 favours products; Kc < 1 favours reactants. Kc changes with temperature only -- concentration, pressure, and catalysts do not change its value.

**Le Chatelier's Principle**: If a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to partially counteract the change. Increasing temperature favours the endothermic direction. Increasing pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas. A catalyst speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally, reaching equilibrium faster but not changing Kc.

## Intuition

Think of kinetics as "how fast" and equilibrium as "how far." A reaction might be thermodynamically favourable (large Kc) but kinetically slow (high Ea). A catalyst is like building a tunnel through a mountain -- it lowers the energy barrier without changing the starting or ending altitude. Le Chatelier's principle is like a thermostat -- the system resists change by shifting in the direction that opposes the disturbance.

## Common Pitfalls

- Using stoichiometric coefficients as rate equation orders. Orders must be found experimentally. A common exam trap is giving balanced equations and expecting you to guess the orders.
- Forgetting that only temperature changes Kc. Changing concentration or pressure shifts the position of equilibrium but does not change Kc itself. Only temperature does that.
- Confusing Kc with Kp. Kc uses concentrations (mol/dm³); Kp uses partial pressures. For gas equilibria, Kp is often more convenient. The relationship is Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn.
