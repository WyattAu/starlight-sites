### Key Concepts

**Significant Figures**: All non-zero digits are significant. Leading zeros are not significant. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant. In calculations, the result should be reported to the same number of significant figures as the least precise measurement.

**Uncertainty**: Every measurement has uncertainty. Absolute uncertainty is ± the smallest division of the instrument. Relative uncertainty = (absolute uncertainty / measured value) × 100%. For readings from analogue scales, uncertainty is typically ± half the smallest division.

**Propagation of Uncertainties**: When adding/subtracting, add absolute uncertainties. When multiplying/dividing, add percentage uncertainties. For powers, multiply the percentage uncertainty by the power. These rules ensure the final uncertainty reflects all sources of error.

**Graphical Analysis**: A best-fit line should pass through or near most points, with roughly equal numbers above and below. The gradient gives the rate of change; the area under a curve gives the integrated quantity. Outliers should be identified but not automatically excluded without justification.

## Intuition

Think of uncertainty as the "fuzziness" of a measurement. A ruler marked in millimetres is fuzzy to ±0.5 mm; a burette marked in 0.1 mL increments is fuzzy to ±0.05 mL. When you combine measurements (like dividing mass by volume), the fuzziness accumulates -- that's propagation of uncertainty. Significant figures are the code for communicating how fuzzy your result is.

## Common Pitfalls

- Reporting too many significant figures. If your balance reads to ±0.01 g and you measure 2.34 g, claiming the result is 2.3412 g implies false precision. Match your sig figs to your measurement precision.
- Forgetting to include units in uncertainty calculations. Uncertainties must have the same units as the measured value -- you can't add a uncertainty in grams to one in millilitres.
- Using the wrong propagation rule. Addition/subtraction uses absolute uncertainties; multiplication/division uses percentage uncertainties. Mixing these up gives incorrect final uncertainties.
