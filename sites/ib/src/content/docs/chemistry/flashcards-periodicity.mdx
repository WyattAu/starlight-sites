### Key Concepts

**Period 3 Trends**: Across Period 3 (Na to Ar), atomic radius decreases, ionisation energy increases, and electronegativity increases. The metallic character decreases (Na is a metal, Cl is a non-metal). Oxide acidity increases (Na₂O is basic; Cl₂O₇ is acidic).

**Group 1 (Alkali Metals)**: Reactivity increases down the group as ionisation energy decreases (outer electron is further from nucleus and more shielded). They react vigorously with water to form hydroxides and hydrogen. Flame colours: Li = red, Na = yellow, K = lilac.

**Group 17 (Halogens)**: Reactivity decreases down the group as electronegativity decreases. They are diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂). Displacement reactions: more reactive halogens displace less reactive ones from their salts. Colour deepens down the group (pale yellow → purple).

**Transition Metals**: Characteristic properties include variable oxidation states, coloured compounds, catalytic activity, and complex ion formation. They form ions with incomplete d-subshells. Iron, copper, and zinc are the most commonly studied in IB Chemistry.

## Intuition

Think of the periodic table as a city map. Moving left to right across a period is like walking down a single street -- properties change gradually as you pass each building (element). Moving down a group is like looking at the same building in different cities -- the basic structure is similar, but things get bigger and more spread out. Group 1 metals are like eager volunteers (reactive, willing to give away electrons), while Group 17 halogens are like aggressive collectors (reactive, wanting to take electrons).

## Common Pitfalls

- Assuming all Group 1 metals react the same way. They do react similarly, but reactivity increases down the group -- lithium reacts gently with water while potassium reacts violently.
- Confusing ionisation energy trends across and down the period table. Across a period: increases (more protons, smaller radius). Down a group: decreases (more shielding, larger radius).
- Forgetting that transition metals have variable oxidation states. Iron can be Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺; copper can be Cu⁺ or Cu²⁺. The oxidation state determines the compound's formula and properties.
