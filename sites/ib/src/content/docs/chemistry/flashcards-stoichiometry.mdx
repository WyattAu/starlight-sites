### Key Concepts

**Balancing Equations**: Chemical equations must be balanced to satisfy the law of conservation of mass (atoms are neither created nor destroyed). Balance by inspection: start with the most complex molecule, balance metals first, then non-metals, then hydrogen, then oxygen. The coefficients represent the mole ratio -- they tell you how many moles of each substance are involved. Never change subscripts to balance equations -- only change coefficients.

**Mole Ratios**: The balanced equation gives the mole ratio between reactants and products. For example, in 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the ratio H₂:O₂:H₂O is 2:1:2. Use this ratio to convert between moles of different substances in calculations. The mole ratio is the key to all stoichiometric calculations -- it links the quantities of different substances in a reaction.

**Limiting Reagent**: The reactant that is completely consumed first determines the maximum amount of product. Calculate moles of each reactant, divide by the stoichiometric coefficient, and the smallest number identifies the limiting reagent. The excess reagent remains unreacted. The limiting reagent is like the bottleneck in a production line -- it determines how much product can be made, regardless of how much excess material is available.

**Percentage Yield**: Yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100%. The theoretical yield is calculated from the limiting reagent assuming 100% conversion. Yields below 100% result from side reactions, incomplete reactions, equilibrium limitations, or loss during purification. Atom economy = (desired product Mr × coefficient / total Mr of all reactants) × 100%. High atom economy means less waste -- a key principle of green chemistry.

## Intuition

Think of stoichiometry as a recipe. The balanced equation is the recipe card that tells you exactly how many of each ingredient (reactant) you need. The limiting reagent is the ingredient you run out of first -- it determines how much product you can make. The theoretical yield is the maximum you could make if everything went perfectly; the actual yield is what you actually get. Atom economy is like a waste audit -- it tells you how much of your raw material ends up in the desired product vs waste.

**Why it matters**: Stoichiometry is the foundation of all chemical calculations. It's essential for industrial chemistry (maximising yield, minimising waste), pharmaceutical synthesis (calculating doses), environmental chemistry (pollution calculations), and laboratory work (preparing solutions and reactions).

## Common Pitfalls

- Forgetting to balance the equation before calculating. An unbalanced equation gives wrong mole ratios and therefore wrong answers. Always balance first -- check that atoms of each element are equal on both sides.
- Assuming the reactant with the least moles is the limiting reagent. You must divide by the stoichiometric coefficient first. A small amount of a reactant with a large coefficient might not be limiting. Example: 2 mol A + 1 mol B -- B has fewer moles but A is used twice as fast, so A could be limiting.
- Confusing percentage yield with atom economy. Percentage yield compares actual to theoretical output (how efficient is the process?). Atom economy compares desired product to total mass of reactants (how much waste is produced?). Both are important for different reasons -- a reaction can have high yield but low atom economy.
- Forgetting that theoretical yield is based on the limiting reagent, not the excess reagent. Always identify the limiting reagent first.
