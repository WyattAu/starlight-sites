### Key Concepts

**Balancing Equations**: Chemical equations must be balanced to satisfy the law of conservation of mass. Balance by inspection: start with the most complex molecule, balance metals first, then non-metals, then hydrogen, then oxygen. The coefficients represent the mole ratio.

**Mole Ratios**: The balanced equation gives the mole ratio between reactants and products. For example, in 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the ratio H₂:O₂:H₂O is 2:1:2. Use this ratio to convert between moles of different substances in calculations.

**Limiting Reagent**: The reactant that is completely consumed first determines the maximum amount of product. Calculate moles of each reactant, divide by the stoichiometric coefficient, and the smallest number identifies the limiting reagent. Excess reagent remains unreacted.

**Percentage Yield**: Yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100%. The theoretical yield is calculated from the limiting reagent. Yields below 100% result from side reactions, incomplete reactions, or loss during purification. Atom economy = (desired product mass / total reactant mass) × 100%.

## Intuition

Think of stoichiometry as a recipe. The balanced equation is the recipe card that tells you exactly how many of each ingredient (reactant) you need. The limiting reagent is the ingredient you run out of first -- it determines how much product you can make. The theoretical yield is the maximum you could make if everything went perfectly; the actual yield is what you actually get.

## Common Pitfalls

- Forgetting to balance the equation before calculating. An unbalanced equation gives wrong mole ratios and therefore wrong answers. Always balance first.
- Assuming the reactant with the least moles is the limiting reagent. You must divide by the stoichiometric coefficient first. A small amount of a reactant with a large coefficient might not be limiting.
- Confusing percentage yield with atom economy. Percentage yield compares actual to theoretical output (how efficient is the process?). Atom economy compares desired product to total mass (how much waste is produced?). Both are important for different reasons.
