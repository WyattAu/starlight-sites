### Key Concepts

**Gross Domestic Product (GDP)**: The total monetary value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period. Real GDP is adjusted for inflation; nominal GDP is not. GDP per capita (GDP ÷ population) is a common measure of living standards, but ignores income distribution.

**Inflation and Deflation**: Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level (CPI). Moderate inflation (2-3%) is normal; hyperinflation (>50% per month) is destructive. Deflation (falling prices) can cause reduced spending as consumers delay purchases, leading to recession.

**Fiscal Policy**: Government spending and taxation to influence the economy. Expansionary fiscal policy (increased spending or tax cuts) stimulates aggregate demand but may increase budget deficit and national debt. Contractionary fiscal policy (spending cuts or tax increases) reduces demand but may slow growth.

**Aggregate Demand and Supply**: AD = C + I + G + (X − M). Factors shifting AD include consumer confidence, interest rates, government policy, and exchange rates. AS is affected by input costs, productivity, and government regulation. Equilibrium output and price level are determined by AD-AS intersection.

## Intuition

Think of the macroeconomy as a giant machine with three main controls: fiscal policy (government spending/taxing), monetary policy (central bank interest rates), and supply-side policies (improving productivity). When the machine runs too hot (high inflation), you cool it down with contractionary policies. When it runs too cold (recession), you warm it up with expansionary policies. The challenge is finding the right balance -- too much cooling causes unemployment; too much heating causes inflation.

## Common Pitfalls

- Confusing GDP with GDP per capita. GDP measures total output; GDP per capita measures average output per person. A country can have high GDP but low GDP per capita if it has a large population.
- Assuming fiscal expansion is always good. Increased government spending stimulates the economy but also increases the budget deficit and national debt. The multiplier effect can amplify both benefits and costs.
- Forgetting the difference between demand-pull and cost-push inflation. Demand-pull = too much spending chasing too few goods. Cost-push = rising production costs push up prices. They require different policy responses.
