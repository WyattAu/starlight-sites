### Key Concepts

**Demand and Supply**: The demand curve slopes downward (higher price → lower quantity demanded). The supply curve slopes upward (higher price → higher quantity supplied). Market equilibrium occurs where the curves intersect. Shifts in curves (due to non-price factors) change equilibrium price and quantity.

**Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)**: PED = %ΔQd / %ΔP. If |PED| > 1, demand is elastic (sensitive to price). If |PED| < 1, demand is inelastic (insensitive). Determinants include availability of substitutes, necessity vs luxury, proportion of income, and time horizon.

**Market Structures**: Perfect competition (many firms, identical products, free entry), monopoly (one firm, unique product, barriers to entry), monopolistic competition (many firms, differentiated products), and oligopoly (few large firms, interdependent). Each has different implications for price, output, and efficiency.

**Market Failure**: Occurs when the free market doesn't allocate resources efficiently. Externalities (costs/benefits affecting third parties), public goods (non-excludable, non-rival), information asymmetry, and merit/demerit goods all cause market failure. Government intervention aims to correct these failures.

## Intuition

Think of the market as a negotiation between buyers and sellers. Price is the mediator -- it rises until sellers are willing to supply exactly what buyers demand (equilibrium). Elasticity measures how stubborn each side is -- elastic demand means buyers will walk away if price rises; inelastic means they'll pay almost anything. Market failure is when the negotiation goes wrong and the outcome isn't the best for society.

## Common Pitfalls

- Confusing movement along a demand curve with a shift of the curve. A price change causes movement along the curve. A change in income, tastes, or other non-price factors shifts the entire curve. Mixing these up is the most common microeconomics error.
- Forgetting that PED is usually negative (inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded). IB exams accept either the absolute value or the negative sign, but be consistent.
- Assuming government intervention always improves outcomes. Indirect taxes can cause deadweight loss; subsidies can create moral hazard; price controls can cause shortages or surpluses. Intervention has trade-offs.
