### Key Concepts

**Figurative Language**: Metaphor compares two things directly ("the world is a stage") -- it states that one thing IS another, creating a deeper connection than a simple comparison. Simile compares using "like" or "as" ("brave as a lion") -- it acknowledges the comparison rather than equating the two things. Personification gives human qualities to non-human things ("the wind whispered"), creating vivid imagery and emotional resonance. Hyperbole uses exaggeration for effect ("I've told you a million times"), emphasising the intensity of an experience. Irony says the opposite of what is meant, often for humorous or critical effect.

**Sound Devices**: Alliteration repeats initial consonant sounds ("Peter Piper picked"), creating rhythm and emphasis. Assonance repeats vowel sounds ("go slow"), creating internal rhyme and musicality. Consonance repeats consonant sounds at the end of words ("luck and lark"), creating subtle sound patterns. Onomatopoeia words imitate sounds ("buzz", "crash"), creating sensory immersion. Internal rhyme occurs within a line, while end rhyme occurs at line endings. Sibilance specifically repeats s/sh sounds, creating a hissing or whispering effect.

**Structural Terms**: Stanza is a group of lines (like a paragraph in prose), creating visual and rhythmic units. Enjambment continues a sentence across line breaks, creating a sense of flow or urgency. Caesura is a pause within a line (often marked by punctuation), creating emphasis or a shift in tone. Refrain is a repeated line or stanza, often at the end of a verse, reinforcing the poem's theme. Volta is a "turn" in the poem, in most cases at the end of a sonnet, where the argument or tone shifts.

**Imagery**: Language that appeals to the senses -- visual (sight), auditory (sound), tactile (touch), olfactory (smell), gustatory (taste), and kinesthetic (movement). Effective imagery creates vivid mental pictures and emotional responses in the reader. Multiple senses can be engaged simultaneously (synesthesia) for a richer effect. Imagery is the primary tool poets use to make abstract ideas concrete and emotionally resonant.

## Intuition

Think of poetry as music made with words. Sound devices are the instruments -- alliteration is the drumbeat, assonance is the melody, consonance is the bass line. Figurative language is the paint -- metaphors and similes add colour and depth. Structure is the rhythm and timing -- stanzas are the verses, enjambment creates flow, caesura creates pauses. Together, they create a multi-sensory experience. The best poems work on multiple levels simultaneously -- the sound reinforces the meaning, and the structure mirrors the content.

**Why it matters**: Understanding poetry terminology is essential for IB English Paper 1 (unseen commentary) and Paper 2 (comparative essay). These terms provide the vocabulary for analysing how poets create meaning and effect. Without this technical language, your analysis will lack precision and depth.

## Common Pitfalls

- Confusing metaphor and simile. Both make comparisons, but similes use "like" or "as" while metaphors state something IS something else. "Her eyes are diamonds" (metaphor) vs "Her eyes sparkle like diamonds" (simile). Metaphors are generally considered more powerful because they equate rather than compare.
- Assuming alliteration only matters at the start of words. It's the repetition of initial consonant SOUNDS, not just letters. "Unique unicorn" is not alliteration (different initial sounds); "cat kite" is alliteration (same initial /k/ sound). The key is sound, not spelling.
- Forgetting to link poetic techniques to effect. IB exams require you to explain WHY a technique is used, not just identify it. Always ask: what does this technique show, suggest, or emphasise? How does it contribute to the poem's meaning or mood?
- Confusing enjambment with caesura. Enjambment continues a sentence across line breaks (no pause); caesura creates a pause within a line (often with punctuation). They have opposite effects -- enjambment creates flow, caesura creates interruption.
