# IB Language and Literature Course Overview

IB English A: Language and Literature is a two-year course that examines the relationship between language and literature across a range of text types. It differs from English A: Literature in its inclusion of non-literary texts and its emphasis on how language constructs meaning in all forms of communication. This page provides a comprehensive overview of the course structure, assessment components, and strategies for success.

## 1. Course Structure

The course is organised into four parts, each addressing a different aspect of the relationship between language and literature:

### 1.1 Part 1: Language in Cultural Context

Students study how language use is shaped by cultural, historical, and social contexts. Text types include speeches, editorials, advertisements, and visual texts. The emphasis is on how writers and speakers use linguistic choices to position audiences and construct meaning.

### 1.2 Part 2: Language and Mass Communication

Students examine how mass media -- newspapers, television, digital platforms -- use language to inform, persuade, and entertain. The focus is on the conventions of media texts and the ways in which they construct representations of individuals, events, and issues.

### 1.3 Part 3: Literature in Translation (HL) / Literature (SL)

Students study literary texts -- novels, poetry, drama, short fiction -- with attention to their formal features, cultural contexts, and thematic concerns. This part provides the literary foundation that connects the Language and Literature course to the broader tradition of literary study.

### 1.4 Part 4: Literature in Context (HL only)

Students study a body of literary works connected by a specific theme, genre, or period. This part develops the comparative and contextual analysis skills required for the HL Essay.

## Intuition

**Language and literature are like two sides of the same coin — one studies how language works, the other studies what language creates:** Understanding how language shapes meaning is essential for analyzing texts and crafting effective communication

**Why it matters:** Language and literature studies develop critical thinking about media, rhetoric, and cultural communication

## 2. Assessment Components

### 2.1 Paper 1: Textual Analysis

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Duration | SL: 75 minutes; HL: 135 minutes |
| Texts | SL: one unseen text; HL: two unseen texts (comparative) |
| Weighting | 25% of final grade |
| Marking criteria | Knowledge, understanding, and interpretation; Analysis and evaluation; Focus and organisation; Language |

Paper 1 presents students with unseen texts -- literary or non-literary -- and requires a guided analytical essay in response to a guiding question. At SL, students analyse one text; at HL, students analyse two texts comparatively.

#### Text Types

The following text types may appear on Paper 1:

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| Literary | Poetry, prose fiction, drama excerpts |
| Non-literary | Speeches, editorials, advertisements, letters, blogs |
| Visual | Photographs, posters, infographics, advertisements |

#### Guiding Question Types

| Type | Example |
|------|---------|
| Authorial choices | "How does the author use language to convey the speaker's attitude?" |
| Structural analysis | "Analyse the ways in which the structure of this passage shapes its meaning." |
| Audience and purpose | "Explore how this text positions its intended reader." |
| Comparative (HL only) | "Compare and contrast how the two texts present the relationship between the individual and society." |

#### Key Requirements

- **Response to the guiding question**: Every paragraph must address the question directly.
- **Integration of quotations**: Evidence must be embedded smoothly within the analytical argument.
- **Literary and linguistic terminology**: Precise use of terms (metaphor, irony, semantic field, register) is expected.
- **No personal opinion**: The response must be analytical, not evaluative or personal.

### 2.2 Paper 2: Comparative Essay

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Duration | SL: 75 minutes; HL: 90 minutes |
| Texts | One question from a choice, relating to the works studied in the course |
| Weighting | 35% of final grade |
| Marking criteria | Knowledge, understanding, and interpretation; Analysis and evaluation; Focus and organisation; Language |

Paper 2 requires students to write a comparative essay on two or more works studied during the course. The question is general enough to allow multiple approaches but specific enough to require engagement with the particular texts.

#### Key Requirements

- **Comparative structure**: The essay must compare texts, not discuss them in isolation.
- **Textual evidence**: Arguments must be supported by specific, accurately quoted evidence.
- **Contextual awareness**: The response should demonstrate knowledge of the cultural and historical contexts of the texts.
- **Thematic or formal focus**: The essay should be organised around a clear thematic or formal argument.

### 2.3 HL Essay

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Length | 1,200--1,500 words |
| Weighting | 20% of final grade (HL only) |
| Marking criteria | Focus and organisation; Knowledge, understanding, and interpretation; Analysis and evaluation; Language |

The HL Essay is an independent, formal essay on a question or statement of the student's choice. It must relate to one of the four parts of the course and must be based on material studied during the course.

#### Key Requirements

- **Original question**: The student must formulate their own research question.
- **Sustained argument**: The essay must develop a single, coherent argument over 1,200--1,500 words.
- **Secondary research**: Students may, but are not required to, incorporate secondary sources.
- **Formal register**: The essay must be written in a formal, academic register.

### 2.4 Individual Oral (IOC)

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Duration | SL: 15 minutes (10-minute preparation, 5-minute oral); HL: 20 minutes (20-minute preparation, 10-minute oral) |
| Text | One passage from a literary work studied in Part 3 or 4 |
| Weighting | SL: 30% of final grade; HL: 20% of final grade |
| Marking criteria | Knowledge, understanding, and interpretation; Analysis and evaluation; Focus and organisation; Language |

The IOC is a prepared oral commentary on a passage from a literary text studied during the course. The passage is selected by the teacher from a selection provided by the IB.

#### Key Requirements

- **Close reading**: The commentary must demonstrate sustained, detailed attention to the passage's language and structure.
- **Contextual knowledge**: The commentary must connect the passage to the text as a whole and to its broader context.
- **Coherent structure**: The commentary must be organised around a clear argument, not presented as a list of observations.
- **Spoken register**: The commentary must be delivered in a formal, analytical register appropriate to an academic presentation.

### 2.5 Assessment Weightings Summary

| Component | SL Weighting | HL Weighting |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Paper 1 | 25% | 25% |
| Paper 2 | 35% | 35% |
| HL Essay | -- | 20% |
| IOC | 30% | 20% |

## 3. Key Differences from English A: Literature

Students choosing between Language and Literature and Literature should understand the fundamental differences between the two courses:

| Feature | Language and Literature | Literature |
|---------|------------------------|------------|
| Text types | Literary and non-literary | Literary only |
| Part 1 focus | Language in cultural context | Works in translation |
| Part 2 focus | Language and mass communication | Detailed study |
| Paper 1 texts | May include non-literary and visual texts | Literary texts only |
| Analytical focus | How language constructs meaning in all texts | How literary conventions produce meaning |
| HL Essay | Required (20%) | Required (20%) |
| IOC | Required (30% SL / 20% HL) | Required (35% SL / 20% HL) |

The most significant difference is the scope of text types. Language and Literature students must be able to analyse advertisements, speeches, editorials, and visual texts with the same rigour they apply to poetry and prose. This requires a broader analytical toolkit, including knowledge of media conventions, rhetorical strategies, and visual grammar.

## 4. Study Strategies

### 4.1 For Paper 1

1. **Practise with diverse text types**: Analyse newspaper editorials, advertisements, speeches, and visual texts in addition to literary passages.
2. **Build a terminology bank**: Maintain a running glossary of literary and linguistic terms, with definitions and examples.
3. **Develop a consistent essay structure**: Use a clear introduction (thesis statement), body paragraphs (topic sentence, evidence, analysis, linking), and conclusion (restatement and extension).
4. **Time yourself**: Practice completing a full essay under timed conditions (75 minutes for SL, 135 minutes for HL).
5. **Review examiner reports**: Read IB examiner reports to understand common mistakes and high-scoring strategies.

### 4.2 For Paper 2

1. **Study texts comparatively**: When reading each text, consider how it relates to other texts on the syllabus. Note points of comparison in terms of theme, technique, and context.
2. **Prepare thematic frameworks**: Develop flexible arguments that can be applied to multiple question types. For example, an argument about the construction of identity through language can be applied to a wide range of questions.
3. **Practise writing comparative essays**: Ensure that your essays compare texts throughout, not merely discuss them in parallel paragraphs.
4. **Memorise key quotations**: Maintain a bank of precisely quoted passages for each text, organised by theme and technique.

### 4.3 For the HL Essay

1. **Choose a question that interests you**: The HL Essay is an extended piece of work, and sustained interest is essential for quality.
2. **Develop a clear thesis**: The question must be specific enough to support a 1,200--1,500-word argument.
3. **Create an outline before writing**: Organise your argument into clear sections, each with a topic sentence and supporting evidence.
4. **Revise for register and coherence**: The HL Essay must maintain a formal, academic register throughout. Review drafts for coherence, transitions, and precision.

### 4.4 For the IOC

1. **Practise speaking about texts**: Develop the habit of analysing passages aloud, not merely in writing.
2. **Prepare flexible notes**: The passage is selected by the teacher, so students should prepare by revising the entire text, not merely memorising notes on specific passages.
3. **Develop a commentary structure**: Use a consistent structure -- for example, beginning with context and overview, moving through close reading of key passages, and concluding with a synthesis of the passage's significance.
4. **Record yourself**: Practise delivering the commentary and review recordings for pace, clarity, and analytical depth.

### 4.5 General Strategies

1. **Read widely and regularly**: The breadth of text types in Language and Literature demands wide reading. Read newspapers, literary journals, and critical essays regularly.
2. **Annotate actively**: Mark texts with marginal notes that identify techniques, patterns, and connections. Do not merely highlight; annotate with analytical observations.
3. **Discuss texts with peers**: Collaborative interpretation exposes assumptions and generates new readings. Participate actively in class discussions.
4. **Reflect on your progress**: Use the reflective writing framework described in The IB Learner Profile in English to track your development and set goals.
5. **Seek feedback**: Ask teachers to review drafts and provide specific, actionable feedback on analytical depth, structural coherence, and language precision.

## 5. Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

### 5.1 Identification Without Analysis

**The pitfall**: Naming a technique (metaphor, irony, alliteration) without explaining how it produces meaning.

**The solution**: For every technique identified, ask: "What effect does this have on the reader? How does it contribute to the text's overall meaning? What would be lost if the technique were removed?"

### 5.2 Summary Instead of Analysis

**The pitfall**: Retelling the content of a text rather than explaining how the text constructs its effects.

**The solution**: Limit summary to one or two sentences at the beginning of a paragraph, then move immediately to analysis of how the text produces the effects you have identified.

### 5.3 Personal Opinion as Analysis

**The pitfall**: Stating personal preferences ("I liked this passage because it was beautiful") rather than analysing the text's techniques.

**The solution**: Replace evaluative statements with analytical ones. "This passage is powerful" becomes "The passage's use of accumulation creates a sense of overwhelming pressure that mirrors the speaker's emotional state."

### 5.4 Generic Contextualisation

**The pitfall**: Introducing contextual information (historical events, author biography) without connecting it to the specific text being analysed.

**The solution**: Context should illuminate the text, not merely accompany it. Every contextual reference must be explicitly linked to a specific feature of the text under analysis.

### 5.5 Neglecting the Guiding Question

**The pitfall**: Writing a general essay about a text rather than responding to the specific guiding question.

**The solution**: Return to the guiding question at the beginning of each body paragraph. Every point must serve the argument requested by the question.

## 6. Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Anaphora** | The repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses or lines |
| **Audience** | The intended readers, viewers, or listeners of a text |
| **Connotation** | The associative or emotional meaning of a word, beyond its dictionary definition |
| **Denotation** | The literal, dictionary meaning of a word |
| **Enjambment** | The continuation of a sentence across a line break in poetry |
| **Evaluative language** | Judgemental statements (good, bad, effective) that express personal opinion rather than analytical observation |
| **Genre** | A category or type of text defined by shared conventions (e.g., news report, sonnet, novel) |
| **Guiding question** | The question on Paper 1 that directs the student's analytical focus |
| **Irony** | A disparity between appearance and reality, or between expectation and outcome |
| **Register** | The level of formality in language use, shaped by context, audience, and purpose |
| **Rhetoric** | The art of persuasive language; the strategic use of linguistic choices to influence an audience |
| **Semantic field** | A group of words related by meaning that creates a particular tone or atmosphere |
| **Tone** | The attitude of a text toward its subject or audience, conveyed through linguistic choices |

## 7. Self-Assessment Checklist

After reviewing this overview, assess your readiness for each component:

1. Can you identify the difference between literary and non-literary text types and analyse each appropriately?
2. Do you understand the guiding question types for Paper 1 and can you respond to each with a sustained argument?
3. Can you write a comparative essay that discusses texts throughout, rather than in parallel?
4. Have you selected a viable topic for the HL Essay and developed a clear research question?
5. Can you deliver a prepared oral commentary that demonstrates close reading, contextual knowledge, and coherent structure?
6. Are you familiar with the marking criteria for each component and able to self-assess against them?
7. Have you developed a consistent revision schedule that covers all four parts of the course?
8. Can you distinguish between identification of techniques and analysis of their effects?
9. Do you maintain a glossary of literary and linguistic terminology?
10. Are you engaging in regular reflective practice to track your progress?

If you answered "no" to any of these questions, refer to the relevant section above and develop a specific plan for improvement. The Language and Literature course rewards students who combine broad knowledge of text types with precise analytical skill and disciplined self-assessment.
