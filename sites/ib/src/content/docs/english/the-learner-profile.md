# The IB Learner Profile in English

The IB Learner Profile defines the attributes that the International Baccalaureate programme aims to develop in its students. In English, these attributes are not abstract ideals but practical requirements that shape how students read, write, discuss, and think. This page examines each relevant learner profile attribute in the context of English study, with specific attention to approaches to learning (ATL) skills, reflective practice, intellectual honesty, and connections to CAS (Creativity, Activity, Service).

---

## 1. The Ten Learner Profile Attributes

The IB identifies ten attributes that describe the qualities of an IB learner. While all ten are relevant to English study, certain attributes have particular resonance for the discipline. The following analysis addresses each attribute with specific reference to English.

### 1.1 Inquirers

Inquirers ask questions and pursue understanding with curiosity and independence. In English, inquiry manifests as the willingness to interrogate a text beyond its surface meaning. An inquiring reader does not accept a single interpretation as definitive; they test readings against the text, consider alternative perspectives, and recognise that meaning is constructed, not merely discovered.

**Practical application**: When analysing a poem, an inquirer asks not "What does this poem mean?" but "What are the possible meanings this poem supports, and what evidence exists for each?" This shift from seeking certainty to evaluating possibilities is the foundation of literary analysis.

### 1.2 Knowledgeable

Knowledgeable students develop understanding across disciplines and engage with issues of local and global significance. In English, knowledge encompasses both literary history and the cultural contexts in which texts are produced and received. A knowledgeable English student can situate a text within its historical moment while also recognising its continuing relevance.

**Practical application**: When studying a postcolonial text, a knowledgeable student understands the specific historical event the text addresses (for example, the Mau Mau Uprising in Walcott's "A Far Cry from Africa") while also connecting that event to broader questions about identity, language, and power.

### 1.3 Thinkers

Thinkers use critical and creative thinking skills to analyse complex problems and make reasoned decisions. In English, thinking involves the ability to evaluate arguments, identify assumptions, and construct coherent interpretations. Thinkers do not merely respond to texts; they evaluate their own responses and consider how their assumptions shape their readings.

**Practical application**: When reading a novel with an unreliable narrator, a thinker asks: "How does the narrator's unreliability shape my understanding of events? What does the author gain by presenting the story through this perspective? What alternative perspectives might the text imply?"

### 1.4 Communicators

Communicators express ideas and information confidently in more than one language and in a variety of modes. In English, communication is both the medium and the subject of study. Students must communicate their interpretations clearly, precisely, and persuasively in written and oral forms. The ability to adjust register, structure, and style for different audiences is a core competency.

**Practical application**: A Paper 1 essay requires a different register than a reflective journal entry. A knowledgeable communicator recognises these differences and adapts their writing accordingly. The analytical essay demands formal diction, integrated quotations, and sustained argument; the reflective journal allows for tentativeness, personal voice, and exploratory thinking.

### 1.5 Principled

Principled students act with integrity and honesty, with a strong sense of fairness and justice. In English, principled conduct manifests primarily as intellectual honesty: the accurate representation of sources, the proper attribution of ideas, and the refusal to claim credit for work that is not one's own. This attribute also extends to the ethical dimension of literary study -- the recognition that texts represent real human experiences and that analysis should be conducted with respect and sensitivity.

**Practical application**: When writing about a text that deals with trauma, poverty, or oppression, a principled student avoids sensationalising or trivialising the subject matter. They represent the text's content accurately, attribute interpretations to their sources, and acknowledge the limits of their own understanding.

### 1.6 Open-minded

Open-minded students critically appreciate their own cultures and personal histories, as well as the values and traditions of others. In English, open-mindedness is essential for the study of texts from diverse cultural, historical, and linguistic traditions. An open-minded reader approaches unfamiliar texts with curiosity rather than judgement, and recognises that their own cultural position shapes their interpretation.

**Practical application**: When reading a text from a culture different from one's own, an open-minded student avoids imposing their own cultural assumptions on the text. They seek to understand the text on its own terms, consult contextual material where necessary, and acknowledge the limits of their cultural perspective.

### 1.7 Caring

Caring students show empathy, compassion, and respect. In English, caring manifests as attentiveness to the human experiences represented in texts. A caring reader recognises that literature is not merely a technical exercise but a means of understanding other people's lives, fears, and aspirations. This attribute also extends to collaborative work: a caring student listens to others' interpretations with genuine interest and responds with respect.

**Practical application**: In a class discussion about a text dealing with displacement or loss, a caring student considers how their own comments might affect classmates who have personal connections to the subject matter. They distinguish between analytical detachment and emotional insensitivity.

### 1.8 Risk-takers

Risk-takers approach unfamiliar situations and uncertain circumstances with courage and forethought. In English, risk-taking involves the willingness to propose unconventional interpretations, to challenge received readings, and to expose one's thinking to critique. The tentative, exploratory nature of literary analysis requires a tolerance for uncertainty and a willingness to be wrong.

**Practical application**: When a student proposes an interpretation that differs from the consensus view, they are taking a risk. A risk-taker supports their interpretation with evidence, acknowledges alternative readings, and remains open to revision. This is distinct from mere contrarianism: the risk is intellectual, not performative.

### 1.9 Balanced

Balanced students understand the importance of balancing different aspects of their lives -- intellectual, physical, and emotional -- to achieve well-being. In English, balance manifests as the ability to hold multiple perspectives simultaneously without collapsing into relativism. A balanced reader can acknowledge that a text supports multiple interpretations without concluding that all interpretations are equally valid. Balance also involves the ability to move between close reading (attending to individual words and phrases) and distant reading (considering the text's broader context and significance).

**Practical application**: When analysing a poem, a balanced student can attend to the sonic qualities of the language (alliteration, assonance, rhythm) while simultaneously considering the poem's thematic concerns and historical context. They do not reduce the poem to a single dimension.

### 1.10 Reflective

Reflective students thoughtfully consider the world and their own ideas and experience. They work to understand their strengths and weaknesses in order to support their learning and personal development. In English, reflective practice is essential for the development of analytical skill. Students who reflect on their own reading processes -- what they noticed, what they missed, what assumptions they brought -- develop deeper understanding over time.

**Practical application**: After completing a practice essay, a reflective student reviews their work and asks: "What did I do well? Where did my analysis fall short? What assumptions did I make that I did not examine? How might I approach this text differently next time?" This metacognitive awareness is one of the most powerful tools for improvement.

## 2. Approaches to Learning (ATL) Skills for English

The ATL framework identifies five categories of skills that IB students are expected to develop across all subjects. In English, these skills have specific applications.

### 2.1 Communication Skills

| Skill | Application in English |
|-------|----------------------|
| Listening | Active listening during class discussions; attending to the oral qualities of poetry and drama |
| Speaking | Articulating interpretations clearly in class discussion and oral presentations |
| Reading | Close reading, critical reading, contextual reading |
| Writing | Analytical essays, reflective journals, creative responses, research essays |

### 2.2 Social Skills

| Skill | Application in English |
|-------|----------------------|
| Cooperation | Collaborative interpretation in group work; peer review of written work |
| Conflict resolution | Navigating disagreements about textual interpretation with respect |
| Group decision-making | Developing shared interpretations in literature circles |

### 2.3 Self-Management Skills

| Skill | Application in English |
|-------|----------------------|
| Organisation | Managing reading schedules, essay drafts, and revision timelines |
| Affective skills | Recognising and managing one's emotional responses to challenging texts |
| Reflection | Regular self-assessment of reading and writing practice |

### 2.4 Research Skills

| Skill | Application in English |
|-------|----------------------|
| Information literacy | Evaluating the reliability and relevance of secondary sources |
| Media literacy | Analysing how media texts construct meaning and position audiences |
| Ethics | Proper attribution, avoidance of plagiarism, honest representation of sources |

### 2.5 Thinking Skills

| Skill | Application in English |
|-------|----------------------|
| Critical thinking | Evaluating arguments, identifying assumptions, assessing evidence |
| Creative thinking | Generating original interpretations, exploring multiple possibilities |
| Transfer | Applying analytical skills learned in one text to new and unfamiliar texts |

## 3. Reflective Writing in English

Reflective writing is a structured form of self-assessment that helps students develop metacognitive awareness of their own learning processes. In English, reflective writing serves several purposes:

### 3.1 Purposes of Reflective Writing

1. **Self-assessment**: identifying strengths and weaknesses in reading and writing practice.
2. **Goal-setting**: establishing specific, achievable objectives for improvement.
3. **Process awareness**: understanding how one arrives at interpretations, and how those interpretations might be refined.
4. **Emotional processing**: acknowledging the emotional dimension of literary study, particularly when dealing with difficult or traumatic subject matter.

### 3.2 Structured Reflective Framework

The following framework provides a consistent structure for reflective entries:

**Observation**: What did I read, write, or discuss? What was the text about?

**Analysis**: What did I notice in the text? What techniques did the author use? How did the text affect me?

**Evaluation**: What did I do well? Where did I fall short? What assumptions did I bring?

**Planning**: What will I do differently next time? What specific skill will I practise?

### 3.3 Example Reflective Entry

> **Observation**: Today we discussed Heaney's "Digging" and I noticed the simile "snug as a gun" for the first time.
>
> **Analysis**: The comparison between the pen and a weapon suggests that writing carries a kind of force. I had previously read the poem as simply nostalgic, but this detail introduces an element of violence or threat that complicates that reading.
>
> **Evaluation**: I tend to read for overall mood rather than attending to individual words. I missed this simile in my initial reading because I was focused on the broader themes of memory and labour.
>
> **Planning**: Next time I read a poem, I will annotate every striking word or phrase before trying to identify the overall theme. I will attend to the parts before constructing a whole.

## 4. Intellectual Honesty in Literary Analysis

Intellectual honesty in English means representing texts, sources, and one's own understanding accurately and ethically. This encompasses several specific practices:

### 4.1 Accurate Quotation

When supporting an argument with evidence from a text, the student must quote accurately. This means reproducing the exact words, punctuation, and spelling of the original. Altering a quotation to fit an argument -- even inadvertently -- is a form of intellectual dishonesty. If a student needs to omit part of a quotation, they must use ellipsis marks; if they need to add a word for clarity, they must use square brackets.

### 4.2 Proper Attribution

All ideas derived from secondary sources must be attributed. This includes not only direct quotations but also paraphrased ideas, frameworks, and critical perspectives. The failure to attribute ideas -- even when the student has genuinely forgotten the source -- is plagiarism. The IB treats plagiarism as a disciplinary matter that can result in the cancellation of the student's diploma.

### 4.3 Acknowledging Uncertainty

Intellectual honesty also means acknowledging the limits of one's understanding. A student who is uncertain about an interpretation should say so, rather than presenting a tentative reading as definitive. The phrasing "This suggests..." or "One possible reading is..." is more honest -- and more analytically rigorous -- than the phrasing "This means..."

### 4.4 Distinguishing Analysis from Opinion

A common form of intellectual dishonesty in English is the presentation of personal preference as analytical judgement. The statement "I liked this poem because it was beautiful" is an opinion; the statement "The poem's use of sibilance creates a sense of unease that complicates its apparently serene imagery" is an analysis. The IB requires analysis, not opinion, and the distinction between the two must be maintained throughout all written work.

### 4.5 Avoiding Confirmation Bias

Intellectual honesty requires the student to consider evidence that contradicts their interpretation, not merely evidence that supports it. When analysing a text, a student should actively seek out moments that challenge their thesis. The ability to revise an interpretation in light of contradictory evidence is a sign of intellectual maturity, not weakness.

## 5. CAS Connections

The CAS programme requires students to engage in creative, physical, and service-oriented activities. English study connects to CAS in several ways:

### 5.1 Creativity

English study is inherently creative: the act of interpretation is an act of creative reconstruction, and the act of writing is an act of creative expression. Specific CAS-connected creative activities include:

- **Creative writing**: composing original poetry, prose, or drama in response to studied texts.
- **Dramatic performance**: staging scenes from studied plays or adapting prose for performance.
- **Critical writing**: producing analytical essays, reviews, or research papers.
- **Multimedia projects**: creating digital presentations, podcasts, or visual responses to literary texts.

### 5.2 Activity

While English study is primarily intellectual, it connects to the Activity strand through:

- **Oral performance**: reciting poetry, performing dramatic monologues, or presenting analytical arguments.
- **Collaborative projects**: working in groups to produce presentations, performances, or publications.
- **Physical engagement with text**: walking in landscapes described in studied texts, visiting locations connected to literary works.

### 5.3 Service

English connects to the Service strand through:

- **Community literacy**: tutoring younger students in reading and writing.
- **Cultural advocacy**: promoting awareness of literary traditions from underrepresented cultures.
- **Accessible communication**: translating or adapting literary texts for audiences with limited access to them.
- **Critical media literacy**: helping community members develop the analytical skills needed to evaluate media texts.

### 5.4 CAS Portfolio Integration

Students should document their English-related CAS activities in their CAS portfolio, reflecting on how these activities develop the learner profile attributes described above. A student who tutors younger readers in reading demonstrates the attributes of Caring and Communicator; a student who stages a dramatic performance demonstrates the attributes of Risk-taker and Thinker. The portfolio should include specific, honest reflection on what the student learned and how they grew through the activity.

## 6. Self-Assessment: Learner Profile Audit

Review the following statements and rate your development honestly:

| Attribute | Developing | Competent | Exemplary |
|-----------|------------|-----------|-----------|
| Inquirer | I read texts for plot rather than for meaning | I ask questions about how texts produce their effects | I generate original interpretive questions that lead to deeper analysis |
| Knowledgeable | I can summarise what a text is about | I can situate a text within its historical and cultural context | I can connect a text to broader literary and intellectual traditions |
| Thinker | I accept interpretations presented to me | I evaluate interpretations against textual evidence | I construct and defend original interpretations |
| Communicator | I can express ideas in writing | I adjust my writing for different purposes and audiences | I communicate complex ideas with precision and elegance |
| Principled | I understand the rules about plagiarism | I cite sources accurately and represent texts honestly | I acknowledge the limits of my understanding and the assumptions I bring |
| Open-minded | I read texts from my own culture | I read texts from diverse cultures with curiosity | I recognise how my cultural position shapes my interpretation |
| Caring | I respect others' interpretations | I listen to interpretations that differ from my own | I consider the ethical implications of how texts represent human experience |
| Risk-taker | I propose interpretations only when I am certain | I propose interpretations supported by evidence, even when uncertain | I revise my interpretations in light of contradictory evidence |
| Balanced | I focus on close reading or context, but not both | I can move between close reading and contextual analysis | I hold multiple perspectives simultaneously without collapsing into relativism |
| Reflective | I complete assignments without reviewing my process | I review my work to identify strengths and weaknesses | I use reflection to develop specific strategies for improvement |

Use this audit to identify areas for growth and to set goals for the remainder of the course.
