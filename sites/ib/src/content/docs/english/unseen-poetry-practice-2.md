# IB Paper 1 Unseen Poetry Practice: Sylvia Plath's "Mirror"

This page provides a detailed analysis of Sylvia Plath's "Mirror" as practice for IB English Paper 1. Published in *Crossing the Water* (1961), the poem is a compact, formally disciplined exploration of identity, ageing, and self-knowledge. Its first-person speaker -- the mirror itself -- makes it an ideal text for examining how persona, imagery, and tone interact to produce complex meaning. The analysis below is accompanied by comparison with Heaney's "Digging" and a self-assessment checklist for candidates.

---

## 1. Poem Overview

"Mirror" is divided into two stanzas of nine and five lines respectively. The first stanza presents the mirror's account of itself: its impartiality, its devotion to truth, and its relationship with the woman who gazes into it. The second stanza shifts perspective as the woman's response is filtered through the mirror's observation, culminating in the poem's striking final image. The poem is notable for its restrained tone, its precise imagery, and its use of personification to defamiliarise a mundane object.

### 1.1 Key Contextual Information

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Poet | Sylvia Plath (1932--1963) |
| Collection | *Crossing the Water* (1961) |
| Form | Free verse, two stanzas (nine and five lines) |
| Speaker | A mirror (first-person personification) |
| Subject | A woman's confrontation with ageing |

### 1.2 IB Relevance

"Mirror" is an effective unseen text for IB Paper 1 because it requires candidates to:

- **Analyse personification**: the mirror's voice must be understood as a literary device, not taken literally.
- **Track tone shifts**: the poem moves from confident assertion to something approaching pity or unease.
- **Interpret imagery**: the lake and the moon are extended metaphors that demand careful unpacking.
- **Address the ending**: the final image of the "little face" resists easy interpretation and rewards considered analysis.

## 2. Close Reading: Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis

### Stanza 1: The Mirror's Testimony

> I am silver and exact. I have no preconceptions.

The opening line establishes the mirror's voice with deceptive simplicity. The declaration "I am silver and exact" combines material composition with a claim to precision. The adjective "exact" is significant: the mirror does not say "accurate" or "truthful" -- it claims to be without interpretation, a surface that simply reflects. The phrase "no preconceptions" extends this claim, suggesting that the mirror is free from the biases and distortions that affect human perception. For an IB candidate, the immediate question is whether the mirror's self-assessment is reliable: is the poem endorsing the mirror's claim or ironising it?

> Whatever I see I swallow immediately.

The verb "swallow" is the stanza's most important word choice. Swallowing implies ingestion, consumption, and the absence of selectivity. The mirror does not choose what to reflect; it absorbs everything indiscriminately. The verb also carries connotations of submission and passivity -- the mirror does not resist or filter. Yet there is something slightly sinister in the image: swallowing suggests a kind of hunger, an appetite for visual experience that borders on the predatory. The IB candidate must consider whether "swallow" is purely descriptive or whether it introduces an unsettling undertone.

> Not the first to describe such a passage -- / I am important to her.

The dash at the end of line 4 creates a caesura that enacts a pause in the mirror's thought, as if it momentarily considers something beyond itself before returning to its subject. The phrase "I am important to her" is notable for its simplicity: the mirror states its significance without elaboration. The word "important" is deliberately ambiguous -- the mirror is important because it reflects truth, or because it is the site of the woman's obsessive self-scrutiny, or both.

The first stanza continues with the mirror's claim to provide "the opposite of truth" and its description of the woman's daily visits:

> Each morning it is her face that replaces the darkness.

The juxtaposition of "her face" and "the darkness" is striking. The darkness could be the mirror's own emptiness when unoccupied, the woman's fear of what the mirror will reveal, or the void of non-existence that ageing approaches. The verb "replaces" implies that the woman's face is an interruption of the darkness, a temporary displacement of something that will ultimately prevail. For an IB candidate, this line rewards attention because it compresses the poem's central anxiety -- the confrontation with mortality -- into a single, precisely worded image.

### Stanza 2: The Woman's Response

> She comes and goes. Each morning it is her face that replaces the darkness.

The repetition of this observation across the stanza break reinforces the ritualistic quality of the woman's behaviour. She "comes and goes" -- the phrase is neutral, almost procedural, yet it conveys the compulsive nature of her visits. The repetition also suggests the mirror's own monotony: it records the same face, the same ritual, the same anxiety, day after day.

> She rewards me with tears and an agitation of hands.

The verb "rewards" is deeply ironic. The woman's distress -- tears and agitated hands -- is framed as a gift to the mirror, as if her suffering is its just compensation. The irony operates on multiple levels: the mirror claims to be impartial, yet it takes pleasure in the woman's pain; the mirror claims to be important to the woman, yet its importance derives from her unhappiness. The IB candidate must be able to articulate this irony and explain how it complicates the mirror's stated objectivity.

> I am important to her. She could neither replace nor lose me.

The repetition of "I am important to her" across the two stanzas creates a structural echo that mirrors (the word is unavoidable) the woman's compulsive return. The phrase "could neither replace nor lose me" suggests both the mirror's indispensability and the woman's entrapment. She cannot replace the mirror because no other surface provides the same truthful reflection; she cannot lose it because she is compelled to return. The word "lose" is particularly resonant -- it implies that the mirror is something she possesses, yet the poem suggests the reverse: she belongs to the mirror.

The poem concludes with its most enigmatic image:

> In me she has drowned a young girl, and in me an old woman / Rises toward her day after day, like a terrible fish.

The extended metaphor of drowning and rising establishes a temporal sequence: the young girl the woman once was has been submerged in the mirror's reflective surface, and what rises in her place is something alien and monstrous. The simile "like a terrible fish" is deliberately incongruous -- a fish has no obvious connection to a woman's face. The incongruity is the point: the image conveys the horror of self-recognition, the sense that what ageing reveals is something unrecognisable and predatory. The adjective "terrible" carries both the sense of "frightening" and the archaic sense of "awe-inspiring" -- the old woman is both repellent and sublime.

**Examiner Commentary**: The final simile is the element that most distinguishes a top-band response. Candidates who merely note that "the fish is a metaphor for ageing" produce an inadequate answer. The strength of the image lies in its strangeness, its refusal to be easily decoded. A strong response acknowledges this strangeness and explores its effect on the reader.

## 3. Imagery and Personification

### 3.1 The Mirror as Speaker

The decision to give the mirror a first-person voice is the poem's foundational conceit. Personification allows Plath to defamiliarise the act of looking in a mirror: by speaking _as_ the mirror, she forces the reader to see the act of self-scrutiny from the outside. The mirror's voice is measured, precise, and slightly clinical -- it describes the woman's behaviour without empathy or judgement, yet the very act of description implies a relationship that contradicts the mirror's claimed detachment.

For IB candidates, the key analytical move is to identify the gap between what the mirror claims (impartiality, exactness) and what it reveals (a compulsive, almost predatory interest in the woman's distress). This gap is where the poem's meaning resides.

### 3.2 The Lake and the Moon

The mirror compares itself to a lake:

> I am silver and exact. I have no preconceptions. / .../ I am not cruel, only truthful.

And later:

> Now I am a lake. A woman bends over me, / Searching my surface for what she really is.

The shift from mirror to lake is significant. A lake is a natural body of water that reflects the world above it, yet it also has depth, darkness, and life beneath its surface. The metaphor introduces the possibility that the mirror -- or the truth it reflects -- is deeper and more complex than its surface suggests. The woman "searches" the surface, but the poem implies that what she seeks cannot be found on any surface. The lake metaphor also evokes the myth of Narcissus, who drowned while gazing at his own reflection -- an allusion that reinforces the poem's association between self-scrutiny and destruction.

The moon is described as "a god" or "an intermediate," and the lake is said to be "like a terrible fish." These images accumulate around the central metaphor of the reflective surface without forming a single, stable allegory. This multiplicity of association is deliberate: Plath resists the reduction of her imagery to a single referent, and the IB candidate must do the same.

## 4. Comparison with Heaney's "Digging"

### 4.1 Structural Differences

| Feature | Heaney's "Digging" | Plath's "Mirror" |
|---------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Structure | Circular (opens and ends with the pen) | Linear (moves from the mirror's self-description to the woman's transformation) |
| Speaker | First-person human narrator | First-person personified object |
| Tone | Nostalgic, contemplative, ultimately resolved | Detached, clinical, increasingly uneasy |
| Imagery | Tactile, sensory, grounded in physical labour | Visual, abstract, grounded in psychological anxiety |
| Central metaphor | Digging as creative act | The mirror as site of self-confrontation |

### 4.2 Shared Concerns

Despite their differences, both poems explore the relationship between an individual and a defining practice. Heaney's speaker defines himself through writing; Plath's woman defines herself through reflection. Both poems use their central metaphor to interrogate what it means to see clearly -- Heaney through the act of careful observation, Plath through the act of self-scrutiny. Both also address the anxiety of inheritance: Heaney worries about breaking with family tradition; Plath's woman confronts the biological inheritance of ageing.

### 4.3 IB Comparative Strategy

When comparing unseen poems in Paper 1 (HL), candidates should:

1. Identify the controlling metaphor in each poem.
2. Explain how each poet uses structure to enact or complicate that metaphor.
3. Compare the poets' tonal registers and explain how tone shapes the reader's response.
4. Address at least one point of divergence (the poems' different resolutions, for instance) with the same rigour as points of convergence.
5. Avoid the pitfall of discussing the poems in separate paragraphs without explicit comparison.

## 5. IB Assessment Alignment

### 5.1 Assessment Objectives

| Objective | How This Response Addresses It |
|-----------|-------------------------------|
| AO1: Understanding | Demonstrates comprehension of the mirror's personification and the poem's psychological complexity |
| AO2: Analysis | Explains how imagery (lake, fish, darkness) and irony produce meaning |
| AO3: Evaluation | Assesses the significance of the poem's unresolved ending and the gap between the mirror's claims and its behaviour |
| AO4: Communication | Presents a coherent argument with embedded quotations and precise literary terminology |

### 5.2 Marking Criteria Notes

- **Knowledge, understanding, and interpretation**: The response must demonstrate that the candidate understands the poem on both its literal and figurative levels. The mirror is not merely "a metaphor" -- it is a fully realised persona whose self-assessment must be critically evaluated.
- **Analysis and evaluation**: The response must explain how the poem's techniques produce effects, not merely identify those techniques. The analysis of "rewards" as ironic and "terrible fish" as deliberately incongruous exemplifies this requirement.
- **Focus and organisation**: The argument must be sustained throughout the response, with each paragraph contributing to a developing thesis about the poem's treatment of self-knowledge and ageing.
- **Language**: The response must use literary terminology accurately and maintain a formal register. The term "personification" should be used precisely, and the distinction between "simile" and "metaphor" must be observed.

## 6. Self-Assessment Checklist

After completing your own analysis of "Mirror," review your response against the following criteria:

1. Did you identify the poem's personification and explain its effect on the reader?
2. Did you analyse at least three specific images (e.g., "swallow," "darkness," "terrible fish") with embedded quotations?
3. Did you explain _how_ each image produces meaning, not merely _what_ the image is?
4. Did you identify the irony in the mirror's voice and explain how it complicates the poem's surface meaning?
5. Did you address the poem's ending and explain why it resists easy interpretation?
6. Did you discuss the shift from mirror to lake and explain its significance?
7. Did you use precise literary terminology (personification, simile, extended metaphor, irony) throughout?
8. Did you maintain a formal, analytical register without personal opinion or summary?
9. Did your introduction state a clear argument, and did your conclusion return to it?
10. Did you consider the poem's context where relevant, without relying on biographical speculation?

If you are preparing for HL Paper 1, also review your response against the comparison criteria in Section 4.3 above. Ensure that your comparative analysis is explicit, sustained, and balanced between the two texts.
