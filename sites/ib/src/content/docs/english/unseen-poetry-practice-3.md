## IB Paper 1 Unseen Poetry Practice: Derek Walcott's "A Far Cry from Africa"

This page provides a detailed analysis of Derek Walcott's "A Far Cry from Africa" as practice for IB English Paper 1. Published in *In a Green Night* (1962), the poem is one of the most frequently anthologised responses to the Mau Mau Uprising in Kenya (1952--1960). It is an exemplary unseen text for IB candidates because it combines formal metrical control with passionate political content, and it concludes with a question that refuses resolution. The analysis below models the standard of response expected in a top-band Paper 1 answer.

## 1. Poem Overview

The poem addresses the violence of the Mau Mau Uprising, in which the Kikuyu people of Kenya fought against British colonial rule. Walcott, a Caribbean poet of mixed African and European heritage, writes from a position of divided loyalty: he is appalled by the violence on both sides, yet he cannot separate himself from either the colonial oppressors or the anti-colonial insurgents. The poem moves from a panoramic description of the conflict to an increasingly personal and anguished reflection, culminating in a question that the speaker cannot answer.

### 1.1 Key Contextual Information

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Poet | Derek Walcott (1930--2017) |
| Collection | *In a Green Night* (1962) |
| Form | Iambic pentameter, ABAB rhyme scheme (with variations) |
| Length | Four stanzas of eight lines each |
| Subject | The Mau Mau Uprising in Kenya |
| Context | British colonialism, postcolonial identity |

### 1.2 IB Relevance

"A Far Cry from Africa" is a demanding unseen text that rewards candidates who can:

- **Analyse the tension between form and content**: the poem's disciplined meter and rhyme scheme contain and formalise chaotic, violent content.
- **Interpret postcolonial themes**: the poem raises questions about cultural identity, linguistic inheritance, and the ethics of violence.
- **Track a developing argument**: the poem moves from description to personal anguish to an unresolved question, and candidates must trace this trajectory.
- **Engage with the ending**: the poem's final question is its most important moment, and candidates who can analyse its significance demonstrate the highest level of engagement.

## 2. Close Reading: Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis

### Stanza 1: The Landscape of Violence

> A wind is ruffling the tawny pelt / Of Africa.

The opening metaphor establishes Africa as an animal -- specifically, as a creature with a "tawny pelt." The adjective "tawny" evokes both the colour of the African landscape and the fur of a lion or leopard, suggesting a continent that is wild, beautiful, and dangerous. The verb "ruffling" implies disturbance, an agitation that is both physical (wind on fur) and political (uprising against colonial rule). The metaphor of Africa as animal carries the risk of primitivism, yet Walcott deploys it with self-awareness: the metaphor is the kind of reductive image that colonial discourse has historically used to justify domination.

> Kikuyu, quick as flies, / Batten upon the bloodstreams of the veldt.

The comparison of the Kikuyu to flies is deeply unsettling. The simile "quick as flies" reduces the people to insects, yet the phrase also implies a desperate, voracious vitality -- flies survive where other creatures cannot. The verb "batten" means to feed greedily, and it carries connotations of parasitism. The image is deliberately uncomfortable: Walcott is reproducing the dehumanising rhetoric of colonial discourse in order to expose its violence. The IB candidate must recognise that the simile is not the poet's own view but a quotation or echo of colonial language, deployed critically.

> Corpses are scattered through a paradise.

The juxtaposition of "corpses" and "paradise" is the stanza's most striking contrast. The word "paradise" invokes the colonial myth of Africa as an untouched Eden, a place of natural beauty corrupted only by the violence of its inhabitants. Yet the corpses are the product of colonial violence, not indigenous savagery. The line compresses the entire contradiction of colonialism into a single image: a beautiful land destroyed by the very forces that claim to civilise it.

**Examiner Commentary**: The ability to recognise that a simile may be deployed critically -- that the poet is not endorsing the comparison but interrogating it -- is a hallmark of the highest-band responses. Candidates who identify the "flies" simile as an example of colonial rhetoric being reproduced and questioned demonstrate the kind of sophisticated reading that IB examiners reward.

### Stanza 2: The Moral Complexity

> The violence of beast on beast is read / As natural law.

Here Walcott draws a distinction between animal violence and human violence. The phrase "natural law" suggests that the violence of the animal kingdom is accepted as part of the order of things. But the next lines complicate this:

> But, upright man, / Seeks his own paradise / With the violence of man.

The phrase "upright man" is bitterly ironic: the colonial powers present themselves as morally upright, yet their violence is no less bestial than the violence they condemn. The word "paradise" returns, but now it refers not to Africa but to the colonial project of creating a civilised society -- a paradise built on exploitation and blood. The IB candidate must note the recursive structure of the argument: the stanza establishes a moral framework (animal violence is natural, human violence is not) and then dismantles it (human violence is presented as natural by those who commit it).

### Stanza 3: The Personal Turn

> I who am poisoned with the blood of both, / Where shall I turn, divided to the vein?

This is the poem's most personal moment. The speaker reveals his mixed heritage -- African and European -- and the word "poisoned" suggests that this dual inheritance is experienced not as enrichment but as contamination. The question "Where shall I turn, divided to the vein?" is not rhetorical: it expresses genuine uncertainty. The speaker cannot choose between the two sides because both are part of him. The phrase "divided to the vein" implies that the division is not merely intellectual but physiological -- it runs through the speaker's blood, his very body.

> I who have cursed / The drunken officer of British rule, how choose / Between this Africa and the English tongue I love?

The repetition of "I who" creates an anaphoric pattern that emphasises the speaker's self-interrogation. He has "cursed" the colonial officer -- he has taken a moral stand against British rule -- yet he cannot abandon the English language, which is both the instrument of colonial oppression and the medium of his literary expression. The phrase "the English tongue I love" is remarkably candid: the speaker loves the language that has been used to dominate his ancestors. This is not hypocrisy but the genuine dilemma of the postcolonial subject, and Walcott presents it without resolving it.

### Stanza 4: The Unresolved Question

> How can I face such slaughter and be cool? / How can I turn from Africa and live?

The poem's final two questions are its most important moment. They are not rhetorical -- the speaker genuinely does not know the answer. The first question addresses the moral dimension: how can anyone remain detached in the face of violence? The second addresses the personal dimension: how can the speaker abandon the continent that is part of his identity? The repetition of "How can I" creates a rhythmic insistence that underscores the speaker's desperation. The poem ends without resolution, and this irresolution is its most powerful statement: the postcolonial condition is one of permanent, unresolvable tension.

**Examiner Commentary**: The final questions are the element that most distinguishes a top-band response. Candidates who note that the poem "ends with a question" produce an inadequate answer. The strength of the ending lies in its refusal to provide an answer -- it enacts the dilemma rather than describing it. A strong response explains _why_ irresolution is more powerful than resolution in this context.

## 3. Form and Technique

### 3.1 Meter and Rhyme

The poem is written in iambic pentameter with an ABAB rhyme scheme. This is the meter of English literary tradition -- the meter of Shakespeare, Milton, and Wordsworth. The choice of form is significant: Walcott is writing about African subject matter in the language and form of the colonial power. The tension between the disciplined, traditional form and the chaotic, violent content enacts the poem's central dilemma: the speaker is trapped within a linguistic and formal inheritance that he cannot escape.

The rhyme scheme is largely regular, but there are occasional variations and slant rhymes. These variations prevent the form from becoming too neat or too comfortable -- they introduce a slight discord that mirrors the speaker's unease. IB candidates should note that the regularity of the form is itself meaningful: it represents the colonial order that contains and constrains the violence the poem describes.

### 3.2 Imagery

The poem's imagery operates on two registers simultaneously: the macroscopic (Africa as a whole, the political conflict) and the microscopic (the speaker's blood, his personal dilemma). The movement between these registers is one of the poem's most effective strategies. The opening stanza presents a panoramic view of the continent; the third stanza narrows to the speaker's divided body. This movement from the general to the personal enacts the poem's argument: political violence is experienced in the body, in the blood, in the vein.

### 3.3 Allusion

The poem alludes to several traditions and texts:

- **Biblical**: the phrase "paradise" evokes the Garden of Eden, and the fall from innocence.
- **Classical**: the iambic pentameter and ABAB rhyme scheme recall the English literary tradition from Shakespeare to Wordsworth.
- **Political**: the Mau Mau Uprising is the specific historical event, but the poem addresses the general condition of colonial violence.

IB candidates should be able to identify these allusions where they are relevant to the argument, but should avoid the trap of listing allusions without explaining their significance.

## 4. Postcolonial Themes

### 4.1 Divided Identity

The poem's central theme is the experience of cultural and linguistic division. The speaker is "poisoned with the blood of both" -- he belongs fully to neither Africa nor Europe. This divided identity is presented not as a problem to be solved but as a condition to be endured. The poem's final questions are unanswerable because the dilemma they pose is structural: it is built into the speaker's very being.

### 4.2 The English Language as Colonial Inheritance

The speaker's love of the English language is presented as both a gift and a curse. English is the medium of his literary expression, but it is also the language of the coloniser. The poem does not resolve this tension; it names it. For IB candidates, the key insight is that the poem is itself an example of the dilemma it describes: it uses English to critique English, it uses the coloniser's form to express the colonised subject's anguish.

### 4.3 Violence and Morality

The poem refuses to moralise about violence. It does not condemn the Mau Mau insurgents or exonerate the colonial regime; it presents the violence of both sides and asks the reader to confront it. The phrase "slaughter" is applied to both sides, and the speaker's question -- "How can I face such slaughter and be cool?" -- addresses his own inability to remain detached. The poem's moral position is one of anguished neutrality: the speaker cannot choose a side because both sides are guilty.

## 5. Examiner Tips for Higher Marks

### 5.1 Avoid Biographical Reduction

The most common mistake candidates make with Walcott is to reduce the poem to autobiography. While the speaker's mixed heritage reflects Walcott's own background, the poem is not a diary entry. It is a carefully constructed literary artefact that uses personal experience to address universal questions about identity, language, and violence. A strong response discusses the speaker's identity as a structural feature of the poem, not as a biographical fact.

### 5.2 Address the Ending

The poem's final questions are its most important moment. Many candidates summarise the ending without analysing it. The difference between a competent and an excellent response is the ability to explain _why_ irresolution is more powerful than resolution. The poem does not fail to find an answer; it demonstrates that no answer is possible. This is a deliberate artistic choice, and the candidate must explain its significance.

### 5.3 Analyse the Tension Between Form and Content

The poem's formal regularity (iambic pentameter, ABAB rhyme) is not merely decorative. It represents the colonial order that contains and constrains the violence the poem describes. A strong response identifies this tension and explains how it contributes to the poem's meaning. The form is not separate from the content; it is a vehicle for the content's deepest implications.

### 5.4 Use Precise Literary Terminology

IB examiners reward the accurate use of literary terms. Candidates should be able to distinguish between:

- **Simile** ("quick as flies") and **metaphor** ("the tawny pelt of Africa")
- **Irony** (the bitter irony of "upright man") and **understatement**
- **Anaphora** ("I who am poisoned," "I who have cursed") and **repetition**
- **Enjambment** (the continuation of a sentence across a line break) and **end-stopping** (the completion of a sentence at the end of a line)

The precise use of these terms demonstrates analytical rigour and contributes to the overall quality of the response.

### 5.5 Engage with the Poem's Complexity

The poem resists simple interpretation. It does not take a clear political position; it does not resolve its central dilemma; it does not offer comfort or hope. A strong response acknowledges this complexity and explores it rather than trying to reduce the poem to a single, tidy thesis. The IB rewards responses that can hold multiple possibilities in tension without forcing a premature resolution.

## 6. IB Assessment Alignment

### 6.1 Assessment Objectives

| Objective | How This Response Addresses It |
|-----------|-------------------------------|
| AO1: Understanding | Demonstrates comprehension of the poem's historical context, its speaker's divided identity, and its formal strategies |
| AO2: Analysis | Explains how imagery, irony, and form produce meaning and emotional effect |
| AO3: Evaluation | Assesses the significance of the poem's unresolved ending and its refusal to moralise |
| AO4: Communication | Presents a coherent, well-organised argument with integrated quotations and precise terminology |

### 6.2 Paper 1 Marking Criteria Alignment

- **Knowledge, understanding, and interpretation**: The response must demonstrate that the candidate understands the poem's historical and literary context, as well as its formal and figurative strategies. The analysis of the "flies" simile as colonial rhetoric being reproduced critically exemplifies this.
- **Analysis and evaluation**: The response must explain _how_ the poem's techniques produce meaning. The analysis of the tension between iambic pentameter and violent content demonstrates this.
- **Focus and organisation**: The argument must be sustained throughout, with each paragraph contributing to a developing thesis about the poem's treatment of divided identity and irresolvable tension.
- **Language**: The response must use literary terminology accurately and maintain a formal register throughout.

## 7. Self-Assessment Checklist

After completing your own analysis of "A Far Cry from Africa," review your response against the following criteria:

1. Did you identify the poem's central tension (divided identity, cultural inheritance) and explain how it is developed?
2. Did you analyse at least three specific techniques (e.g., the "flies" simile, the irony of "upright man," the anaphora of "I who") with embedded quotations?
3. Did you explain _how_ each technique produces meaning, not merely _what_ the technique is?
4. Did you address the poem's form (iambic pentameter, rhyme scheme) and explain how it contributes to meaning?
5. Did you recognise the tension between formal regularity and violent content?
6. Did you address the poem's ending and explain why irresolution is more powerful than resolution?
7. Did you discuss the postcolonial themes without reducing the poem to autobiography?
8. Did you use precise literary terminology (simile, metaphor, irony, anaphora) throughout?
9. Did you maintain a formal, analytical register without personal opinion or summary?
10. Did your introduction state a clear argument, and did your conclusion return to it?

If you answered "no" to any of these questions, revise your response to address the gap. The IB rewards responses that demonstrate both technical precision and interpretive depth -- the ability to identify _what_ a poem does and to explain _why_ it matters.
