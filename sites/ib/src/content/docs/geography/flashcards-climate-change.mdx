### Key Concepts

**Enhanced Greenhouse Effect**: Human activities (burning fossil fuels, deforestation, agriculture) increase concentrations of greenhouse gases (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O) in the atmosphere. These gases absorb and re-emit infrared radiation, trapping more heat and raising global temperatures beyond natural variability.

**Carbon Cycle Feedback Loops**: Warming can trigger positive feedback loops. For example, permafrost thawing releases stored methane, which causes further warming, which thaws more permafrost. Similarly, reduced ice cover lowers albedo, increasing solar absorption and warming.

**Mitigation vs Adaptation**: Mitigation aims to reduce the causes of climate change (e.g., renewable energy, carbon taxes, reforestation). Adaptation addresses the effects (e.g., sea walls, drought-resistant crops, managed retreat). Both strategies are needed simultaneously.

**Paris Agreement Goals**: The 2015 Paris Agreement aims to limit global warming to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, with efforts to limit it to 1.5°C. Countries submit nationally determined contributions (NDCs) outlining their emissions reduction targets.

## Intuition

Think of the greenhouse effect like a blanket around the Earth. Some blanket is good -- without it, Earth would be frozen. But adding too many layers (more greenhouse gases) makes the blanket too thick, trapping too much heat. Feedback loops are like a snowball rolling downhill -- once they start, they accelerate. The Paris Agreement is essentially the world trying to agree on how many blanket layers to keep.

## Common Pitfalls

- Confusing mitigation with adaptation. Mitigation reduces the cause (emissions); adaptation manages the effects (flooding, drought). Both are needed but address different parts of the problem.
- Thinking that the greenhouse effect is inherently bad. The natural greenhouse effect is essential for life -- it's the enhanced (human-caused) increase that's the problem.
- Assuming climate change only causes warming. It also increases the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, including cold snaps in some regions due to disrupted jet streams.
