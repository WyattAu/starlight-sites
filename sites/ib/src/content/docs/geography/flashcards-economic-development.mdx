### Key Concepts

**Development Indicators**: GDP per capita is the most common measure but has limitations -- it ignores income distribution, informal economies, environmental costs, and quality of life. HDI (Human Development Index) combines life expectancy (health), education (knowledge), and GNI per capita (standard of living) for a more holistic measure. GDI adjusts HDI for gender inequality. The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) measures deprivations in health, education, and living standards simultaneously.

**Rostow's Model** proposes five stages of economic growth: traditional society (subsistence agriculture, limited technology), preconditions for take-off (infrastructure investment, commercial agriculture), take-off (industrialisation, urbanisation), drive to maturity (diversified economy, technological innovation), and age of high mass consumption (service sector dominance, consumer society). Critics argue it is Eurocentric (assumes all countries follow the Western path), ignores the role of colonialism, and doesn't account for dependency.

**Dependency Theory** argues that developing countries remain poor because they are exploited by wealthy nations through unequal trade relationships. The core (wealthy nations) extracts resources and cheap labour from the periphery (developing nations), creating a core-periphery relationship that perpetuates underdevelopment. This explains why some countries remain poor despite having abundant resources -- the benefits flow to the core. It challenges modernisation theory by arguing that development and underdevelopment are two sides of the same coin.

**Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)**: The UN's 17 SDGs provide a framework for addressing global challenges. Key goals include no poverty (1), zero hunger (2), good health (3), quality education (4), gender equality (5), clean water (6), affordable energy (7), decent work (8), reduced inequalities (10), sustainable cities (11), responsible consumption (12), and climate action (13). The SDGs recognise that development must balance economic growth, social inclusion, and environmental protection.

## Intuition

Think of development as more than just money -- it's about health, education, and opportunity. A country might have a high GDP per capita (like an oil-rich nation) but poor education or health outcomes. HDI captures this multidimensional nature. Rostow's model is like a ladder: every country starts at the bottom and climbs up. Dependency theory argues the ladder is rigged -- the rich countries hold it in place. The SDGs are like a roadmap for climbing while keeping everyone safe and leaving the environment intact.

**Why it matters**: Understanding development is essential for addressing global inequality, poverty, and environmental degradation. It helps you evaluate aid effectiveness, trade policies, and government strategies. Development geography connects economic, social, and environmental issues in a holistic framework.

## Common Pitfalls

- Using GDP per capita as the sole measure of development. It doesn't account for income inequality (a country can have high GDP per capita but extreme poverty for most people), the informal economy (unpaid work, black market), or non-market activities (subsistence farming, care work).
- Confusing economic growth with development. Growth is an increase in GDP; development includes improvements in quality of life, education, health, and equality. A country can grow without developing if the benefits only accrue to the wealthy.
- Assuming aid always helps. Poorly targeted aid can create dependency, distort local markets, or be lost to corruption. Effective aid must be tied to capacity building, local ownership, and sustainability. Debt relief may be more effective than new aid.
- Forgetting that development is not linear. Countries can experience setbacks (conflict, natural disasters, economic crises) that reverse decades of progress. The DTM and Rostow's model suggest linear progression, but reality is more complex.
