### Key Concepts

**Development Indicators**: GDP per capita is the most common measure but has limitations -- it ignores income distribution, informal economies, and quality of life. HDI (Human Development Index) combines life expectancy, education, and income for a more holistic measure. GDI adjusts HDI for gender inequality.

**Rostow's Model** proposes five stages of economic growth: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity, and age of high mass consumption. Critics argue it is Eurocentric and assumes all countries follow the same development path.

**Dependency Theory** argues that developing countries remain poor because they are exploited by wealthy nations through unequal trade relationships (e.g., low commodity prices vs high manufactured goods prices). This creates a core-periphery relationship that perpetuates underdevelopment.

**Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)**: The UN's 17 SDGs provide a framework for addressing global challenges including poverty, hunger, health, education, gender equality, clean water, clean energy, economic growth, infrastructure, inequality, sustainable cities, responsible consumption, climate action, life below water, life on land, peace and justice, and partnerships.

## Intuition

Think of development as more than just money -- it's about health, education, and opportunity. A country might have a high GDP per capita (like an oil-rich nation) but poor education or health outcomes. HDI captures this multidimensional nature. Rostow's model is like a ladder: every country starts at the bottom and climbs up. Dependency theory argues the ladder is rigged -- the rich countries hold it in place.

## Common Pitfalls

- Using GDP per capita as the sole measure of development. It doesn't account for income inequality, the informal economy, or non-market activities like unpaid care work.
- Confusing economic growth with development. Growth is an increase in GDP; development includes improvements in quality of life, education, health, and equality.
- Assuming aid always helps. Poorly targeted aid can create dependency, distort local markets, or be lost to corruption. Effective aid must be tied to capacity building and local ownership.
