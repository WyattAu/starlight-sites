### Key Concepts

**Demographic Transition Model (DTM)** describes the shift from high birth and death rates to low rates through five stages: (1) high stationary, (2) early expanding, (3) late expanding, (4) low stationary, (5) declining. Stage 5 features negative natural increase in some developed countries.

**Population Pyramids** visualise age and sex distribution. A wide-based pyramid indicates high birth rates and rapid growth. A column shape indicates stable growth. An inverted pyramid indicates an ageing population with potential workforce shortages.

**Migration Models**: Ravenstein's laws of migration suggest most migration is short-distance, step-wise, and driven by economic factors. The push-pull model identifies factors driving people away from origin (push) and attracting them to destination (pull), including employment, conflict, and quality of life.

**Population Policies**: Pronatalist policies encourage births (e.g., baby bonuses, parental leave). Antinatalist policies reduce births (e.g., China's former one-child policy). Migration policies control population movement through quotas, visas, and border controls.

## Intuition

The DTM is like a life story of a country's population. At first, both birth and death rates are high (like infancy -- lots of change). As healthcare improves, death rates drop first (childhood -- survival improves). Birth rates drop later as societies develop (adulthood -- family sizes shrink). Population pyramids are a snapshot of this story at one moment -- the shape reveals where the country is in its demographic journey.

## Common Pitfalls

- Confusing the DTM stages. Stage 2 has falling death rates but still-high birth rates (rapid growth). Stage 3 has falling birth rates (growth slows). Stage 4 has low, stable rates. Stage 5 has declining population.
- Assuming migration is always economic. Conflict, persecution, environmental disasters, and family reunification are also major drivers.
- Reading population pyramids backwards. The base represents the youngest age group; the top represents the oldest. A wide base means many young people, not many old people.
