## Mussolini's Rise to Power (1919–1925)

### Context: Post-War Italy

Italy fought on the Allied side in the First World War but emerged from the conflict with significant grievances:

- **"Mutilated victory" (vittoria mutilata):** Italy received far less territorial gain than promised in the Treaty of London (1915). The failure to gain Fiume and Dalmatia fuelled nationalist resentment.
- **Economic dislocation:** Wartime inflation, unemployment, and labour unrest. Industrial workers in the north occupied factories in the "Biennio Rosso" (Two Red Years, 1919–1920).
- **Political instability:** The liberal parliamentary system, dominated by Giovanni Giolitti and the "Transformismo" tradition of coalition management, was widely perceived as corrupt and ineffective.
- **Fear of Bolshevism:** The success of the Russian Revolution (1917) alarmed landowners, industrialists, and the middle class, who feared communist revolution.

### The Fasci di Combattimento

- Mussolini founded the Fasci di Combattimento in Milan on 23 March 1919.
- The early programme was eclectic: nationalism, republicanism, universal suffrage, workers' participation in management, and land redistribution.
- The movement attracted war veterans, discontented middle-class professionals, and disaffected socialists.
- The Arditi (elite assault troops) and former socialist syndicalists provided a paramilitary core.

### The Squadristi and the March on Rome

- The Fascist paramilitary squads (squadristi) used violence against socialist organisations, trade unions, and local governments, particularly in rural Tuscany, Emilia-Romagna, and Lombardy.
- Local elites, landowners, and police often colluded with or tolerated Fascist violence.
- Mussolini positioned Fascism as a bulwark against communism, winning the support of industrialists (notably the Confederazione Generale del Lavoro) and the monarchy.
- The March on Rome (28 October 1922): approximately 25,000 Fascists converged on Rome. King Victor Emmanuel III refused to declare martial law and appointed Mussolini as Prime Minister.
- The March was more of a political coup than a military conquest — Mussolini was already in negotiations with the establishment before the march began.

**Key Term:** *Squadristi* — the paramilitary wings of the Fascist movement, responsible for violent attacks on political opponents, particularly socialists and trade unionists. Their violence was systematic and often sanctioned by local authorities.

## The Consolidation of Power (1922–1929)

### The Aventine Secession and the Matteotti Crisis

- The Aventine Secession (1924): Opposition deputies withdrew from parliament after the murder of socialist leader Giacomo Matteotti, who had publicly denounced Fascist electoral fraud.
- Mussolini initially hesitated but then resolved to establish a full dictatorship. In a speech to the Chamber of Deputies on 3 January 1925, he accepted responsibility for Fascist violence.
- The regime progressively dismantled constitutional restrictions: press censorship, the dissolution of opposition parties, and the creation of a secret police (OVRA).

### The Corporate State

- The Chamber of Deputies was renamed the Chamber of Fasces and Corporations (1939).
- The corporatist system organised the economy into 22 corporations by industry, each comprising representatives of employers, employees, and the state.
- In practice, corporatism served the interests of employers and the state. Real wages declined, independent trade unions were suppressed, and workers lost the right to strike.

**Key Term:** *Corporatism* — an economic and political system in which society is organised into corporate groups (by industry or profession) that mediate between the individual and the state. In Fascist theory, it replaced class conflict with state-managed collaboration; in practice, it was a mechanism of state control over labour.

## The Doctrine of Fascism

Mussolini co-authored *The Doctrine of Fascism* (1932) with the philosopher Giovanni Gentile. Key tenets include:

- **Rejection of liberalism:** Fascism denied the validity of individual rights, parliamentary democracy, and laissez-faire economics.
- **Rejection of socialism:** Fascism rejected class struggle and internationalism, replacing them with national solidarity under authoritarian leadership.
- **The totalitarian state:** "Everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state." The state was the supreme expression of national will.
- **The cult of action and violence:** Fascism glorified war, physical courage, and the "spiritual" transformation of the nation through struggle.
- **Anti-intellectualism:** Fascism was rooted in action, not abstract thought. "To believe, to obey, to combat" (Credere, Obbedire, Combattere).
- **The leader principle:** The Duce embodied the will of the nation. Mussolini's personal authority was the ultimate source of political legitimacy.

## Foreign Policy

### The Ethiopian War (1935–1936)

- Mussolini sought to build an Italian empire in East Africa as a means of bolstering national prestige and distracting from domestic economic problems.
- Italy invaded Ethiopia on 3 October 1935. The League of Nations imposed economic sanctions, but these were ineffective and did not include oil.
- Italy used poison gas against Ethiopian forces and civilians. Ethiopia was annexed in May 1936.
- The war drove Mussolini closer to Nazi Germany, as Britain and France opposed Italian expansion.

### Albania (1939)

- Italy invaded and annexed Albania in April 1939, establishing a personal union under the Italian crown.
- Albania served as a strategic base for Italian operations in the Balkans and the eastern Mediterranean.

### The Axis Alliance

- The Rome-Berlin Axis (October 1936) aligned Italy with Nazi Germany.
- The Pact of Steel (May 1939) committed Italy and Germany to mutual military support.
- Italy entered the Second World War on 10 June 1940, declaring war on France and Britain.
- Military disasters in Greece (1940–1941), North Africa, and the Soviet Union exposed the weakness of Italy's armed forces.
- The Allied invasion of Sicily (July 1943) led to Mussolini's overthrow by the Grand Council of Fascism and arrest by the king on 25 July 1943.
- The German-backed Italian Social Republic (Salò Republic, 1943–1945) was a puppet state in northern Italy. Mussolini was captured and executed by Italian partisans on 28 April 1945.

## Social and Cultural Policies

### The Demographic Campaign

- Mussolini promoted large families through financial incentives, marriage loans, and the "Battle for Births."
- Population growth was framed as essential for imperial expansion and military power.
- The policy had limited success: birth rates declined throughout the Fascist period.

### Education and Youth

- The Opera Nazionale Balilla (1926) organised children and adolescents into age-graded groups: Balilla (8–14), Avanguardisti (14–18).
- Universities were subjected to ideological control. The 1923 Gentile Reform emphasised classical education and Italian nationalism.
- The reformed curriculum privileged literature, history, and philosophy over scientific and technical subjects.

### Church-State Relations

- The Lateran Treaty (1929) resolved the long-standing dispute between the Italian state and the Catholic Church.
- The treaty recognised Vatican City as an independent state, provided financial compensation for the loss of the Papal States, and established Catholicism as the state religion.
- The Concordat gave the Church control over marriage law, religious education, and the appointment of bishops.

### Racial Laws (1938)

- Under pressure from Nazi Germany and the influence of Fascist ideologues, Mussolini introduced anti-Semitic racial laws in September 1938.
- Jews were excluded from public life, education, and professions. Approximately 7,000 Italian Jews were deported to concentration camps; roughly 15% survived.
- The racial laws were not part of the original Fascist programme and represented a late, ideologically driven shift.

**Key Term:** *Lateran Treaty (1929)* — the agreement between the Italian state and the Holy See that established Vatican City as an independent sovereign territory, settled the financial claims of the Church, and defined the relationship between Catholicism and the Italian state.

## Comparison with Nazi Germany and Stalinist USSR

### Methods of Consolidating Power

| Feature | Fascist Italy | Nazi Germany | Stalinist USSR |
| :------ | :------------ | :----------- | :------------- |
| **Seizure of power** | Appointed through constitutional processes; gradual consolidation | Appointed through constitutional processes; rapid elimination of opposition | Rise through party apparatus; elimination of rivals after Lenin's death |
| **One-party state** | Achieved by 1926 | Achieved by 1933 | Already in place since 1917 |
| **Use of terror** | Moderate — OVRA, exile, imprisonment; less systematic than Nazi or Soviet terror | Systematic — Gestapo, SS, concentration camps; escalating to genocide | Systematic — NKVD, purges, Gulag; mass executions and deportations |
| **Leader cult** | The Duce as father of the nation; personal authority | The Führer as embodiment of the racial state; semi-religious devotion | Stalin as the great leader, teacher, and "father of nations" |

### Economic Policy

- **Fascist Italy:** Corporatism suppressed independent trade unions and maintained private ownership. Economic performance was poor — GDP per capita growth was sluggish compared to liberal democracies.
- **Nazi Germany:** Rearmament and public works (Autobahn) drove rapid economic recovery. The economy was restructured for war, with private firms operating under state direction.
- **Stalinist USSR:** Central planning through Five-Year Plans. Rapid industrialisation at the cost of massive human suffering. Forced collectivisation of agriculture.

### Ideology

- **Fascist Italy:** Nationalism, imperial expansion, corporatism. Anti-communist but not initially racial in its ideology.
- **Nazi Germany:** Racial ideology was central — antisemitism, *Lebensraum*, Aryan supremacy. The Volksgemeinschaft (racial community) defined national belonging.
- **Stalinist USSR:** Marxism-Leninism. The class struggle, the vanguard party, and the construction of socialism. Racial ideology was rejected in favour of internationalism, though Soviet nationality policy was complex.

### Historiographical Debates

- **Was Fascism a distinct ideology or merely anti-liberal and anti-communist?** Roger Griffin argues that Fascism possessed a coherent ideological core — "palingenetic ultranationalism" — centred on national rebirth through revolutionary action. Others, such as Renzo De Felice, emphasise the regime's pragmatic adaptation to circumstances.
- **Totalitarian model vs authoritarian model:** Hannah Arendt and Carl Friedrich-Zbigniew Brzezinski argued that both Fascist Italy and Stalinist USSR were totalitarian states characterised by monopoly of mass communication, single-party control, and systematic terror. However, scholars like Juan Linz contended that Fascist Italy was merely authoritarian — lacking the ideological penetration and total control of the Nazi or Soviet systems.
- **The "modernising" thesis:** Some historians, notably Dennis Mack Smith, argued that Fascism was a reactionary movement that preserved the power of the traditional elites (monarchy, Church, military) rather than a modernising force. Others, such as Emilio Gentile, contend that Fascism did pursue a form of political modernisation through mass mobilisation and state intervention.
