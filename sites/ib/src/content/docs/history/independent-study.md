## Overview

Independent historical research is assessed in two components of the IB History course: the Internal Assessment (IA), a historical investigation of up to 2,200 words (20% at SL and HL), and the Extended Essay (EE), a 4,000-word research essay on a topic of the student's choosing (counted toward the diploma score). Both require the application of rigorous historical methodology, critical engagement with sources, and the construction of a sustained, evidence-based argument.

---

## Choosing a Research Question

The quality of the research question determines the quality of the entire investigation. A strong question is focused, arguable, and researchable.

### Characteristics of a Strong Research Question

1. **Specific and focused.** The question must be narrow enough to be addressed within the word limit. "What were the causes of the Cold War?" is too broad. "To what extent was the Truman Doctrine the primary cause of the Cold War?" is focused and arguable.

2. **Arguable.** A question that has a single correct answer does not require historical analysis. A strong question invites interpretation and debate. "Did the Cold War end in 1989?" is less effective than "To what extent did Gorbachev's reforms, rather than Western pressure, cause the end of the Cold War?"

3. **Researchable.** The student must have access to sufficient primary and secondary sources. A question on a topic with limited archival access may be unworkable within the constraints of the course.

4. **Historically significant.** The question should address a topic of genuine historical importance, not a trivial or idiosyncratic matter.

### Common Pitfalls in Question Selection

- Choosing a topic based on personal interest without evaluating source availability.
- Selecting a question that is too broad for the word limit.
- Choosing a question that requires specialised knowledge (e.g., advanced economics, military strategy) that the student does not possess.
- Selecting a question that is too narrow, resulting in insufficient material for a sustained argument.

## Research Methodology

### The Research Process

1. **Preliminary reading:** Survey the secondary literature to understand the scope and key debates surrounding the topic. This informs the formulation of the research question.

2. **Question formulation:** Refine the research question based on preliminary reading. The question may evolve as research deepens — this is normal and expected.

3. **Source gathering:** Collect primary and secondary sources. The IA requires evaluation of at least two sources; the EE requires engagement with a broader range.

4. **Note-taking and organisation:** Organise notes by theme or argument, not by source. This facilitates synthesis.

5. **Argument construction:** Develop a thesis that answers the research question. Structure the essay around the thesis, not around the sources.

6. **Writing and revision:** Draft, revise, and refine. Check for analytical rigour, accuracy, and adherence to word limits.

### Primary vs Secondary Sources

**Primary sources** are materials produced at the time of the event or by participants:

- Government documents (treaties, policy papers, parliamentary records)
- Speeches and public statements
- Diaries, letters, and personal memoirs
- Newspaper articles and contemporary reports
- Photographs, films, and audio recordings
- Statistical data and official reports

**Secondary sources** are analyses produced after the event by historians:

- Academic monographs
- Journal articles
- Textbooks and reference works
- Documentary films and academic lectures

The distinction is contextual. A history textbook is a secondary source for the events it describes, but a primary source for studying historiographical trends.

**For the IA:** At least two sources must be evaluated in detail (origin, purpose, content, value, limitation). These can be primary or secondary, but should be distinct in nature.

**For the EE:** The student should demonstrate a broad reading of secondary literature and selective use of primary evidence. The bibliography should reflect engagement with academic scholarship, not merely online sources or textbooks.

## Source Evaluation Framework

The IA requires critical evaluation of two sources. The following framework should be applied:

### Origin

- **Who** created the source? Consider their identity, nationality, political affiliation, social position, and relationship to the events.
- **When** was it created? Proximity to events provides immediacy but may lack perspective; distance provides perspective but may introduce hindsight bias.
- **Where** was it produced? The geographical and institutional context shapes the source's perspective.

### Purpose

- **Why** was the source created? Was it intended to inform, persuade, record, or reflect?
- **For whom** was it produced? The intended audience shapes the content — a speech to parliament differs from a private diary.
- **What** was the historical context of its production? Political pressures, censorship, and propaganda conditions all affect what could be said.

### Content

- **What** does the source claim? Identify explicit arguments and implicit assumptions.
- **What** is omitted? Silences and omissions are as significant as explicit statements.
- **Is the content consistent** with other evidence? Corroboration strengthens reliability.

### Value

- What does the source contribute that other sources do not?
- Does it provide first-hand testimony, independent corroboration, or a unique perspective?
- How does its value compare to the other sources you have examined?

### Limitation

- What biases, whether conscious or unconscious, shape the source?
- What constraints (political, social, temporal) limit its reliability?
- What aspects of the topic does it fail to address?

## Historiographical Analysis

Engaging with historiography demonstrates sophistication and depth. For both the IA and the EE, students should:

### Identify Interpretive Traditions

- Recognise that historians interpret the same events in fundamentally different ways.
- Identify whether a historian belongs to a broader school of thought (Marxist, revisionist, post-revisionist, liberal, conservative, feminist).
- Understand how the historian's own historical context shapes their interpretation.

### Engage with Specific Historians

- Name specific historians and their arguments.
- Explain where historians agree and disagree.
- Assess the strength of competing interpretations based on their evidence and reasoning.

### Synthesise Interpretations

- Do not merely list different views. Explain why interpretations differ and assess which is most persuasive.
- Consider whether new evidence (e.g., newly opened archives) has altered the historiographical landscape.

## Essay Structure

### Internal Assessment Structure

| Section | Word Count | Content |
| :------ | :--------- | :------ |
| **Identification and Evaluation of Sources** | Up to 500 words | State the research question. Evaluate two sources using the origin-purpose-content-value-limitation framework. |
| **Investigation** | 1,300–1,600 words | The body of the essay. Present a well-argued, evidence-based investigation. Structure by argument, not by source. |
| **Reflection** | Up to 400 words | Reflect on the research process: what was learned about historical methodology, what challenges were encountered, and how the approach could be improved. |

**Important:** The bibliography is not included in the word count. The total word count must not exceed 2,200 words.

### Extended Essay Structure

The EE follows a conventional academic essay structure:

1. **Title page:** Clear, focused title that reflects the research question.
2. **Abstract:** A 300-word summary of the argument, methodology, and conclusion.
3. **Introduction:** State the research question, explain its significance, and outline the argument.
4. **Body:** Develop the argument through structured paragraphs, supported by evidence and historiographical engagement.
5. **Conclusion:** Synthesise the evidence and deliver a substantiated answer to the research question.
6. **Bibliography:** Full citations for all sources used, formatted consistently.
7. **Appendices (optional):** Supporting materials that are too lengthy for the main text.

## Supervisor Meetings

The supervisor guides the student through the research process. Key meetings include:

- **Initial meeting:** Discuss the research question's feasibility, agree on reading, and set milestones.
- **Progress meetings:** Review source material, argument development, and draft sections.
- **Final meeting:** Review the complete draft and check adherence to IB requirements.

The student should maintain a research diary, come to meetings prepared, and meet agreed deadlines.

## Intuition

Independent historical research is like detective work: you begin with a question, gather evidence from multiple witnesses (sources), evaluate their credibility, and construct a case. The research question is your case theory -- if it is weak, no amount of evidence will save the investigation. Source evaluation is not a checklist exercise but an act of empathy: you must imagine the world from the creator's position to understand what they could see and what they chose to hide. The reflection is your opportunity to show the jury how you grew as a thinker.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Descriptive rather than analytical writing.** Both the IA and EE require sustained argument, not narration. Every piece of evidence must be followed by analysis of its significance.

2. **Inadequate source evaluation.** Merely describing a source rather than critically evaluating origin, purpose, content, value, and limitation.

3. **Superficial reflection (IA).** The reflection must demonstrate genuine engagement with the research process, not vague generalisations about the difficulty of history.

4. **Exceeding the word count.** The 2,200-word limit (IA) is strict. Plan word allocation across sections carefully.

5. **Insufficient historiography (EE).** Failure to engage with competing interpretations suggests limited research.

6. **Weak conclusion.** The conclusion must synthesise the evidence discussed, not merely restate the introduction. Deliver a clear, substantiated answer to the research question.

## Exemplar Topics

Well-suited topics for IB History independent research include:

- To what extent was the Long March the decisive factor in the CCP's victory in the Chinese Civil War?
- How effectively did the Marshall Plan achieve its economic and political objectives in Western Europe?
- To what extent was the failure of the League of Nations in Manchuria (1931) a consequence of structural weaknesses rather than member state selfishness?
- How significant was the role of propaganda in consolidating Mussolini's domestic authority between 1925 and 1935?
- To what extent did Gorbachev's reforms cause the collapse of the Soviet Union, as opposed to structural economic and political factors?
- To what extent was the Cold War a conflict between two equally aggressive powers, rather than a primarily American-driven phenomenon?
- To what extent did the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945) determine the outcome of the Chinese Civil War (1946–1949)?
