## Origins of the Cold War (1945–1949)

### Yalta and Potsdam

The wartime alliance between the United States, the United Kingdom, and the Soviet Union disintegrated rapidly after the defeat of Nazi Germany. The conferences at Yalta (February 1945) and Potsdam (July–August 1945) exposed the fundamental incompatibility of American and Soviet objectives.

**Yalta Conference (4–11 February 1945)**

- The Declaration on Liberated Europe promised free elections in countries liberated from Nazi occupation.
- Germany was divided into four occupation zones (US, UK, France, USSR).
- Stalin pledged to join the war against Japan within three months.
- The "percentages agreement" (Churchill–Stalin) informally divided spheres of influence in the Balkans.
- Poland's borders were redrawn, with the Curzon Line as its eastern boundary.

**Potsdam Conference (17 July–2 August 1945)**

- Truman replaced Roosevelt and adopted a firmer anti-Soviet stance.
- The successful atomic bomb test during the conference altered the power dynamic.
- Disagreements over German reparations and the political future of Poland deepened the rift.
- The division of Germany and Berlin into four zones was confirmed, setting the stage for future crises.

### The Truman Doctrine and Containment

On 12 March 1947, President Truman outlined the policy of containment:

- The US would provide political, military, and economic assistance to nations threatened by authoritarianism.
- Immediate context: British withdrawal of support from Greece and Turkey.
- $400 million in aid to Greece and Turkey.
- The Marshall Plan (1948) extended economic reconstruction aid to Western Europe, explicitly excluding the USSR and its satellites.

**Key Term:** *Containment* — the US foreign policy doctrine of preventing the expansion of Soviet communism through a combination of military alliances, economic aid, and political pressure, articulated by George Kennan in his "Long Telegram" (1946) and "X Article" (1947).

## The Berlin Crises

### The First Berlin Crisis (1948–1949)

- The introduction of a new currency (Deutsche Mark) in the Western zones of Germany threatened Soviet control.
- Stalin imposed the Berlin Blockade (June 1948–May 1949), cutting off road, rail, and canal access to West Berlin.
- The Western Allies responded with the Berlin Airlift, flying in supplies for 318 days. Over 2.3 million tonnes of supplies were delivered.
- The blockade was lifted in May 1949. The crisis accelerated the formation of NATO (April 1949) and the Federal Republic of Germany (May 1949).

### The Second Berlin Crisis (1958–1961)

- Khrushchev issued an ultimatum demanding that the Western powers withdraw from Berlin and that West Berlin become a "free city" within six months.
- The deadline passed without confrontation, but the crisis persisted.
- The construction of the Berlin Wall on 13 August 1961 was the Soviet response to the massive exodus of East Germans to the West — an estimated 3.5 million had fled between 1945 and 1961.

### The Third Berlin Crisis (1961)

- The Checkpoint Charlie confrontation (October 1961): US and Soviet tanks faced each other at the crossing point. The incident was resolved through diplomacy but highlighted the risk of direct military confrontation.
- The Berlin Wall became the most visible symbol of the Cold War division of Europe.

**Key Term:** *Berlin Blockade* — the Soviet attempt to force the Western Allies out of West Berlin by severing all land and water access, countered by the Berlin Airlift, which demonstrated Western resolve to maintain their position.

## The Arms Race

### Nuclear Proliferation

- The US monopoly on atomic weapons ended in 1949 when the USSR successfully tested its first atomic bomb.
- The hydrogen bomb was developed by the US (1952) and the USSR (1953).
- By the 1960s, both superpowers possessed enough nuclear weapons to destroy civilisation many times over — a condition termed "mutually assured destruction" (MAD).

### The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)

The most dangerous moment of the Cold War:

- In October 1962, US reconnaissance discovered Soviet nuclear missile sites under construction in Cuba, 90 miles from the US mainland.
- President Kennedy imposed a naval "quarantine" around Cuba.
- For thirteen days (16–28 October), the world stood on the brink of nuclear war.
- Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles in exchange for a US pledge not to invade Cuba and a secret agreement to remove US Jupiter missiles from Turkey.
- The crisis led to the establishment of the Moscow–Washington hotline (1963) and the Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty (1963).

**Key Term:** *Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD)* — the strategic doctrine in which both superpowers maintained sufficient nuclear capability to guarantee the destruction of the other in the event of a first strike, thereby deterring either side from initiating nuclear war.

## Detente (1969–1979)

### Origins

- The Cuban Missile Crisis demonstrated the catastrophic risk of uncontrolled escalation.
- Both superpowers sought to manage their rivalry through diplomacy and arms control.
- Nixon and Kissinger pursued a strategy of "linkage" — connecting progress on different issues to create incentives for cooperation.

### Key Agreements

- **Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I, 1972):** Capped the number of strategic ballistic missile launchers and established the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty.
- **Helsinki Accords (1975):** Recognised post-war European borders, promoted human rights, and facilitated economic and scientific cooperation.
- **SALT II (1979):** Signed by Carter and Brezhnev but never ratified by the US Senate following the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.

### Limits of Detente

- Proxy wars continued: Angola, Mozambique, Ethiopia, Nicaragua.
- The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (December 1979) effectively ended detente.
- Reagan's military buildup and "evil empire" rhetoric (1983) marked a return to confrontation.

## Gorbachev's Reforms (1985–1991)

### Perestroika and Glasnost

- Mikhail Gorbachev became General Secretary in March 1985, inheriting an economy in stagnation and an empire overextended by military commitments.
- **Perestroika** ("restructuring"): Economic reforms introducing limited market mechanisms, decentralising management, and permitting limited private enterprise.
- **Glasnost** ("openness"): Greater political transparency, relaxation of censorship, and limited political liberalisation.
- **Demokratizatsiya** ("democratisation"): Introduction of competitive elections for the Congress of People's Deputies (1989).

### Foreign Policy Revolution

- Gorbachev abandoned the Brezhnev Doctrine, which had justified Soviet military intervention in socialist states.
- The "Sinatra Doctrine" — allowing Eastern Bloc states to determine their own internal affairs.
- The fall of the Berlin Wall (9 November 1989) was a direct consequence of Gorbachev's refusal to use force to maintain the East German regime.
- German reunification (3 October 1990) was achieved peacefully through the Two Plus Four negotiations.

### The Collapse of the USSR

- Gorbachev's reforms unleashed centrifugal forces he could not control.
- Nationalist movements in the Baltic states, the Caucasus, and Central Asia demanded sovereignty.
- The August Coup (19–21 August 1991) by hardline communists failed, but fatally weakened Gorbachev's authority.
- The Soviet Union was formally dissolved on 26 December 1991.

## The End of the Cold War: Interpretations

### Orthodox Interpretation

- The Cold War was caused by Soviet expansionism and ideological aggression.
- Soviet communism was inherently expansionist; the West was forced to respond defensively.
- Gorbachev's reforms and the internal contradictions of the Soviet system ended the conflict.

### Revisionist Interpretation

- The US bears significant responsibility for the Cold War through its economic and military expansionism.
- The Marshall Plan and NATO were instruments of American hegemony.
- The Soviet Union's actions were largely defensive, responding to Western encirclement.

### Post-Revisionist Interpretation

- Both superpowers bear responsibility for the Cold War.
- Domestic politics, ideology, and misperception all contributed to the escalation of tensions.
- The end of the Cold War resulted from a combination of internal Soviet decay, Western pressure, and the agency of individuals (Gorbachev, Reagan, Thatcher).

## Impact on International Relations Post-1991

### The "Unipolar Moment"

- The US emerged as the sole superpower, with unrivalled military and economic power.
- The "end of history" thesis (Francis Fukuyama, 1989) argued that liberal democracy had triumphed as the final form of government.
- This interpretation was challenged by subsequent events: the Rwandan genocide, the Balkan wars, the rise of China, and the emergence of non-state actors.

### NATO Expansion

- NATO expanded eastward to include former Warsaw Pact states (Poland, Hungary, Czech Republic, 1999; Baltic states, Romania, Bulgaria, 2004).
- Russia perceived NATO expansion as a threat to its security interests, contributing to tensions over Georgia (2008) and Ukraine (2014, 2022).

### The Legacy of Containment

- The Cold War framework of containment influenced US foreign policy well after 1991.
- The "War on Terror" after 11 September 2001 borrowed Cold War concepts of ideological confrontation and alliance-building.

## IB Assessment Connections

Cold War superpower relations connects to several IB Paper 2 topics:

- **Topic 2 — Causes and Effects of Wars:** The Cold War as a "long war," its origins, proxy conflicts, and eventual resolution.

### Essay Strategies

For Paper 2 questions on the Cold War, effective responses should:

- Distinguish between long-term and short-term causes of specific crises.
- Engage with historiographical debate (orthodox, revisionist, post-revisionist interpretations).
- Use specific evidence: treaties, dates, statistics (nuclear warhead counts, economic data).
- Analyse the role of individual leaders (Truman, Stalin, Khrushchev, Nixon, Reagan, Gorbachev) alongside structural factors.
- Draw connections between different crises to demonstrate understanding of the Cold War as a coherent, evolving conflict.

### Source Analysis (Paper 1)

When analysing Cold War sources in Paper 1:

- Consider the author's nationality, political affiliation, and access to information.
- Assess whether the source was produced during or after the Cold War, as hindsight alters interpretation.
- Compare sources from different sides of the Iron Curtain to identify divergent perspectives.
- Note the distinction between public rhetoric and private policy documents.

## Intuition

Cold War superpower relations operated like two magnets held close together -- the closer they got, the stronger the repulsive force, yet neither could let go without losing face. The Berlin crises, the Cuban Missile Crisis, and the arms race were all manifestations of this magnetic tension. Gorbachev's reforms were like one magnet suddenly becoming weaker -- the balance of forces collapsed, and the entire structure rearranged itself overnight. The key insight is that the Cold War ended not because one side won militarily but because one side lost the will and capacity to sustain the competition.

## Common Mistakes

**Assuming the Cold War was purely ideological:** National interest, power politics, and leader personalities also drove decisions. Don't reduce everything to capitalism vs communism.

**Confusing détente with the end of the Cold War:** Détente (1970s) reduced tensions but didn't end the rivalry. The Cold War ended in 1989-1991 with Soviet collapse.

**Forgetting to consider multiple historiographical perspectives:** Orthodox, revisionist, and post-revisionist interpretations assign different responsibility. Engage with all three in essays.
