### Key Concepts

**Differentiation** finds the rate of change of a function. The derivative f'(x) gives the gradient of the tangent at any point. Key rules include the power rule (d/dx[xⁿ] = nxⁿ⁻¹), the chain rule (d/dx[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x))·g'(x)), and the product rule (d/dx[uv] = u'v + uv').

**Integration** is the reverse of differentiation and gives the area under a curve. The fundamental theorem of calculus links the two: ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = F(b) − F(a). Key techniques include u-substitution and integration by parts ( ∫u dv = uv − ∫v du).

**Differential Equations** relate a function to its derivatives. In IB, you typically solve separable equations by rearranging to get all y terms on one side and all x terms on the other, then integrating both sides. The general solution contains an arbitrary constant C; an initial condition gives the particular solution.

**Applications of Calculus**: The derivative gives the slope of a curve (used for finding maxima/minima by setting f'(x) = 0). The integral gives areas and volumes. Related rates problems use the chain rule to connect rates of change of different quantities.

## Intuition

Think of differentiation as a "magnifying glass" that zooms in on a curve until it looks straight -- the slope of that straight line is the derivative. Integration is like stacking infinitely many thin rectangles under a curve to find the total area. The fundamental theorem of calculus tells you these two operations undo each other, just like addition and subtraction.

## Common Pitfalls

- Forgetting the constant of integration (+C) in indefinite integrals. This is an easy mark to lose and appears in almost every exam.
- Applying the chain rule incorrectly: you must multiply by the derivative of the inner function. A common mistake is to forget this multiplier, especially with trigonometric and exponential functions.
- Confusing the conditions for a maximum vs minimum. After finding critical points (f'(x) = 0), you must test the second derivative or use a sign chart to determine which is which.
