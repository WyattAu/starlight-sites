### Key Concepts

**Differentiation** finds the rate of change of a function. The derivative f'(x) gives the gradient of the tangent at any point. Key rules include the power rule (d/dx[xⁿ] = nxⁿ⁻¹), the chain rule (d/dx[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x))·g'(x)), and the product rule (d/dx[uv] = u'v + uv'). The quotient rule is d/dx[u/v] = (u'v − uv')/v². Second derivatives f''(x) tell you about concavity -- positive means concave up, negative means concave down. At a stationary point, f'(x) = 0; the second derivative test determines whether it's a maximum (f'' < 0) or minimum (f'' > 0).

**Integration** is the reverse of differentiation and gives the area under a curve. The fundamental theorem of calculus links the two: ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = F(b) − F(a). Key techniques include u-substitution (the reverse of the chain rule) and integration by parts (∫u dv = uv − ∫v du). The constant of integration +C must always be included in indefinite integrals. Definite integrals give numerical values representing signed area -- areas above the x-axis are positive, below are negative.

**Differential Equations** relate a function to its derivatives. In IB, you in standard practice solve separable equations by rearranging to get all y terms on one side and all x terms on the other, then integrating both sides. The general solution contains an arbitrary constant C; an initial condition (a specific value of y at a specific x) gives the particular solution by determining C. These model real-world phenomena like population growth, radioactive decay, and cooling.

**Applications of Calculus**: The derivative gives the slope of a curve (used for finding maxima/minima by setting f'(x) = 0). The integral gives areas and volumes (volumes of revolution: V = π∫y²dx). Related rates problems use the chain rule to connect rates of change of different quantities (e.g., how fast the radius of a balloon changes as it's inflated). Curve sketching uses f'(x) for turning points and f''(x) for concavity.

## Intuition

Think of differentiation as a "magnifying glass" that zooms in on a curve until it looks straight -- the slope of that straight line is the derivative. Integration is like stacking infinitely many thin rectangles under a curve to find the total area. The fundamental theorem of calculus tells you these two operations undo each other, just like addition and subtraction. Differential equations are the "word problems" of calculus -- they describe real situations and ask you to find the function that fits.

**Why it matters**: Calculus is the mathematics of change. It's essential for physics (describing motion and forces), economics (optimising profit), biology (modelling population growth), and engineering (designing structures that withstand changing loads). Without calculus, we couldn't describe most of the natural world.

## Common Pitfalls

- Forgetting the constant of integration (+C) in indefinite integrals. This is an easy mark to lose and appears in almost every exam. Always include it unless the question specifies a definite integral.
- Applying the chain rule incorrectly: you must multiply by the derivative of the inner function. A common mistake is to forget this multiplier, especially with trigonometric and exponential functions. For example, d/dx[sin(3x)] = 3cos(3x), not cos(3x).
- Confusing the conditions for a maximum vs minimum. After finding critical points (f'(x) = 0), you must test the second derivative or use a sign chart to determine which is which. f''(x) > 0 means minimum; f''(x) < 0 means maximum.
- Forgetting that integration gives signed area. Areas below the x-axis are negative. To find total area, split the integral at the x-intercepts and take absolute values.
