### Key Concepts

**Newton's Laws of Motion**: First law (inertia): an object remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by a resultant force. Second law: F = ma (the resultant force equals mass times acceleration). Third law: for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction (forces always come in pairs acting on different objects). These laws form the foundation of classical mechanics and apply in all inertial reference frames.

**Work, Energy, and Power**: Work done = Force × displacement × cosθ (W = Fs cosθ), where θ is the angle between force and displacement. Kinetic energy = ½mv². Gravitational potential energy = mgh. The work-energy theorem states that the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy. Power = work done / time = force × velocity. Energy is conserved in closed systems -- it can be transferred between forms but cannot be created or destroyed.

**Momentum and Impulse**: Momentum p = mv (mass × velocity). Impulse = Force × time = change in momentum (FΔt = Δp). In collisions, total momentum is conserved if no external forces act. Elastic collisions conserve both momentum and kinetic energy; inelastic collisions conserve momentum but not kinetic energy. In a perfectly inelastic collision, the objects stick together.

**Circular Motion**: An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating because its direction changes continuously. Centripetal acceleration = v²/r directed toward the centre. Centripetal force = mv²/r. This force is provided by different mechanisms in different contexts: gravity for orbits, friction for cars on roads, tension for pendulums, electrostatic force for electrons orbiting nuclei.

## Intuition

Mechanics is the branch of physics that describes motion and its causes. Kinematics describes *how* things move (displacement, velocity, acceleration) without worrying about *why*. Dynamics explains *why* through Newton's laws -- forces cause acceleration, and every action has an equal and opposite reaction. Energy conservation provides a powerful shortcut: instead of tracking forces through complex paths, you can compare initial and final energy states directly. Momentum is a "conserved quantity" -- it's the physics equivalent of "what goes around comes around."

**Why it matters**: Mechanics is the foundation of all physics. Understanding forces, energy, and momentum is essential for engineering, sports science, aerospace, and any field involving motion. It teaches systematic problem-solving and the power of conservation laws.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Sign errors in kinematics**: Forgetting that velocity and acceleration are vectors -- if you define "up" as positive, gravity (acting down) must be negative in your equations. A common error is mixing signs within one problem, which gives incorrect answers.
- **Normal force confusion**: The normal force is not always equal to mg -- on an incline, it equals mg cos θ; in accelerating elevators, it changes with the acceleration. The normal force adjusts to prevent objects from passing through surfaces.
- **Momentum in explosions**: Forgetting that momentum is conserved in all directions independently -- a system can have zero total momentum in one direction while having non-zero momentum in another. Always analyse x and y components separately.
- **Confusing elastic and inelastic collisions**: Both conserve momentum, but only elastic collisions conserve kinetic energy. In inelastic collisions, kinetic energy is converted to heat, sound, or deformation.
