### Key Concepts

**Social Identity Theory (Tajfel)**: People categorise themselves and others into in-groups (groups we belong to) and out-groups (groups we don't belong to). Three processes: social categorisation (sorting people into groups), social identification (adopting the identity of the group), and social comparison (comparing in-group favourably to out-groups). In-group favouritism and out-group discrimination arise from the need for positive social identity -- we want our groups to be better than other groups. Minimal group studies (Tajfel, 1970) show that even arbitrary group assignments (preferring Klee over Kandinsky paintings) can produce discrimination -- participants allocated more resources to in-group members even when they didn't know who was in each group.

**Conformity**: Asch's line study (1951) demonstrated that social pressure can cause people to agree with obviously wrong answers. About 75% of participants conformed at least once, with an overall conformity rate of about 37%. Explanations include normative social influence (desire to fit in, be liked, or avoid rejection -- you know the answer is wrong but go along with the group) and informational social influence (relying on others' judgement in ambiguous situations -- you genuinely believe the group knows better). Factors affecting conformity include group size (conformity increases up to about 5-6 people then plateaus), unanimity (conformity drops dramatically if one person breaks ranks), and task difficulty (harder tasks increase informational influence).

**Cultural Dimensions (Hofstede)**: Individualism-collectivism (self vs group orientation -- US scores high individualism, Japan scores high collectivism), power distance (acceptance of hierarchy -- Malaysia scores high, Denmark scores low), uncertainty avoidance (tolerance for ambiguity -- Greece scores high, Singapore scores low), masculinity-femininity (achievement vs nurturing values -- Japan scores high masculinity, Sweden scores high femininity), and long-term orientation (future vs present focus -- China scores high, Nigeria scores low). These dimensions describe cultural tendencies, not absolutes -- individuals within any culture vary enormously.

**Stereotype Threat**: When individuals are aware of a negative stereotype about their group, their performance can decline because anxiety about confirming the stereotype consumes cognitive resources. Steele and Aronson (1995) showed this effect for African American students on academic tests -- performance declined when the test was described as diagnostic of intellectual ability but not when described as a laboratory exercise. The effect is reduced when the stereotype is made less relevant (e.g., telling participants the test is "diagnostic bias-free").

## Intuition

Think of the sociocultural level as the "social software" running in our brains. We're wired to belong to groups (SIT), to follow others when uncertain (conformity), and to absorb cultural programming (Hofstede). Stereotype threat is like having a pop-up window in your mind that says "you probably can't do this" -- it drains processing power even when you try to ignore it. Acculturation is like updating your operating system when you move to a new cultural environment. Minimal group studies reveal how easily we create "us and them" divisions -- even arbitrary categories trigger in-group bias.

**Why it matters**: The sociocultural level explains how social contexts shape behaviour in ways that individual-level analyses cannot. It reveals that much of what we consider "personal" choice is actually influenced by cultural norms, group dynamics, and social identity. This has profound implications for understanding prejudice, discrimination, and intergroup conflict.

## Common Pitfalls

- Confusing normative and informational social influence. Normative = going along with the group to be liked or avoid rejection (you know the answer is wrong). Informational = genuinely believing the group knows better (you doubt your own judgement). Both cause conformity but for different reasons. Asch's study primarily demonstrates normative influence.
- Assuming cultural dimensions are fixed. Hofstede's dimensions describe tendencies, not absolutes. Individuals within any culture vary enormously, and cultures change over time. Hofstede's data is also based on IBM employees in the 1960s-70s, which may not represent current cultural values.
- Forgetting that SIT explains both in-group favouritism AND out-group discrimination. The theory isn't just about liking your own group -- it's also about how that affects treatment of others. This dual process is what drives prejudice and discrimination.
- Forgetting that Asch's study has limitations. The study used artificial tasks with male American students in the 1950s -- ecological validity and generalisability are limited. Modern replications show lower conformity rates, suggesting cultural changes in conformity over time.
