## Intuition

For cognitive level questions, focus on how mental processes (memory, thinking, decision-making) explain behaviour. Memory models are hierarchical -- the MSM is the foundation, the WMM extends it. When discussing biases, always provide specific examples of how the bias operates in real life. For schema theory, use studies like Bartlett's to show how schemas affect memory. When evaluating cognitive explanations, consider whether they are mechanistic (reducing human experience to information processing) and whether they adequately account for individual differences and cultural variation.

**Why it matters**: Understanding cognitive processes reveals why we make predictable errors in judgment, how memory can be both powerful and fallible, and why different people interpret the same event differently. These insights have practical applications in education, eyewitness testimony, and decision-making. These practice questions test your ability to apply cognitive concepts to exam scenarios.

## Worked Example

**Question**: Explain how schemas can affect the accuracy of eyewitness testimony, using research to support your answer.

**Answer Structure**: Define schemas as mental frameworks that organise knowledge and guide perception. Explain that schemas help us process information quickly but can lead to errors when reality doesn't match expectations. Use Bartlett's "War of the Ghosts" study to show how people distort memories to fit existing schemas (constructive memory). Use Loftus and Palmer's car crash study to show how leading questions activate schemas that alter memory -- participants who heard "smashed" estimated higher speeds and were more likely to falsely report seeing broken glass. Evaluate: schemas explain why eyewitness testimony is unreliable (practical application). However, laboratory studies may lack ecological validity, and the research is mostly from Western cultures.

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing the stores in the multi-store model. Sensory register (very brief, modality-specific), STM (limited capacity, ~18 seconds), and LTM (unlimited, potentially permanent) have very different characteristics. Mixing them up loses marks. The MSM also specifies coding: sensory = modality-specific, STM = acoustic, LTM = semantic.
- Assuming schemas are always harmful. They enable rapid information processing -- without them, we'd have to process every situation from scratch. The trade-off is occasional errors when schemas don't fit. Schemas are adaptive -- they just have limitations.
- Confusing proactive and retroactive interference. Proactive = old disrupts new (your old phone number keeps popping up when you try to remember the new one). Retroactive = new disrupts old (learning a new language makes it harder to recall your first language). The direction of disruption determines which type.
- Forgetting to evaluate the working memory model. While the WMM is well-supported by dual-task studies and brain imaging, it may be too Western-centric (focused on verbal and visual processing), and the role of the central executive is not fully understood.

## Cross-References

- **[Biological](../psychology/flashcards-biological):** Biological psychology provides the neural basis for cognitive processes -- brain regions like the hippocampus (memory), prefrontal cortex (working memory), and temporal lobes (language) underpin cognitive functions.
- **[Abnormal](../psychology/flashcards-abnormal):** Abnormal psychology applies cognitive explanations -- Beck's cognitive triad in depression, dysfunctional schemas in OCD, and cognitive biases in anxiety disorders.
- **[Research Methods](../psychology/flashcards-research-methods):** Research methods are essential for investigating cognitive processes -- laboratory experiments, brain imaging, and case studies all contribute evidence for cognitive models.
- **[Sociocultural](../psychology/flashcards-sociocultural):** Sociocultural factors shape cognitive processes -- cultural schemas influence how we categorise information, and social contexts affect decision-making biases.
