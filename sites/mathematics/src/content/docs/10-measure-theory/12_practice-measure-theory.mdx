## Intuition

**Rigorous integration:** Measure theory puts integration on a rigorous foundation — it defines what it means to integrate over weird sets (like the Cantor set) and ensures that limits and integrals can be swapped under appropriate conditions. It is the foundation of modern probability theory.

**Why it matters:** Measure theory enables Lebesgue integration, which is more powerful than Riemann integration — it handles more functions, simplifies limit theorems, and provides the mathematical foundation for probability theory (where probabilities are measures).

**The key insight:** The Lebesgue integral sums function values over sets of constant measure (horizontal slicing) rather than over intervals of constant width (vertical slicing). This simple change enables integration of far more functions.

## Common Mistakes

**Assuming pointwise convergence implies convergence of integrals:** The sequence $f_n = n\chi_{(0,1/n)}$ converges to 0 pointwise on $[0,1]$, yet $\int f_n = 1$ for all $n$. Pointwise convergence alone does not justify passing the limit inside the integral. You need a dominating function (DCT), monotone non-negativity (MCT), or another convergence theorem.

**Confusing sigma-algebra with topology:** A sigma-algebra is closed under countable unions and complements, while a topology is closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. The Borel sigma-algebra is generated by the topology, but they are different structures. A set can be open (in the topology) without being Borel measurable in pathological constructions.

**Confusing $L^p$ inclusion directions on finite vs infinite measure spaces:** On a finite measure space, $L^q \subseteq L^p$ when $q > p$ (higher exponent = smaller space). On an infinite measure space like $[1, \infty)$, this inclusion reverses: $L^p \subseteq L^q$ when $p < q$ is no longer guaranteed. Always check the measure space before applying inclusion results.
