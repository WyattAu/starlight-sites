**The Defect.** Stage J's Stokes theorem integrated forms over
manifolds, and Stage L read spaces through their symmetry groups.
But the deepest invariants of shape are still missing: no tool yet
proves the sphere is not the torus, that knots are knotted, or that
the plane admits no metric of everywhere-positive curvature. The
naive conjecture: such distinctions need ever finer point-set
analysis. The repair is algebra: assign to every space an algebraic
object (a group, a sequence of vector spaces) computable from
combinatorial data, so that a geometric question becomes an
algebraic one the course can already answer. That translation
machine: homology, cohomology, characteristic classes. It is the
shared language of modern geometry, topology, and physics.

**The One-Line Reveal.** <span class="wn-spoiler-inline" tabindex="0">Loops
up to deformation form a group (the fundamental group), chains up

theorem was the shadow of an isomorphism; bundles and curvature
then carry these invariants in their characteristic classes, and
Gauss-Bonnet adds the total: curvature integrated equals the

## 1. The Fundamental Group: Loops Up to Deformation

**Ponder 1 (attempt the invariant).** The circle and the interval
are both one-dimensional connected spaces, yet a loop of string on
the circle cannot be shrunk to a point, while every loop on the
interval can. Invent the algebraic object that sees this
difference: what data does a loop carry, and when should two loops
count as the same?

<details class="wn-spoiler">
<summary>The invariant you are building</summary>
<div class="wn-spoiler-body">

Loops are paths starting and ending at a fixed base point, and the
equivalence is deformation: two loops are the same when one can be
continuously morphed into the other through loops, all pinned at
the base point. The set of equivalence classes is $\pi_1(X,
x_0)$. The miracle: these classes compose (walk one loop, then the
other), and composition makes them a group, the **fundamental
group**. On the interval every loop slides back along itself to
the constant loop, so the group is trivial; on the circle, the
loop that goes around once cannot shrink, and its powers classify
all loops: $\pi_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, Stage C's integers as
winding numbers.

</div>
</details>

**Theorem (the winding number computation).** $\pi_1(S^1) \cong
\mathbb{Z}$, generated by the class of one full rotation; the map
to $\mathbb{Z}$ sends a loop to its net number of turns.

*Proof shape.* The universal covering $\mathbb{R} \to S^1$
($t \mapsto e^{it}$, Stage D-G's exponential) lifts every path to
a path in $\mathbb{R}$ starting at $0$; a closed loop lifts to a
path ending at an integer. Homotopic loops lift to homotopic
paths, which in $\mathbb{R}$ (where the group is trivial) end at
the same point: the ending integer is the invariant. Every integer
occurs, and composition is addition. $\blacksquare$

**The covering-space machinery and Van Kampen, stated.** A
**covering** is a map that is locally a homeomorphism onto evenly
stacked sheets; loops lift uniquely once a starting sheet is
chosen, and the lifting theorem (proved from the uniqueness of
lifts by a connectedness chase) is what powers the winding
number. **Van Kampen's theorem** computes fundamental groups of
unions: if $X = U \cup V$ with $U, V$ open and the
intersection's loops all contractible (the cleanest case for the
amalgamation), handled by the amalgamated-product construction from
Stage L's groups, then $\pi_1(X)$ is built from $\pi_1(U)$ and
$\pi_1(V)$ with the overlaps' relations imposed. It computes the
wedge of two circles as the free group on two generators: the
first non-abelian fundamental group.

<details class="wn-spoiler">
<summary>Answers</summary>
<div class="wn-spoiler-body">

**1.** Homotopy classes of loops at $x_0$, composed by
concatenation; identity: the constant loop; inverses: reversed

**3.** $+2$ and $0$: winding numbers add under concatenation and

## 2. Homological Algebra: Chain Complexes, Ext, Tor

**Definition (chain complex).** A sequence of modules and maps
$\cdots \to C_2 \to C_1 \to C_0 \to 0$ with $\mathrm{im}\, d_{k+1}
\subseteq \ker d_k$ (the **boundary** of anything is a **cycle**).
The **homology** is $H_k := \ker d_k / \mathrm{im}\, d_{k+1}$: the
cycles that are not boundaries.

**Definition (Ext, Tor; declared).** For a ring $R$, the functors
$\mathrm{Ext}^n_R$ and $\mathrm{Tor}^R_n$ measure the failure of
the Hom and tensor functors to be exact: $\mathrm{Ext}^1$ classifies
extensions, $\mathrm{Tor}_1$ detects torsion (the name's origin).
Their construction runs through projective and injective
resolutions; the course declares the machinery (Stage M's module
theory plus this page's complexes is its home) and spends the
reading: torsion in homology is detected by Tor against
$\mathbb{Z}$.

:::note[Quick check]

   defined?

   graph is one-dimensional.
7. State what $H_1$ measures for the same graph, in terms of edges
   and vertices.
8. Give an example of torsion in a module, and say what Tor detects
   about it.

<details class="wn-spoiler">
<summary>Answers</summary>
<div class="wn-spoiler-body">

**1.** $d_k \circ d_{k+1} = 0$: boundaries are cycles, so the
quotient defining homology makes sense. **2.** Cycles modulo
boundaries; $H_0$ measures connected components (0-cycles up to
sums of differences). **3.** Torsion: elements killed by a
nonzero scalar, the obstruction $\mathrm{Tor}$ names. **4.** A
chain complex of nice (projective/injective) modules replacing a
module so that functors can be applied and their deviation
measured. **5.** Without $d \circ d = 0$, images need not sit in
kernels, and the quotient has no meaning.
**6.** 0-cycles are formal sums of vertices with total coefficient zero across each component; boundaries are differences along edges; one quotient dimension per component. **7.** $H_1$ counts independent cycles: loops of edges not filled by faces; the graph's cycle rank, edges minus vertices plus components. **8.** $\mathbb{Z}/2$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module: $2x = 0$ with $x \neq 0$; Tor detects it because tensoring the exact sequence $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{2} \mathbb{Z}$ with it breaks exactness.

</div>
</details>

:::

## 3. Homology: Simplicial and Singular

**Definition (simplicial homology).** A simplicial complex glues
triangles (and their higher-dimensional cousins) along faces; its
$k$-chains are formal sums of $k$-simplices, the boundary map
takes the alternating sum of faces, and the resulting $H_k$ is a
vector space (over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ for the simplest reading) whose
dimensions are the **Betti numbers**: $b_0$ counts components,
$b_1$ counts independent loops, $b_2$ counts enclosed voids.

**Definition (singular homology).** For arbitrary spaces: singular
chains are formal sums of continuous maps from standard simplices
into the space, with the same face-boundary. The price of
generality is that homology is computable only through theorems
(homotopy invariance, excision, Mayer-Vietoris); the payoff is
that every space has the invariants.

**Theorem (the computations; stated with engines).** The sphere
$S^n$ has $H_n \cong \mathbb{Z}$ and all other reduced homology
zero: spheres are the building blocks. The torus has $H_1
\cong \mathbb{Z}^2$ (the two independent loops of the square's
gluing) and $H_2 \cong \mathbb{Z}$ (the whole void). These drive
the invariants that distinguish the sphere from the torus
algebraically, ending the point-set guessing of Stage 2A.

:::note[Quick check]

   0$.
2. Interpret $b_0$, $b_1$, $b_2$ geometrically.

   square's glued edges.
4. What does singular homology buy over simplicial, and what does

   triangulation?
6. Compute the homology of two disjoint circles.
7. Prove that the boundary of a 1-simplex's formal sum equals the
   alternating sum of endpoints, and verify $d \circ d = 0$ on a
   triangle.
8. State the homology of the sphere $S^2$ and what it says about
   the sphere versus the torus.

<details class="wn-spoiler">
<summary>Answers</summary>
<div class="wn-spoiler-body">

**1.** The alternating sum of the three edges; each edge appears
once positively and once negatively across the two adjacent
triangles, so the double boundary vanishes. **2.** Components,
independent loops, enclosed voids. **3.** $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$,
$H_1 = \mathbb{Z}^2$, $H_2 = \mathbb{Z}$; the two $H_1$ generators
are the horizontal and vertical glued edge classes. **4.** It
covers arbitrary spaces; the cost is computability, restored by
invariance and Mayer-Vietoris. **5.** Homotopy invariance:
homeomorphic (indeed homotopy equivalent) spaces have isomorphic
homology.
**6.** $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}^2$ (two components), $H_1 = \mathbb{Z}^2$ (one independent loop per circle), $H_2 = 0$: two copies of the circle's invariants, added component-wise. **7.** $d$ of the edge from vertex $a$ to $b$ is $b - a$; around the triangle the three signed endpoint differences cancel in the sum: $d(d(\text{face})) = 0$. **8.** $H_0 = H_2 = \mathbb{Z}$, $H_1 = 0$: the sphere encloses a void but carries no loops; the torus carries two, which is the algebraic distinction.

</div>
</details>

:::

## 4. de Rham Cohomology: Stokes as an Isomorphism

**Definition.** On a manifold $M$: the **closed** forms are those
with $d\omega = 0$; the **exact** forms are those with $\omega =
d\eta$. The **de Rham cohomology** $H^k_{dR}(M) := \frac{\ker d}{\mathrm{im}\, d}$
on $k$-forms.

**Ponder (attempt the identification).** Stage J's Stokes says
$\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega$. Attempt to see what
this says about a closed $k$-form integrated over two different
$k$-submanifolds with the same boundary, before reading the
theorem.

<details class="wn-spoiler">
<summary>The observation Stokes was hiding</summary>
<div class="wn-spoiler-body">

If $\omega$ is closed ($d\omega = 0$) and $\Sigma_1, \Sigma_2$
share a boundary with opposite orientations, the union
$\Sigma_1 \cup (-\Sigma_2)$ is a closed $k$-chain $\Gamma$, and
Stokes gives $\int_{\Sigma_1} \omega - \int_{\Sigma_2} \omega =
\int_\Gamma \omega = \int_\Gamma d\eta$-style reasoning: the
integrals agree whenever the difference chain bounds. The integral
of a closed form depends only on the homology class of the chain
it is integrated over: closed forms are *invariants of
homology*. That is exactly the statement that the pairing
(closed form, cycle) descends to (cohomology class, homology
class).

</div>
</details>

**Theorem (de Rham; stated).** The map sending a closed form to
its integration pairing against singular cycles is an isomorphism
from $H^k_{dR}(M)$ to $\mathrm{Hom}(H_k(M), \mathbb{R})$: every
homology class is detected by some closed form, and forms with
identical pairings differ by an exact form.

*Honest scope.* The proof (sheaf-theoretic in the modern route,
Baire-category in the classical route along Stage H's lines) is
declared; what the course owns is the consequence chain: Stokes is
the naturality statement of this isomorphism, the winding number
of Section 1 is de Rham $H^1$ of the circle paired against the
fundamental class, and the Betti numbers over $\mathbb{R}$ are the
dimensions of the de Rham spaces. **The capstone computation:** on
the sphere, every closed form of positive degree is exact (Stage
K's machinery, or directly: the radial contraction homotopy
contracts $\omega$ to $d$ of its interior product), so
$H^k_{dR}(S^n) = 0$ for $0 < k < n$; on the torus, the two
coordinate forms $dx, dy$ are closed but not exact (their
integrals over the glued edges are nonzero), so $H^1_{dR} =
\mathbb{R}^2$: cohomology sees the torus's holes exactly as
Section 3's homology promised.

   degrees.
8. Explain what the pairing statement means for a closed form on
   the torus integrated over the two edge loops.

<details class="wn-spoiler">
<summary>Answers</summary>
<div class="wn-spoiler-body">

**1.** $d\omega = 0$; $\omega = d\eta$; the quotient
$\ker d / \mathrm{im}\, d$ on $k$-forms. **2.** The integrals are
equal: the difference chain is closed, and Stokes collapses its
integral to an exact term over nothing. **3.** $\mathbb{R}^2$,
generated by the two coordinate forms $dx, dy$, one per glued
edge. **4.** The winding number is the pairing of $d\theta/2\pi$
(closed, not exact on the circle) against the fundamental class.
**5.** $\mathbb{R}$ in degrees $0$ and $n$, zero between: the
radial contraction contracts all positive-degree closed forms.
**6.** $\omega = d\eta$ implies $d\omega = d \circ d \eta = 0$: the exterior derivative's defining nilpotence. **7.** $H^0 = \mathbb{R}$ (constants) and $H^1 = \mathbb{R}$ (generated by $d\theta$); everything higher vanishes on the one-dimensional circle. **8.** The integrals of the closed form over the two edge loops give a linear map $\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$, and the theorem says every such pairing arises from a unique cohomology class: pairings classify the forms.

</div>
</details>

## 5. Fibre Bundles, Connections, and Characteristic Classes

**Definition.** A **fibre bundle** is a manifold $E$ mapped to a
base $B$ such that locally $E$ looks like $B \times F$ (fibre $F$),
with smooth transition maps between the local trivialisations:
Stage K's covering circle was the simplest bundle. A **connection**
is the rule for comparing fibres along paths: on a vector bundle,
$\nabla_U V$, Stage J6's projected derivative made intrinsic, the
construction H5's price discussion demanded.

**Definition (curvature; characteristic classes).** The
**curvature** of a connection is $F := dA + A \wedge A$ (the
failure of the connection's potential to commute with itself);
its trace-polynomials give closed forms, the **characteristic
classes**, whose cohomology classes are invariants of the bundle
independent of the connection chosen.

**The capstone instance.** The tangent bundle of the sphere:
its curvature form integrates to $\int_{S^2} K \, dA = 4\pi$, and
the associated Euler class integrates to the **Euler
characteristic** $\chi = 2$. Gauss's theorema egregium (J6-J7)
said $K$ was intrinsic; the characteristic-class formalism says
$\int K \, dA$ is a *bundle invariant*: the same integer the
triangulation of Section 3 would count. The bridge from calculus
to combinatorics is the chapter's crown.

8. State what the curvature form's integral computes for a

<details class="wn-spoiler">
<summary>Answers</summary>
<div class="wn-spoiler-body">

**1.** Locally trivial: $E$ over $B$ looks like $B \times F$ with
smooth transition maps; the covering $\\mathbb{R} \to S^1$.
**2.** The rule $\nabla_U V$ for comparing neighbouring fibres;
Stage J6's projected derivative. **3.** $F = dA + A \wedge A$: the
failure of parallel transport around infinitesimal loops to be
trivial. **4.** Closed forms built from curvature by trace
polynomials; their cohomology classes do not change when the
connection changes. **5.** The Euler class of the tangent bundle
integrates to $\chi(S^2) = 2$.
**6.** Over each semicircle of $S^1$, the covering sheet label flips by one integer between the two preimages: the transition map adds or subtracts $1$ in $\mathbb{Z}$. **7.** A nowhere-vanishing field would trivialise the tangent bundle, making its Euler class integrate to zero; but the Euler class integrates to $\chi(S^2) = 2$: contradiction. **8.** By Gauss-Bonnet, $\int K \, dA = 2\pi \chi = 4\pi$: the curvature integral is the Euler class's characteristic number.

</div>
</details>

:::

## 6. Riemannian Geometry: Hopf-Rinow, Gauss-Bonnet, Comparison

**Definition.** A **Riemannian metric** is an inner product on
each tangent space, varying smoothly; it creates lengths, geodesics
(J6-J7), and curvature from the manifold alone.

**Theorem (Hopf-Rinow; stated).** For a connected Riemannian
manifold, completeness of the metric is equivalent to: all
geodesics extend forever, and any two points are joined by a
minimising geodesic. The proof spends Stage G's ODE theory
(geodesic flows extend) and 2B's completeness (the limit point
exists). On a complete manifold, Stage G9's contraction and
existence machinery runs globally.

**Theorem (Gauss-Bonnet; stated with engine).** On a compact
surface: $\int_S K \, dA + \int_{\partial S} k_g \, ds = 2\pi
\chi(S)$. The total Gaussian curvature plus the total boundary
geodesic curvature equals $2\pi$ times the Euler characteristic.
The engine is Section 5's Euler class (the curvature form is the
Euler class's representative, so Stokes integrates it to the
characteristic number) together with the triangulation count of
Section 3. The consequence chain: a compact surface's curvature
integral is independent of the metric; the sphere's is $4\pi$;
the torus's is $0$; and no metric on the torus can have everywhere
positive curvature.

<details class="wn-spoiler">
<summary>Answers</summary>
<div class="wn-spoiler-body">

**1.** A smoothly varying inner product on the tangent spaces.
**2.** Metric completeness iff geodesic completeness and
minimising geodesics between points; engines: Stage G's geodesic
ODEs and 2B's completeness. **3.** $\int K \, dA + \int_{\partial}
k_g \, ds = 2\pi \chi(S)$. **4.** $0$ for the flat torus, and by
Gauss-Bonnet for *every* metric on the torus: no metric has
everywhere positive curvature. **5.** Stage J's Stokes, via
Section 5's Euler class.
**6.** On the flat torus $\mathbb{R}^2/\mathbb{Z}^2$, the connection is trivial, so $\nabla_{\gamma'} \gamma' = 0$ is the straight-line equation in the plane; projected to the quotient, those are exactly the geodesics. **7.** The sphere is complete, so any two points are joined by a geodesic realising the distance: minimising paths exist, not merely approach. **8.** Gauss's theorema egregium makes $K$ intrinsic, and Gauss-Bonnet equates its integral to $2\pi\chi$, a topological constant of the surface: changing the metric changes $K$ pointwise but not the integral.

</div>
</details>

## 7. Representation Theory: Characters and Peter-Weyl

**Definition.** A **representation** of a group $G$ is a
homomorphism $G \to GL(V)$: symmetry made linear, Stage L's
actions with Stage F's matrices. A **character** is its trace
function $\chi(g) := \mathrm{tr}(\rho(g))$.

**Theorem (the character dichotomy, stated).** For a compact group
with an invariant inner product on $V$ (averaged over the group,
which compactness makes possible): $\rho$ is determined up to
isomorphism by its character, characters of irreducible
representations are orthonormal in $L^2(G)$, and every
representation splits into irreducibles. The engine: Stage M2's
Hilbert-space geometry with Stage L's group structure, integrated
by Stage I.

**Theorem (Peter-Weyl; stated).** The matrix coefficients of the
irreducible representations are dense in $\mathcal{C}(G)$: the
generalisation of Stage H's Weierstrass approximation from the
circle to every compact group, with the circle's Fourier theory
(Stage I Section 7) as the abelian special case. The consequence:
every compact group embeds in matrices, and the irreducible
characters classify its harmonic analysis.

   $SO(2)$.

8. State what Peter-Weyl says for the circle, matching Stage I's
   Fourier theory.

<details class="wn-spoiler">
<summary>Answers</summary>
<div class="wn-spoiler-body">

**1.** A homomorphism into $GL(V)$; its trace function.
**2.** Average any inner product over the group: compactness makes
the integral finite and the invariance exact. **3.** Distinct
irreducibles have $L^2$-orthogonal characters. **4.** Matrix
coefficients of irreducibles are dense in continuous functions on
$G$; the circle's Fourier series is the abelian case. **5.** Every
compact group embeds into some $GL_n$: finite-dimensional symmetry
suffices.
**6.** A rotation by $\theta$ has trace $2\cos\theta$: the character of $SO(2)$'s standard representation. **7.** $\mathrm{tr}(\rho(hgh^{-1})) = \mathrm{tr}(\rho(h)\rho(g)\rho(h)^{-1}) = \mathrm{tr}(\rho(g))$: traces are invariant under cyclic permutation. **8.** The irreducible characters are $e^{int}$ for integers $n$, and their denseness in continuous functions on the circle is exactly Stage I's Fourier completeness.

</div>
</details>

   exactness cannot be tested locally.
   *(Hint: $\int_{S^1} d\theta = 2\pi$ while an exact form would

   neighbourhood on which $d\theta = d(\text{an angle branch})$.)*
4. Compute the Euler characteristic of the torus from a square

   *(Hint: one face, two edges after gluing, one vertex after the

5. Prove that the characters of a compact abelian group are exactly

   *(Hint: abelian makes every irreducible one-dimensional, so
