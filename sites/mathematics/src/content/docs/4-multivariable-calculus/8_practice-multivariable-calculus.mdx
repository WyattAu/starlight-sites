## Intuition

**Calculus in higher dimensions:** Multivariable calculus extends single-variable calculus to functions of several variables — surfaces instead of curves, volumes instead of areas. It is the mathematics of fields (temperature, gravity, electromagnetic) that vary across space.

**Why it matters:** Machine learning optimises functions of millions of variables (gradients), physics describes fields in 3D space (divergence, curl), and economics models multi-factor systems. Multivariable calculus is the mathematical language of all these.

**The key insight:** The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent — this is why gradient descent (following the negative gradient) finds minima, which is the foundation of neural network training.

## Common Mistakes

**Forgetting the Jacobian determinant when changing variables in integrals:** When converting from Cartesian to polar, spherical, or other coordinate systems, you must multiply by the Jacobian determinant (e.g., $r$ for polar coordinates). The integral $\iint f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$ becomes $\iint f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta$, not just $\iint f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)\,dr\,d\theta$.

**Confusing the gradient with the directional derivative:** The gradient $\nabla f$ is a vector, while the directional derivative $D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$ is a scalar. The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent and its magnitude is the maximum directional derivative. Do not confuse $\nabla f$ with $D_{\mathbf{u}} f$ — they are different objects.

**Misapplying Lagrange multipliers by forgetting the constraint:** The method finds extrema of $f$ subject to $g = c$ by solving $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ together with $g = c$. A common error is solving $\nabla f = 0$ (unconstrained critical points) instead of including the constraint equation. Always verify your solutions satisfy $g(x,y) = c$.
