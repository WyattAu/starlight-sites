### 9.1 Pell's Equation

**Definition.** For a nonsquare positive integer $D$, **Pell's equation** is

$$
x^2 - D y^2 = 1, \qquad x, y \in \mathbb{Z}.
$$

The trivial solutions $(\pm 1, 0)$ always exist; the equation is interesting because whenever one
non-trivial solution $(x_1, y_1)$ exists, **infinitely many** do — all generated by powers of the
fundamental solution in the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{D}]$:

$$
x_k + y_k \sqrt{D} = (x_1 + y_1 \sqrt{D})^k, \qquad k = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
$$

(the algebra works because $(x_1^2 - D y_1^2)(x_2^2 - D y_2^2) = (x_1 x_2 - D y_1 y_2)^2 -
D(x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1)^2$ — Brahmagupta's identity).

**Example.** $x^2 - 2y^2 = 1$: fundamental solution $(3, 2)$, and successive solutions
$(17, 12), (99, 70), (577, 408), \ldots$ — the $y$-values grow like $(3 + 2\sqrt 2)^k / (2\sqrt 2)$,
exponentially.

**Example (the cattle problem of Archimedes, simplified).** $x^2 - 61 y^2 = 1$ has fundamental
solution $x = 1766319049$, $y = 226153980$ — a famously enormous answer discovered via continued
fractions, illustrating why the equation resisted solution for centuries.

### 9.2 Solving Pell via Continued Fractions

The **continued fraction expansion** of $\sqrt{D}$ is periodic for every nonsquare $D$:

$$
\sqrt{D} = [a_0; \overline{a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_r}].
$$

The solutions of Pell's equation come from the convergents $p_k/q_k$:

- If the period length $r$ is even, $(p_{r-1}, q_{r-1})$ is the fundamental solution of
  $x^2 - Dy^2 = 1$.
- If $r$ is odd, $(p_{2r-1}, q_{2r-1})$ solves $= 1$, and $(p_{r-1}, q_{r-1})$ solves the
  **negative Pell** equation $x^2 - D y^2 = -1$.

**Worked example.** $\sqrt{7} = [2; \overline{1, 1, 1, 4}]$ ($r = 4$, even). Convergents: $2/1$,
$3/1$, $5/2$, $8/3$ — check $8^2 - 7 \cdot 3^2 = 64 - 63 = 1$: fundamental solution $(8, 3)$, and
the next is $(127, 48)$.

### 9.3 Sums of Two Squares

**Theorem (Fermat, two squares).** An odd prime $p$ satisfies $p = x^2 + y^2$ iff
$p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$; and a general integer $n$ is a sum of two squares iff every prime
$q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ divides $n$ to an **even** power.

*Proof idea of the prime case.* For $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $-1$ is a QR (Euler's criterion), so
$x^2 \equiv -1 \pmod p$ has a solution; $x^2 + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod p$ means $p \mid x^2 + 1$ with
$0 < x^2 + 1 < p^2$, so by **descent** (repeatedly halving via a multiple of $p$) a genuine
representation is produced. $\blacksquare$

Examples: $5 = 1 + 4$, $13 = 4 + 9$, $17 = 16 + 1$, $29 = 25 + 4$ ($p \equiv 1 \bmod 4$); while
$3, 7, 11, 19 \equiv 3 \bmod 4$ are never sums of two squares. $21 = 3 \cdot 7$ has 3 to an odd
power → not a sum of two squares. $45 = 3^2 \cdot 5$ → $45 = 36 + 9$ ✓ (3 appears to an even
power).

### 9.4 Four Squares and Beyond

**Lagrange's four-square theorem**: every $n \geq 0$ is a sum of four squares (proven by Euler's
identity plus descent). Waring's theorem generalises: every $n$ is a sum of at most $g(k)$
$k$-th powers; the conjectured values $g(2) = 4$, $g(3) = 9$, $g(4) = 19$ are known.

Two squares → at most four squares → the pattern of *which* numbers need how many squares is
governed by Legendre's three-square theorem: $n$ is a sum of three squares iff $n$ is **not** of
the form $4^a(8b + 7)$.

### 9.5 Common Pitfalls

- Pell has **no solutions** when $D$ is a perfect square ($x^2 - k^2 y^2 = 1$ factors over
  $\mathbb{Z}$, forcing $y = 0$).
- The fundamental solution can be astronomically large ($D = 61$ case) — never guess it; use the
  continued fraction of $\sqrt{D}$.
- The two-squares criterion fails on the *power* of a $3 \bmod 4$ prime: $9 = 3^2$ is not a sum of
  two squares (odd power test), but $9 \cdot 5 = 45$ is.
- Products of sums of two squares are again sums of two squares (Brahmagupta) — quotable in
  constructions.

### 9.x A Second Pell Cascade

For $D = 5$: $\sqrt5 = [2; \overline{4}]$, period 1 (odd), so $(9, 4)$ solves $x^2 - 5y^2 = 1$ from the convergent $9/4$, and $(2, 1)$ solves $x^2 - 5y^2 = -1$.
