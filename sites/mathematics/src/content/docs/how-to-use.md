## How to Use These Notes

These notes are built around a small number of findings from education research
that are large in effect and robustly replicated. Knowing them changes how you
should read what follows, because several of them contradict what feels
productive.

## 1. Study worked examples before solving problems

The most replicated finding in mathematics education research over the last
forty years is the **worked example effect** (Sweller & Cooper, 1985; Chen,
Kalyuga & Sweller, 2015). Novices who study fully worked solutions learn more,
and learn faster, than novices who attempt the same problems themselves.

The reason is *element interactivity*. Solving a problem by means-ends analysis
forces you to hold in working memory your current state, the goal state, the
differences between them, and candidate moves — all at once. A worked example
shows you each state and the move that gets you to the next one, so the number
of interacting elements collapses. What remains is the schema you were trying
to learn.

So: **read the worked examples first, and read them actively.** Cover the
solution, predict the next step, uncover, check. A worked example you read
passively is worth a fraction of one you interrogate.

## 2. Then solve problems — the expertise reversal

The worked example effect reverses once you are no longer a novice. Kalyuga,
Chandler, Tuovinen and Sweller (2001) found that for learners who already knew
the material, studying worked examples was *worse* than solving problems: the
example adds redundancy, and redundancy costs attention.

Every page here therefore fades from worked examples to completion problems to
bare problems. The point at which you should stop reading examples and start
solving is the point where you can predict the next step correctly most of the
time. That transition is not optional — continuing to study examples after you
have the schema actively slows you down.

## 3. Test yourself; do not reread

**Retrieval practice** — attempting to recall an answer rather than rereading
it — produces far better retention than restudy, an effect large enough that
Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated it among the two most effective techniques
available to learners.

Rereading feels effective because the text becomes familiar. Familiarity is
*not* learning: it is recognition, and recognition does not help you produce a
proof in an exam. The uncomfortable version — closing the notes and trying to
reconstruct the argument — is the one that works.

Each page here opens with **recall prompts**: questions to attempt from memory
*before* reading. Attempting them and failing is not wasted effort; the failure
is what makes the subsequent reading stick.

## 4. Space your practice, and mix topics

The spacing effect is, in Bjork's words, one of the most general and robust
effects in the history of research on learning and memory. Spaced practice beats
massed practice on delayed tests, often by a factor of two, at equal total study
time.

**Interleaving** — mixing problem types rather than blocking them — is the
efficient way to space. It also does something spacing alone does not: it forces
you to *select* the method, which is the part of problem solving that blocked
practice never exercises. Rohrer's work on mathematics practice shows interleaved
practice substantially outperforming blocked practice on delayed tests, while
learners overwhelmingly report the opposite preference.

That mismatch matters. Blocked practice produces rapid gains that feel like
learning and do not survive the week. Interleaved practice feels worse and
produces knowledge that does. **Do not trust your feeling of fluency as a
measure of learning.** Bjork separates *retrieval strength* — how easily
something comes to mind now — from *storage strength* — how durably it is held.
Massed practice inflates the first and does little for the second.

## 5. Intuition first, then rigour, then both

A definition you cannot motivate is a string of symbols. Each page here follows
the same order:

1. **Motivation.** A concrete problem the concept exists to solve.
2. **Intuition.** What it means, in pictures and plain language.
3. **Definition.** Precisely, with the reason for each hypothesis.
4. **Theorem and proof.** With the *idea* of the proof separated from its
   verification, because the idea is what generalises.
5. **Worked examples.** Fully worked, then faded.
6. **Counterexamples.** Where the naive version breaks, and why the hypotheses
   are load-bearing.
7. **Recall prompts and problems.**

Skipping to (3) and (4) is how mathematics is usually examined and almost never
how it is understood. The counterexamples in (6) are the part most notes omit
and most research depends on: knowing that continuity does not imply
differentiability is worth less than knowing *Weierstrass' function* and being
able to say what it does.

## 6. The rigour ladder

Sections are marked so you can see how deep a given page goes:

| Mark | Meaning |
| ---- | ------- |
| **F** Foundation | Definitions and first examples. Assumed later. |
| **U** Undergraduate | The standard degree-level treatment, with proofs. |
| **A** Advanced | Graduate-level: the machinery needed for research. |
| **R** Research | Open problems, recent results, and where the machinery fails. |

A fresh undergraduate should read every **F** and **U** on a topic, then the
**A** sections of the topics they intend to specialise in. **R** sections assume
comfort with everything before them and are written to be read after the rest.

## 7. A practical schedule

The research above translates into a routine:

- **First pass.** Read the page. Attempt the recall prompts from memory. Read
  the worked examples actively, predicting each step.
- **Second pass, 1–2 days later.** Solve the completion problems without the
  notes. Anything you cannot do, mark.
- **Third pass, 1–2 weeks later.** Redo the marked problems *and two from an
  earlier topic* — that is the interleaving.
- **Before assessment.** Reconstruct the key definitions and theorem statements
  from memory, then check. Reconstructing a statement is harder and more useful
  than recognising it.

The second and third passes are where the learning happens. The first pass only
makes them possible.
