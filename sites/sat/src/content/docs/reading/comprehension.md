# Comprehension

SAT reading study notes - Comprehension

## Key Concepts

- **Main Idea**: the central point the author is making. Often found in the first or last paragraph. Every detail connects back to this.
- **Supporting Details**: facts, examples, and evidence that support the main idea. Always verify answer choices against specific textual evidence.
- **Inferences**: logical conclusions drawn from textual evidence. Must be directly supported — not reasonable guesses.
- **Purpose**: why the author wrote the passage or included a specific detail — to inform, persuade, entertain, or explain.
- **Tone**: the author's attitude toward the subject — objective, critical, enthusiastic, skeptical. Determined by word choice.

## Worked Example

**Problem:** The passage states that "the committee decided to postpone the project indefinitely." What can be inferred?

**Solution:**
Step 1: "Postpone" means delay; "indefinitely" means with no set end date
Step 2: The committee has not cancelled the project but has no current plans to continue
Step 3: The correct inference is that the project may resume in the future but there is no timeline

## Intuition

Reading comprehension is like being a detective at a crime scene — every sentence is a clue, and you need to piece together what happened, why, and what it means. The main idea is the suspect you are after; supporting details are the evidence that points to them. Inference is reading between the lines — the author did not say it directly, but the clues are there if you look. Purpose is asking "why did the author write this?" — were they trying to inform, persuade, or entertain? Every passage has a structure, and understanding that structure is like understanding the blueprint of a building.

## Common Mistakes

**Choosing answers based on outside knowledge rather than the passage.** The SAT reading section tests what the passage says, not what you already know about the topic. An answer choice that is true in general but not supported by the passage is incorrect. Always find specific textual evidence for your answer before selecting it.

**Confusing inference with explicit statements.** Some questions ask what the passage "implies" or "suggests," which requires drawing a logical conclusion from the text. Other questions ask what is "stated" or "directly supported." Students often select explicit statements for inference questions or make unsupported leaps for explicit questions. Identify the question type before answering.

**Overlooking qualifying language.** Passages often include words like "may," "sometimes," "often," or "in most cases" that limit the scope of a claim. Answer choices that remove these qualifiers and make absolute statements (using "always," "never," "all," or "none") are typically incorrect. Match the strength of the answer to the strength of the passage's claim.
